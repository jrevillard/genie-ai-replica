## I. INTRODUCTION

<!-- image -->

Sorghum, also known as sorghum, is the second most produced grain in volume in El Salvador, after white corn. According to DGEA 2016-2017, the national production reached 20.32 million quintals in 433,603 manzanas (303,522 hectares) planted at the national level, with an average yield of 46.9 qq/mz.

Sorghum is a crop that, in some regions of the world, is replacing maize due to its resistance to diseases, as well as its low water requirements. This makes it a viable alternative in the face of climate change.

The importance of this crop has increased significantly in recent years due to its use in human food; in the baking industry, sorghum flour is gaining popularity as it can replace up to 50% of wheat flour in breadmaking without affecting its quality. Another important use is in animal feed.

**I. BOTANICAL ASPECTS**

**ORIGIN**

Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor, L. Moench), belonging to the grass family, originated in Africa and arrived in Central America through India, China, and the United States.

**ROOT**

The adventitious, fibrous root system develops from the lower nodes of the stem; the rooting depth is generally between 1 and 1.3 meters, with 80% of the roots in the first 30 centimeters. The number of absorbent hairs can be twice that of maize, and the supporting roots can grow from radical shoots, but they are not effective in absorbing water and nutrients.

**STEM**

Sorghum is a single-stem plant, but it can develop other shoots (suckers) depending on the variety and the environment. It is composed of a series of nodes and internodes, with a length ranging from 0.5 to 4 meters, a diameter of 0.5 to 5 cm near the base, becoming narrower at the top; its consistency is solid with a hard outer rind or tissue and a soft medulla. The stems have 7 to 24 nodes and are erect.

**LEAVES**

The number of leaves varies from 7 to 24 depending on the variety and the growth period, they are erect until almost horizontal and bend with age. The length of a mature leaf ranges from 30 to 135 cm and its width from 5 to 15 cm; they are alternate and lanceolate or linear-lanceolate, with a smooth and waxy surface.

**INFLORESCENCE**

It is a panicle with a cluster, with a central rachis completely hidden by the density of its branches or fully exposed when immature, it is forced upwards inside the upper spike (spikelet), after the last leaf (flag leaf) expands, stretching it in its path. The exsertion is important for mechanized harvesting and for tolerance to pests and diseases. The panicle is short or long, loose and open, and compact or semi-compact (Fig. 1). It can be 10 to 35 cm long, 5 to 20 cm wide, and contain 400 to 1000 grains, depending on the type of panicle and variety.

**II. SORGHUM GROWTH STAGES**

Sorghum cultivation has three well-defined growth stages, each lasting approximately 30 days, depending on the variety used and the agroclimatic conditions.

**A) STAGE 1**

Vegetative: This stage begins with the imbibition of water by the seed, the formation of the radicle and coleoptile, and ends with the growth of leaves and stems.

**B) STAGE 2**

Reproductive: This stage begins with the emergence of the floral primordium, continues with the initiation of primary and secondary branches, the enlargement of the floral apex, glumes, spikelets, and the formation of flowers with their stamens and pistils, and ends with the maturation (maturity) of the reproductive organs.

**C) STAGE 3**

This stage includes pollination, fertilization of the ovary, development and maturation of the grain.

**IV. EDAPHOCLIMATIC REQUIREMENTS**

**SOIL**

Sorghum is susceptible to iron, zinc, and manganese deficiencies, especially in vertisols with high levels of calcium carbonate. These deficiencies can be observed as chlorotic (yellow) plants or reddish spots on the leaves. It responds well to a variety of soils, even with adverse fertility, texture, slope, and stone characteristics, and pH (5.5-7.8).

**ALTITUDE**

Sorghum can be cultivated at altitudes from 0 to 1000 meters above sea level, but the best yields are obtained in areas between 0 and 500 meters above sea level.

**DAYLENGTH**

Sorghum, depending on its physiological condition, can be either day-neutral or day-sensitive. Day-neutral varieties are those whose flowering is not affected by the amount of daylight and flower independently of the time of year. Day-sensitive varieties are affected by the amount of daylight in their flowering, and this occurs when the days are shorter (November-December).

**V. HUMIDITY**

Day-neutral sorghum varieties require a higher level of soil moisture for pollination and grain filling, compared to day-sensitive (criollo) varieties, which require a minimum level of soil moisture to complete the development stages satisfactorily. In general, sorghum requires 550 mm of water during the growing cycle, well distributed for optimal production.

**TEMPERATURE**

Due to its tropical origin, sorghum adapts well to temperatures between 20 and 40°C. Temperatures outside this range cause a delay or acceleration of anthesis (pre-flowering), flower abortion, and embryo development.

## VI. CULTIVATION PRACTICES

Cultivation management should be optimal to take full advantage of the genetic potential of the varieties or hybrids:

## SOIL PREPARATION.

Cultivation: This involves manually, mechanically, or chemically removing weeds 8 to 15 days before planting, depending on the type and development of the weeds, as well as the soil's topographic conditions.

Harrowing: This activity allows the soil to be loosened to favor seed germination and seedling emergence. Depending on the soil condition, 2 to 3 passes with a harrow are recommended.

Plowing: Should have a depth of 10 to 15 cm for seed germination and to promote drainage. It is recommended to perform one plowing pass (every 2 or 3 years) to prevent soil compaction. In soils with slopes greater than 15%, minimum or zero tillage should be implemented, and the planting bed should be free of weeds, which can be achieved with chemical or manual methods.

## PLANTING TIMES AND SYSTEMS

## SORGHUM FOR GRAIN

First planting: Planting should take place when the rains begin (between the second half of May or the first half of June) with photosensitive materials, which will allow for two harvests in a year, either through regrowth management or by planting new seeds; the first harvest should be dried with machinery to avoid losses due to rot or germination. This planting season is not the most recommended for grain production, but rather for forage production for silage.

Second planting (winter): This is the most recommended and widespread season, and should be carried out in the first half of August, to harvest in a dry period without the need for artificial drying. It can also be cultivated under irrigation or with residual moisture (planting no later than early December), taking into account that the harvest should be obtained before the start of the rains to avoid problems with moisture in the grain.

## a) Materials for grain (heirloom) in association

This should be carried out during the first half of June, when it is associated with maize (Fig. 5), which coincides with the harvest of maize (30 to 35 days after planting) or in the first half of August when it is planted in rotation with maize.

Figure 5. Sorghum in an association system with maize.

## b) Planting of dual-purpose sorghum

The planting of CENTA S-2, CENTA S-3, CENTA S-2 bmr, CENTA S-3 bmr, CENTA S-4 bmr, and other dual-purpose sorghums is recommended for the first half of May, which will allow for 2 cuts; the first cut between 75 to 90 days after planting when the grain is in the dough stage and suitable for silage; the second cut should be made 75 to 80 days after the first for use in silage or 100 days after the first to obtain grain.

## c) Planting of multi-cut forage hybrid such as CENTA SS-44

This material is recommended to be planted in the first half of April if irrigation is available, otherwise, plant in the first half of May (at the beginning of the rains) and thus obtain the maximum number of cuts during the year. To achieve this goal, it is recommended to plant in staggered rows, weekly, until the sixth week, to ensure a permanent supply of high-quality nutritional forage. This is an important practice in the management of this type of sorghum, as it allows the farmer to have a permanent supply of fresh product and to take advantage of the forage at its peak protein level (17%), the size of the plot will depend on the number of cows to feed and on the capacity and logistics of the farm for forage management.

## Calculation of the size of the plots.

To calculate the plots, the following data should be taken into account:

- Daily forage consumption of sorghum per cow in lactation: 60 lbs = 27.27 kg

- Yield per cut: 35 tons/hectare = 5 kg/m2
- Weekly consumption per cow: 191 kg.

Surface required to feed one lactating cow for one week: 191 kg, divided by 5 kg/m2 = 38.2 m2, equivalent to a plot of 9 rows of 6.3 meters long, planted at 70 cm.

## d) Planting time under irrigation

If the goal is to produce grain, it is recommended to use photosensitive varieties and plant them between November 15 and December 31, as the deadline, and January 15, to obtain the harvest before the start of the rains of the following year and thus avoid losses due to rot, germination and grain staining.

In forage sorghums such as CENTA SS-44 and dual-purpose sorghums, the photoperiod (short days) from November to January, is a factor to take into account, as it reduces the growth of the plant, affecting forage yields, without affecting grain yield.

## ROW SPACING

Row spacing is an activity that increases costs, which should be avoided by depositing a large amount of seed at the bottom of the row or in each planting. This labor, also known as weeding, consists of removing excess plants to regulate the plant population. It depends on the purpose of the crop and the variety or hybrid planted. It is recommended to carry out weeding when the plants reach a height of 10 to 15 cm, which occurs between 10 to 15 days after planting, depending on the soil moisture conditions and the cultivation systems used.

## WEED CONTROL

Sorghum is a crop with a slow initial growth rate, which is accentuated by weed competition. Therefore, it is essential to prepare the soil well to keep the crop clean in the early stages and, consequently, to ensure better utilization of the applied fertilizers and, thus, better development of the crop.

Weed control can be carried out mechanically, manually, and chemically. Certain cultural practices contribute to reducing the effects of weeds, such as: using improved seeds, appropriate plant density, and reduced planting distances.

## Mechanical control:

This involves eliminating weeds using agricultural implements. It will primarily depend on the topography of the land and the availability of equipment on the farm (plow, harrow, subsoiler). It should be carried out preferably when the soil is relatively dry, in order to facilitate this work and prevent the weeds from re-establishing.

## Manual control:

This involves eliminating weeds using simple agricultural tools (hoe, spade). Generally, it is recommended to carry out two cleanings: the number will depend on the population and type of weeds present, as well as on the preparation of the soil. Traditionally, they are carried out before planting and between 25 and 30 days after.

## Chemical control:

The use of selective herbicides eliminates weeds without damaging the crop. For effective chemical control, it is necessary to know the complex of weeds and their stage of development; type of herbicide, dosage, and weather conditions during application (Table 11).

## VII. PEST CONTROL

According to Compton (1990), pest control should be carried out through integrated pest management (IPM), which includes: using appropriate insecticides, using resistant or tolerant varieties, cultural practices (planting date, crop rotation, elimination of insect hosts), biological control, and, of course, detailed observation of the crop to verify pest populations and the damage caused.

It is very important, where possible, to find out about the life cycle of the insects considered as pests, as it should not be expected to find a crop completely free of insects, as even when they are present, they may not reach levels of damage to the crop that could transform into economic losses. We must also remember that the larvae of insects have different names, depending on the group to which they belong: in the group of cutworms, they are called "caterpillars", the larvae of flies are called "crawlers", and those of beetles are known as "wireworms, black cutworms, grubs". These larvae undergo morphological changes, which influence their feeding habits, and it is in their initial stages (L2 and L3) that they are most voracious and, therefore, cause the most damage to the sorghum plant or grain.

## SOIL PESTS

## WHITE GRUB (Cylas formicarius)

The damage caused by these white, C-shaped grubs, measuring 2 to 3 mm to 3 cm (Fig. 6), is first manifested in wilted seedlings, and then in areas with a low population of plants, which are leaning, curved, or stunted and grow irregularly. A white grub can destroy all plants in a 5-meter row.

Figure 6. White grub infestation.

## WIREWORMS (Melanotos spp., Agriotes spp.)

These are thin, cylindrical, and segmented worms, soft and white when young. When they develop, they measure 40 mm, becoming shiny, smooth, hard, and slow-moving, yellow or brown (Fig. 7). They feed on the embryo of the seed, and also damage the base of the stems and cut the roots of the plants. Their damage is manifested by areas without seedlings, wilting of these, or the stunting of plants already developed.

## CONTROL OF SOIL INSECTS

Adult weevils (beetles) can be captured using homemade light traps, thus reducing a large number of future larvae populations in the soil. It is recommended to till the soil, during the dry season, to expose weevil eggs and larvae, as well as other pest insects, to sunlight and predation. For general soil pest control, it is recommended to use seed treatments. These products protect the seeds from pest and disease attacks during storage and subsequent planting.

YELLOW SOUTHERN PEACHY (Melanaphis sacchari, Zehntner )

During the sorghum planting cycle in August 2015, a high infestation of yellow peach, primarily in the crops of producers in the municipality of Texistepeque, in the western part of El Salvador, was reported for the first time. Due to the severity of the damage observed, in October of the same year, technical researchers and extension agents from CENTA carried out the collection of the insect for identification. Subsequently, entomologists from the Faculty of Agronomy of the University of El Salvador (UES) and technicians from the CENTA Plant Pathology Laboratory, joined efforts for its identification, and concluded that the insect found was the yellow peach (Melanaphis sacchari), an insect originating in Africa, which has been distributed in various countries in Asia, Australia, the Caribbean, South America, Mexico, and in the states of Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi in the United States.

## Biology

The yellow peach reproduces rapidly and asexually, in favorable temperature conditions (10 to 40°C). The female, without a male, gives rise to many individuals, who in a week are able to damage stems, leaves and flower heads. They have a short life cycle (5 to 7 days), with multiple generations per year, gregarious habits, meaning they gather in abundant colonies; and they inhabit wild grasses and sorghum (Fig. 11).

## Damage

Damage can begin from the eighth day of life until the physiological maturity of the crop, and the insect is located on the basal part of the leaf and on the underside, sucking sap from the leaves, causing reddish lesions, reducing photosynthesis in the plant. Associated with these damages are fungi such as fumagina, due to the mealy substance excreted by the peach. In addition, in extreme cases, it favors the transmission of viral diseases; all this association can cause the complete loss of the crop.

## Prevention and control measures

Based on observations made from the appearance of the pest in our country, and in consultation of information generated in other countries, the following is recommended:

* Conduct sampling at 10 points within the field, in a zig-zag pattern, each point will be a plant sampled in its entirety, if three points show peach colonies, control is recommended.

* Place yellow sticky traps on the edges of the field to detect the arrival of the peach.

## Cultural control

* Incorporate crop residues.
* Eliminate host weeds before and during the development of the crop.
* Eliminate sorghum and other volunteer grasses and wild host plants such as Johnson grass, during the fallow period.

## Biological control

It is necessary to consider the integrated management of the pest, using natural enemies of the peach such as predators (ladybugs, lacewings and aphids), parasitoids (wasps) and entomopathogens, which must be protected by not using pesticides indiscriminately.

## Biochemical control

If the crop is 1.5 meters high, it is recommended to apply organic formulations to care for the health of the spraying personnel and the economy.

Applications should be made every three days to break the reproduction cycle until a low density of the pest is monitored. It is recommended to apply from bottom to top of the plant, as the insect is located on the underside of the leaf.

Recommended mixtures include:

1. Mix 100 grams of common salt (3 cups of 25 cc), 100 grams of flour (5 cups of 25 cc), 60 grams of powdered detergent (3 cups of 25 cc); place this mixture in a 4-gallon backpack sprayer and spray over the crop.

2. Mix in 20 liters of water and ferment for two days tobacco, half a pound of finely chopped chili pepper, half a pound of finely chopped epazina and five cups of 25 cc of powdered detergent. Apply a dose of half a liter of the preparation per 4-gallon backpack sprayer.

## INSECTS IN STORED GRAINS

The most common are weevils (Sitophilus sp.) and weevilly grubs (Sitotroga cerealella). These pests cause damage to stored grains if they contain more than 12% moisture (Fig. 15). The grain should be stored in a dry and clean environment. If the moisture content of the grain is less than 9%, the insect is unable to reproduce, thus reducing the damage.

It is necessary to take precautions when using fumigant products. Grain containers should be completely sealed, in well-ventilated areas, and never in the living area.

## DISEASE CONTROL

The most common diseases in sorghum cultivation are: sorghum rust (Puccinia sorghi), leaf speckled blight (Gloeocercospora sorghi), leaf grey spot (Cercospora sorghi), leaf blight (Helminthosporium sp.), anthracnose/red rot (Colletotrichum graminicola), stem rot (Fusarium sp.), twisted panicle (Fusarium moniliformis) and velvety mildew (Peronosclerospora sorghi). Recently, the sorghum panicle disease, also known as "sugar disease" (Clavicep africana) and bacterial blight (Pseudomonas rubrilineans) have been reported.

## SORGHUM RUST (Puccinia sorghi)

This is one of the main diseases with the highest incidence and severity in sorghum, especially in traditional varieties. Improved sorghum varieties are affected to a lesser extent, as they have been incorporated with resistance through breeding programs. This disease appears when the grain begins to mature, from the last stages of sorghum cultivation, increasing the severity of the damage in susceptible varieties, making the foliage unsuitable for animal feed (Fig. 16). The best control is through resistant or tolerant varieties such as CENTA Texistepeque, CENTA Soberano, CENTA RCV and CENTA SS-44.

LEAF SPOTTED BLIGHT (Gloeocercospora sorghi)

This disease begins to damage young plants around 45 days of age, until the 80th day (Fig. 17). If the plant is susceptible, it can completely damage the leaf, affecting photosynthesis. The most effective control is the use of tolerant or resistant sorghum varieties, such as improved varieties RCV and Soberano.

Figure 17. Damage to foliage caused by the fungus.

## GRAY LEAF SPOT (Cercospora sorghi)

This disease appears on sorghum during the intermediate stage of the crop's vegetative cycle, approximately 60-70 days after planting. When varieties are highly susceptible, the fungus completely damages the foliage, causing the death of older leaves, which is where the disease begins (Fig. 18). Local varieties are very susceptible to this fungus, while improved varieties (CENTA S-3, CENTA RCV, and CENTA Soberano), which are more tolerant, are less affected.

Figure 18. Damage caused by the fungus on the foliage and a microscopic view of the fungus.

## LEAF BLIGHT (Helmintosporium sp.)

This disease appears during the intermediate and final stages of the crop's vegetative cycle (60-90 days after planting). When varieties are highly susceptible, the fungus damages the foliage, causing it to completely wither, leading to the death of intermediate leaves, where the disease begins (Fig. 19). The damage caused by this disease is less severe in local and improved varieties compared to the diseases described previously.

Figure 19. Damage caused by the fungus on the foliage and a microscopic view of the fungus.

## ANTHRACNOSE AND/OR RED ROT (Colletotrichum graminícola)

This disease appears on sorghum in the final stage of the crop's vegetative cycle (80-90 days after planting). It is not very common in the varieties planted in the country, and is considered of minor importance (Fig. 20).

Figure 20. Lesions on leaves caused by anthracnose.

## WAVY STEM (Fusarium moniliformis)

This disease appears from the beginning of the crop's vegetative cycle. It is frequently observed in local varieties, and is considered of minor importance in improved varieties (Fig. 21).

Figure 21. Deformation in the stem caused by Fusarium moniliformis.

## VELVETY MILDEW (Peronosclerospora sorghi)

This disease appears on sorghum from the beginning of the crop's vegetative cycle. Its appearance is considered less frequent; however, in recent years, its presence has increased, as varieties that were resistant to the disease are losing that resistance (Fig. 22).

Figure 22. Typical chlorosis of velvety mildew and a microscopic view of the fungus.

## ERGOT OR SUGAR DISEASE OF SORGHUM (Clavicep africana)

It is commonly found in sterile lines (sorghum lines that only have a viable female reproductive organ in their flowers). In the panicle, a mycellous or sticky substance appears that inhibits grain formation, as it is replaced by the fungus's spores (Fig. 23).

Figure 23. Ergot or sugar disease of sorghum.

For the control of diseases in commercial sorghum production, fungicides are generally not applied (except for ergot) because most of these occur after flowering, which does not cause significant economic damage, so their application is not cost-effective. The best control is to select resistant varieties or hybrids and to use seed treated with fungicides.

## VIII. HARVEST AND POST-HARVEST MANAGEMENT OF SORGHUM

Post-harvest management of sorghum begins with the selection of mature grain, free of pests and diseases, and the harvest can be carried out in two ways: manually using pruning shears or mechanically using a combine harvester; at the time of cutting the panicles, sacks of mescal or polypropylene can be used as containers for transport to the drying areas; where they are spread out to be fully exposed to the sun, so that the grain detaches easily. It takes about two days for the panicles to dry completely, and this process can be checked by pressing a panicle with the hand; if the grain detaches easily, it means that it is at the optimal point for threshing. Threshing can be done manually (with a stick) or mechanically (with a thresher). After threshing, the grain is blown to remove impurities and checked for moisture, which should be between 10 and 12%, the grain should not contain soil, broken grains or foreign matter, as these particles usually have a higher moisture content, and are prone to fungal growth and mycotoxins.

The final step is to store the grain, and for this, it is important to use granaries or polypropylene bags. It is also important that the grain is clean and healthy before entering storage. This means that the grain should be free of insects in any stage of development. If we are going to store it for more than three months, it is important to find a suitable place, where there is not much humidity and that is indoors.

Ideally, the grain should be cured to prevent damage from storage pests such as the weevil, which can be done using garlic and onions or with a chemical product inside the container, which must be sealed hermetically and opened only when the grain is ready to be commercialized.

## IX. INDUSTRIALIZATION OF SORGHUM GRAIN FOR HUMAN CONSUMPTION

In El Salvador, sorghum has been mainly consumed by low-income populations, replacing corn. Sorghum consumption is traditionally in the form of tortillas, traditional bread (salpores and cookies), reconstituted beverages such as atoles and refreshing drinks, and in the preparation of alboroto (a sweet made from revented sorghum grain mixed with panela honey, which is consumed in some Central American countries).

CENTA promotes the use of sorghum flour in the bakery and confectionery industry, as a thickener in processed foods, and in other applications, as it has been demonstrated that the technique and economy are feasible for the production of various products without compromising their nutritional quality.

Sorghum has the advantage of not containing gluten; gluten is a mixture of proteins with low nutritional and biological value, with low content of essential amino acids, so from a nutritional point of view, its exclusion from the diet does not pose any problems. Therefore, sorghum flour is an excellent alternative for the production of "gluten-free" products.

In other countries, sorghum is used for the production of alcoholic beverages, wines, empanadas, snacks, and other food products, according to local consumption customs.

## Varieties of Sorghum for Food Production

When selecting sorghum varieties for food production, it is important to consider the quality parameters specific to the intended use. White grain sorghum, golden sorghum, and floury endosperm varieties are generally preferred. These varieties typically have low levels of tannins, which are considered to be anti-nutritional substances. High concentrations of tannins can interfere with the absorption of certain nutrients, such as iron and protein. However, the highly antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and UV-protective properties of tannins can be beneficial for human health.

Valle Herrera and Lopez Acevedo (2016) conducted a study on the physical, chemical, and nutritional characteristics of sorghum varieties and found that the varieties RCV, LIBERAL, JOCORO, SOBERANO, S-2 NORMAL, S-3 NORMAL, S-2 BMR, S-3 BMR, CENTA ZAM-911, CENTA ZAM-912, SCP-805, ES-226, and ES-TEJ are suitable for producing fine flour. In a test of germination, 7 of these varieties exceeded 90% germination rate, making them suitable for processing into flour. Regarding grain volume after germination, the variety S-2 BMR was found to be the best. In the detection of tannins, the varieties RCV and S-3 NORMAL exceeded the 0.3% limit established by the Codex Standard for Sorghum in Grain (6.0% and 16.0% respectively). This could compromise the palatability and reduce the biological value of the protein in these varieties.

The bromatological analysis showed that all varieties significantly exceeded the minimum protein content established by the Codex Standard (7.0%), making them an excellent source of nitrogen. The ash content of most varieties also met the Codex Standard (maximum 1.5%), providing a good source of micronutrients.

## Processing Sorghum Grain for Flour Production

It is important to consider the production of sorghum flour, as industrial production, compared to artisanal production, could reduce the import and use of wheat flour. Producing fine flour allows for a greater substitution of wheat flour in various food formulations, while also providing a marketable product with desirable characteristics to meet the specific dietary needs of people with celiac disease (gluten allergy) and diabetics.

Sorghum flour produced through artisanal methods and intended for food production must meet minimum quality requirements, including good hygiene practices during processing and an appropriate particle size. There are specialized mills available in the domestic market for dry grain milling, which can achieve an acceptable particle size.

## Sorghum Flour Production Process

Industrial and artisanal flour production requires compliance with quality requirements from the selection of raw materials to the final packaged product. The following parameters are detailed below:

## Variety Selection

Both traditional and improved varieties can be used, taking into account their physical characteristics: white or cream-colored grain, light-colored glume, and soft or floury endosperm (Fig. 24).

Figure 24. Sorghum grains.

A high concentration of tannins in the grain can make the flour somewhat astringent. Most sorghum varieties grown in El Salvador do not contain tannins. The presence of tannins can be determined qualitatively using the blanching test, which involves adding a solution of sodium hypochlorite (bleach) and sodium hydroxide to the grain. This solution removes the pericarp and pigments the testa of the grain containing tannins with a black layer (Fig. 25).

Figure 25. Blanching test of sorghum grain.

Figure 26. Cross-section of sorghum grain to check hardness.

## Protein Content

The protein content should not be less than 7.0% according to the Codex Standard for Sorghum in Grain, as determined by the Kjeldahl method in the laboratory.

## Grain Hardness

Grain hardness depends on moisture and grain structure and can be determined using a simple test, which involves cutting the grain in half and observing the proportion of hard endosperm versus floury endosperm (Figure 26). It can also be measured using a special device called a Single Hardness Tester.

## Operations for Sorghum Flour Production

## Cleaning and Washing

Remove all types of impurities and foreign materials (insects, dirt, stones). After cleaning, wash the grain with plenty of clean water.

## Drying

The washed and drained grain is dried in the sun or in an oven at a temperature between 60-80°C, stirring the grain periodically to ensure even drying without browning.

## Milling

The milling process depends on the desired products and the equipment available. The use of disc mills is recommended, as they produce the best results in terms of the particle size of cereal flours.

## Sieving

To obtain a "fine" flour, 100% of the flour must pass through a sieve with a mesh size of 0.5 mm and 1 mm for "medium" flour, according to the Codex Standard for Sorghum Flour.

## Cooling

Allow the flour to cool before packaging to prevent moisture condensation.

## Packaging and Labeling

Package the flour in polyethylene bags or aluminized bags and seal. Label as "Sorghum Flour," including the content, manufacturing date, and expiration date.

## Storage

The packaged flour should be stored in a cool, dry place, protected from light.

## Shelf Life of Flour

If good manufacturing practices are followed and the storage conditions are appropriate, a shelf life of 3-4 months can be ensured.

## QUALITY CONTROL OF SORGHUM FLOUR

Sorghum flour must be safe and suitable for human consumption, and therefore must meet the following quality characteristics:

## COLOR

The color of sorghum flour should be white or light beige, which can be ensured through proper selection of grain and removal of the glume.

## ODOR

It should have a characteristic sorghum flour odor, and should not possess any unusual odors such as: musty, earthy, moldy, etc.

## FOREIGN MATTER:

Sorghum flour must be free of dirt, insects, stones, and other materials that could pose a health risk.

## MOISTURE

The moisture content should not exceed 14%. Assess climate, transportation time, and storage conditions.

## TANNIN CONTENT

The tannin content of sorghum flour should not exceed 0.3% of the dry matter.

## PARTICLE SIZE

At least 100% of the flour should pass through a sieve with a mesh size of 0.5 mm for "fine" flour and 1 mm for "medium" flour.

## HEAVY METALS

Sorghum flour must be free of heavy metals in amounts that could pose a health risk. The maximum allowable limit for arsenic and copper content is 1.0 mg/kg.

## PESTICIDE AND MYCOTOXIN RESIDUES

Sorghum flour must comply with the maximum limits established by the Codex Alimentarius Commission for this product.

## MICROBIOLOGICAL QUALITY

Sorghum flour must be free of microorganisms and parasites in amounts that could pose a health risk.
