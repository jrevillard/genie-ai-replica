## Botany and Phenology

The lemon (Citrus latifolia, Tanaka), also known as the Tahitian lemon, is a medium to large-sized tree with vigorous growth, an extended shape, and few thorns.

The foliage is dense and green, with medium-sized, lance-shaped leaves and winged petioles. New leaves and shoots are typically purplish in color.

Flowering occurs throughout much of the year, primarily during the months of May-June.

The fruits are medium to large in size; they are oval, oblong, or slightly elliptical, with a base that is generally rounded; a round apex, and an elevated auricled surface on a small mound. The seeds are scarce or absent.

The rind is generally thin, with a smooth surface and a pale yellow color when ripe; however, for export, the fruit should have a rind with a slightly rough, dark green surface and a medium size.

On average, the fruits are ready for harvest 100-120 days after flowering and have approximately 10 segments with a small, generally solid, central axis; the pulp is pale greenish-yellow, juicy, and acidic.

The juice represents about 50% of the fruit's weight, with an average Brix of 9%, an acidity of 6%, a total soluble solids (TSS) to acidity ratio of 1.5, and an ascorbic acid content ranging from 20 to 40 mg/100 ml (Hodgson, 1967, Passor et al, 1977, Figueiredo, 1986, and Marcondes, 1991).

Knowledge of the lemon's phenology in tropical conditions is of great importance for optimizing cultivation management and establishing the necessary conditions for increased productivity.

## Environmental Factors

Among the environmental factors that influence the commercial production of lemon, the following are important: temperature, water, light, and wind.

<!-- image -->

## Temperature

Generally, lemons thrive at temperatures between 25°C and 31°C, as this range promotes maximum growth and frequent flowering, although it can also lead to periods of water stress. In contrast, temperatures between 12°C and 13°C cause most citrus species to stop growing.

## Water Requirements

The continuous production of lemons requires reasonable amounts of water for irrigation during periods of water stress, to ensure that the harvest is not interrupted. A level of 150-180 mm per month is sufficient to meet the plant's needs. Water stress can lead to the interruption of flowering, fruit set, premature fruit ripening, smaller fruits, and a lower juice content.

## Light

For the lemon tree and its fruits to develop properly, they require high levels of light. It has been observed that as light intensity decreases, the growth of new shoots becomes elongated and weaker, flowering is reduced, the fruits become more yellow, and there is an increased incidence of insects and diseases. Therefore, it is necessary for producers to carry out pruning to improve light penetration.

## Wind

Due to its dense foliage, wind is a factor that should be considered when establishing a plantation. It is not recommended to establish plantations in areas exposed to winds with speeds greater than 20 km/h, as this can damage the leaves and cause fruit to rub against the branches, which can negatively affect the quality, to the point that it is not marketable. Therefore, the use of windbreaks is recommended.

## Rootstock Selection

It is important to note that using only one rootstock in lemon cultivation in El Salvador is dangerous, especially when continuing to graft onto sour orange, which is currently known to be highly susceptible to the viral disease tristeza. Therefore, it is recommended to use more than two rootstocks with proven tolerance or resistance to known diseases, as new diseases are constantly emerging.

Using different rootstocks with varying degrees of tolerance or resistance minimizes the risk of severe damage in commercial plantations.

The disease known as Gummosis, caused by Phytophthora sp, is the main cause of tree death in the country. Therefore, it is advisable to select rootstocks that also have tolerance or resistance to this disease.

## Recommended Rootstocks by CENTA:

- Cleopatra mandarin
- Citrus Volkameriana (Pasquales)
- Trifoliate: Swingle, Citranges Troyer and Carrizo.

## The main characteristics of some of these are:

## Cleopatra Mandarin

This rootstock produces vigorous plants, has a long productive lifespan, high yields, and fruits of good quality, comparable to those produced on sour orange. It is tolerant of viruses that cause tristeza, xyloporosis, psorosis, and exocortis. It is also tolerant of Gummosis. One kilogram of seed contains approximately 10,500 units.

## Swingle Citrus

This rootstock is resistant to root rot caused by Phytophthora and to tristeza. It is moderately resistant to drought. It produces numerous fruits. One kilogram of seed contains approximately 7,750 units.

## Citrus Volkameriana

This rootstock is tolerant of Phytophthora, tristeza, exocortis, psorosis, and xyloporosis; it is also a very vigorous rootstock. It produces high yields of lemons. One kilogram of seed from this rootstock contains approximately 12,000 seeds.

## Characteristics of a grafted plant

The grafted plant must be of unquestionable quality, complying with certain requirements, such as:

- Provenance and guaranteed health by the General Directorate of Plant and Animal Health (DGSVA) of the MAG.
- Rootstock used, resistant and/or tolerant to known diseases, recommended by CENTA
- Grafting at a height of 20-30 cm
- Possessing 2-4 branches at a height of 40-60 cm from the ground
- Well-developed root system
- Plants of 1 year old, in nursery bags of 8x14' to 9x14'
- The graft and rootstock should not have a diameter difference greater than 0.5 cm
- The cut of the rootstock should be healed and the plant should not have broken or torn branches.

## Soil

The citrus tree is not very demanding on certain types of soil, it adapts to a wide variety, ranging from sandy soils to relatively clayey soils; however, the most suitable soils are well-drained and deep soils with a pH of 5.5 to 6.5.

Clay soils should be avoided, as they hinder the development and aeration of the root system, which harms the growth of the plants and creates conditions that favor the incidence of certain diseases, such as phytophthora.

## Cultivation Establishment

Once the area for planting is selected, the activities for establishing the crop consist of: weed removal, deep plowing, when the topography of the land allows, and incorporating lime if necessary.

## Planting

Planting begins by placing 10 pounds of organic matter at the bottom of the hole; subsequently, soil is added and compacted until it reaches the height of the bag, and 4 ounces of 16-20-0 and 4 to 5 grams of a nematicide insecticide are added, covering this with a layer of 0.02 m of soil, and the plant is placed in the planting hole, so that the base of the plant is slightly above the level of the soil. (when the planting hole is half full, the remaining 4 ounces of the formula and 3 to 4 grams of the nematicide insecticide are added, and the soil is continued to be placed and compacted). The space between the roots is filled with soil, leaving the same in the position they were in the nursery. After planting, a dike is made around the plant, it is watered abundantly, and a mulch of dry straw is placed, and a tutor is attached, placed parallel to the plant and in an opposite position to the graft union.

## Staking

Staking for marking the area should comply with the recommended distances. In El Salvador, the recommended distances for lemon trees are from 6 x 3 to 7 x 5 m, which provide populations of 555 plants/ha (389 plants/m2) and 286 plants/ha (200 plants/m2), respectively. Small variations around these recommendations can be adopted without compromising the success of the crop.

## Hilling

Hilling for lemon planting will depend on the type of soil; generally, the recommended dimensions are 0.40 x 0.40 x 0.40m to 0.60 x 0.60 x 0.60 m, leaving the position of the stake used in the center of the hole, where the plant will be placed.

In the case of large plantations, it is advisable to divide them into lots and parcels, using roads that allow for vehicle traffic without damaging the trees' branches.

A lot is the unit that represents a cost center in a large property. The lot should have a size large enough to subdivide it into parcels, which will be the units for harvesting. The roads, primarily for vehicles, should be parallel, dividing the plantation into parcels with a maximum of 400-500 m, in the direction perpendicular to the contour lines. Secondary roads can be scheduled every 10-12 meters between rows for harvesting.

## Pruning

Pruning citrus trees is a practice that should be carried out very rationally to achieve the desired results, taking into account the climate, the objectives of the pruning, the nutritional state of the plant, and other factors. In general, some basic principles should be considered:

Fruit production pruning

<!-- image -->

- To obtain good fruit production, it is necessary for the tree to have an adequate number of productive branches. If these branches are pruned, only vegetative growth will be stimulated; conversely, if younger branches (less than 6 months old) are pruned, flowering will occur in 5-6 weeks.
- It is important to avoid imbalances between foliage and fruit production. This relationship will determine the yield per tree.

In addition, all wounds should be covered with copper-based wound dressings and/or Bordeaux mixture. The same should be done with branches and trunks that are exposed to the sun.

## Productivity and Yield

Citrus is one of the most precocious citrus varieties, generally producing significantly in the third year and beyond.

For comparison, it is worth mentioning that in Florida, experimental plantings produce an average of 9.1 to 13.6 kg per plant (91 to 136 fruits) in the third year; 27.2 to 40.9 kg in the fourth year; 59.0 to 81.7 kg in the fifth year, and 90.8 to 113.5 kg per plant in the sixth year. After this period, the yield per plant will depend on the spacing used. In Brazil (São Paulo), data from commercial plantings indicate the following production curve:

Research studies conducted in commercial plantings have shown that irrigation and pruning management, combined with higher nitrogen fertilization and foliar applications, have resulted in higher yields during the dry season, which is when prices per unit are highest, mainly in March and April. Pruning for fruit production, when performed, induces flowering 5-6 weeks after.

## Intercropping

The use of intercropping in citrus cultivation is a common practice in small and medium-sized properties. This practice offers several advantages, such as: income during the unproductive phase of the main crop, rational use of fertilizers, better utilization of factors such as water, soil, light, and labor, and protection against erosion.

Sometimes, the association aims to increase the availability of nutrients, especially nitrogen, by using nitrogen-fixing legumes, and incorporating a large amount of green matter into the soil after harvest.

To ensure the success of this system, it is important to consider the following recommendations:

- a. Prefer small-sized and short-duration crops such as: beans, corn, sweet potatoes, vegetables, papayas, pineapples, peanuts, and some cover legumes.
- b. Maintain the intercrop at a minimum distance of 1.5 m from the citrus tree.
- c. Supply the nutritional needs of the intercrop with specific fertilizers.
- d. Orient the intercrop as much as possible in an East-West direction to reduce shading.
- e. Remove the intercrop when it starts to compete with the citrus trees, gradually limiting it to the center of the row as the citrus trees develop.
- f. In the case of intercrops with high growth, the minimum distance from the citrus rows should be 2.0 m to minimize competition for space.

## Procedures for collecting leaf samples

To perform nutritional analysis using leaves, take 3 or 4 leaves.

Source: E Malavolta 1979.

Some criteria that should be considered when taking leaf samples are:

1. The leaves to be collected should be 5-7 months old, of medium size, and free of pests and diseases.
2. The leaves should be cut near the plant, at a medium height between the base and the upper part of the canopy.
3. The samples should be taken from fruiting or non-fruiting branches, but without mixing the two types of leaves.
4. An area of 2.5 ha (3.6 m2) represents a sample of 100 leaves, taken from 4-5 branches with 20-25 trees.
5. The collected leaves should be placed in paper or plastic bags and, if not taken to the laboratory immediately, should be stored in refrigerators, but not frozen (Malavolta, 1983).

## Irrigation

Irrigation allows plants to maintain a constant flow of water and nutrients from the soil to the leaves, promoting photosynthesis and transpiration, resulting in more vigorous trees with larger and better fruits, increased leaf cover, and consequently, increased productivity.

Statistically, starting in November, the rainy season ends and the dry season begins. Irrigation should begin immediately to replenish the water lost from the soil, and not wait until the soil is completely dry. This also helps the fruit to develop fully.

## Selection of irrigation method

The selection of the irrigation method to use is highly influenced by the availability of water, soil, topography, climate, labor capacity, energy, and the relative cost of each of these resources. It has been considered that the initial investment, operating and maintenance costs, and irrigation efficiency are the most important parameters.

Numerous studies have compared different irrigation methods in terms of the advantages and disadvantages of one method over the others, and have also compared the costs of surface irrigation with sprinkler irrigation. It has been found that pressurized irrigation methods are superior to surface methods in terms of yield, soil conservation, and water conservation, however, when comparing different irrigation methods based on soil conservation and water conservation, it has been

concluded that the selection should be made taking into account the cost of the systems and their effect on water management and conservation.

The following is a brief description of each of the irrigation systems that can be applied to lemon cultivation.

## Surface Irrigation

## Furrow Method

Gravity irrigation is the most commonly used method and requires large amounts of water, a soil texture ideal for (sandy to clayey) and a natural topography or suitable for its implementation (flat, semi-flat, or with level curves).

This method consists of carrying the water to the field by means of channels or pipes. The water is discharged onto the land with siphons or pipes with valves. The water extends in an indefinite manner through the furrows in the direction of the slope, the excess water is collected in the drainage channel.

It is suitable for all types, except sandy soils with very poor water infiltration, soils with high salt concentrations, and soils that crack, which can lead to furrow overflow or erosion.

The furrow method requires a topographic adaptation of the land before planting, with this practice, the minimum slope of the furrows is achieved to manage the irrigation water properly. In places where the precipitation is not intense, a slope of 3% can be accepted, and in areas with high precipitation that cause erosion, a slope of 0.5% can be accepted. Generally, the slope should be less than 1% in the furrows, and the land should be level with uniform slopes. In this case, the furrows can be transverse to the natural slope of the land, which can be up to 15%. The length of the furrows depends on the infiltration rate, the maximum allowable flow in the furrow, which in turn depends on the slope and erodability of the soil.

In soils with poor permeability, the irrigation furrows can be more than 200 meters long. For sandy soils, it is recommended that the length of the furrow is not greater than 70 meters.

## Drip Irrigation

The irrigation water is pumped from the source, transported through pipes to the drip emitters, and then sprayed into the air, resembling rain.

There are different types of water output:

1.  Rotating sprinklers.
2.  Fixed sprinklers.
3.  Perforated tubes.
4.  Micro-sprinklers.

Recommended for all types of soil with infiltration rates greater than 0.5 cm/hour. It works very well in sandy soils. The maximum recommended slope for use is 20%.

When there is no adequate water distribution, waterlogging often occurs, usually where the sprinklers are placed, causing an imbalance in the oxygen/water ratio and, as a result, damage to the plant.

There is a variety of drip emitter models available on the market, and the catalogs provided by suppliers specify the diameter of the emitter, its capacity, operating pressure, and wetting diameter, etc.

Micro-sprinklers are being used in some plantations in the country, and globally, they are among the most used. The system operates at low pressure (100-300 kPa) and uses micro-sprinklers that discharge between 20-175 l/h.

Waterlogging should be avoided, as it can shorten the lifespan of the tree in the long term.

## Drip Irrigation

This system allows for watering trees using devices called drip emitters, which are inserted into plastic hoses, providing a flow rate of 2 to 10 l/hour, wetting a portion of the soil surface occupied by the crop.

Around each emitter, a zone of moist soil is formed, called a "bulge," due to its characteristic shape.

The characteristics of the soil determine the movement of water under drip irrigation. Therefore, there is a relationship between the horizontal dimension (radius of wetting) and the vertical dimension (depth of wetting) in which the irrigation water is distributed. Both dimensions constitute the limits of the wetted bulge.

## Drip Emitter Output

The lateral (horizontal) distribution of water depends on the output of the drip emitter. A 2 l/h emitter produces a narrower bulge than a 4 or 8 l/h emitter.

When comparing sandy soil with clay soil, we will see that the former requires a drip emitter with a higher output and a smaller spacing than the latter.

## Irrigation Duration

The longer the irrigation, the greater the horizontal dimension, up to a certain limit. Beyond this limit, the water is lost below the root zone, reducing the efficiency of the irrigation.

## Irrigation Frequency

As the soil dries, the tension with which it retains water increases. High tensions reduce the speed of water movement in the soil. Therefore, drip irrigation in a dry soil will produce a bulge that is too narrow, requiring frequent watering.

## Watering Frequency

Watering frequency is defined as the number of days it takes for the irrigation layer to be used up, and it is determined by the ratio between the irrigation layer and the daily consumption.

The frequency of watering depends on the type of soil, meaning that a clay soil will not retain water for as long as a sandy soil. Therefore, the normal watering frequency for a given area in a clay soil is 10-12 days, while in sandy soils, two waterings should be provided at these intervals with half the flow rate.

In sandy soils, frequent watering with a low flow rate is preferable, in order to keep the root zone moist and prevent fertilizers from accumulating in layers where the root system may not be able to access the moisture and fertilizers.

Once the volume of water per plant per month is known, it is necessary to distribute it over the days of the month.

As a guideline:

| Month       | Days per week   |
|-----------|---------------------|
| January     | 2-3                 |
| February   | 2-3                 |
| March     | 3-4                 |
| April     | 3-4                 |
| May      | 4-5                 |
| December | 2-3                 |

A direct way to determine the watering frequency is by using a tensiometer.

## Tensiometer

There are direct methods to indicate watering, based on the moisture content of the soil by taking soil samples. In Florida, it is recommended to maintain a moisture level of no less than 55-65% of the field capacity, especially from flowering until the fruit exceeds 25 mm in diameter, using a drier regime for the rest of the year. Tensiometers are very helpful in this regard.

The tensiometer mainly consists of an instrument made of a porous ceramic or porcelain capsule connected to a tube containing a vacuum or pressure gauge and used to measure the water tension in the soil.

Once the tensiometer is filled with water and installed in the root zone, it can measure the tension of the soil moisture, which is directly related to the soil moisture content. As the soil dries, the water inside the tensiometer is drawn out through the porous capsule by the soil matrix, creating a partial vacuum that is detected by the pressure gauge. This vacuum is approximately equal to the suction potential of the soil at the current moisture content. When the soil is watered, the water moves from the moist soil to the inside of the tensiometer, reducing the vacuum, and the pressure gauge detects the new tension or relative soil moisture content.

Tensiometers are calibrated to work in a range of values between 0 and 100 centibars. A reading of zero indicates a saturated soil, while a maximum reading typically reaches 75 centibars indicates that the soil lacks available moisture for the plant and needs to be replenished to field capacity. For fruit trees, it is recommended to water when the tensiometer reads 50-60%. The depth of placement of the tensiometer in lemon trees is 18 to 36 inches.

## Pests

## Insects.

## 1. Woolly Aphids,

*   *Aphis citri* (Comst., 1983). *Pinus aspidistria* (Sign., 1869)

This scale insect mainly develops on the trunk and branches of plants, presenting a whitish coloration. The intense sucking of sap by the insect causes, among other damages, cracking of the trunk and branches, facilitating the entry of pathogenic organisms, especially fungi of the *Phytophthora* genus, which causes gummosis. Control can be carried out by painting the trunk and main branches with a mixture of: 1 kg of Sulfur; 2 kg of Lime; 0.5 kg of table salt; 15 g. of Diazinon or 35 cc of Malathion in 15 liters of water.

## 2. Citrus Leafminer,

*   *Phyllocnistis citrella* stnt.

The larvae are light gray in color and can reach up to 3.5 mm in length, emerging the larvae break the epidermis as they feed on the plant tissue, forming long and sinuous galleries, often in a serpentine shape.

When the damage is recent, the young leaves affected have a silvery coloration. The damage is almost always observed on the underside of the leaves (envés); however, damage has also been found on the upper side (haz).

In cases of severe damage, the leaves dry, affecting the photosynthetic function of the plant. Mature leaves are not attacked, however, if the damage to young shoots is continuous, the plants will be affected, reducing fruit production.

## Control

In established orchards, widespread application of pesticides is not recommended, as they can decimate natural controllers (wasps, ladybugs, and others). If the problem occurs in nurseries, and is very severe, chemical control is recommended using: Ciflutrin, Basudin, Disulfoton, Endosulfan, and Cartap, among others.

## 3. Brown Scale

*Phyllocoptruta oleivora (Ashm., 1879)*

This scale insect infects leaves, branches, and fruits. On the latter, it causes damage to the epidermal cells, which turn a dark brown and have a rough appearance.

Fruits infested with the scale insect generally have reduced size, weight, and juice content. As a consequence of the infestation, the leaves also exhibit a symptom called "Greasy Spot," caused by the fungus *Mycosphaerela sp.*, which develops in the affected areas (Puzzi; Arruda, 1974). In severe cases, abundant leaf and fruit drop is observed.

For chemical control, it is recommended to collect a sample of the population, taking randomly 1% of the plants in each 2000-plant lot and 5 fruits from each plant. Control should be carried out when 10% of the fruits show 30 or more scales. The following insecticides can be used:

Disulfoton, Copper Sulphate, Abamectin, among others.

## 4. Woolly Aphids

*Crysomphalus ficus (Ashm., 1880)*

This woolly aphid is circular, convex, and violet in color, with a lighter border, measuring about 2 mm in diameter, and resembles the head of a nail. Under favorable conditions, with dry periods and high temperatures, populations multiply rapidly, reaching up to five generations per year. The dust also benefits the pest, as it repels natural enemies and prevents the development of entomopathogenic fungi.

The insect is mainly located on the underside of the leaves and on the fruits, forming dense clusters. The presence on the fruits means a loss in commercial value, as the insect is not easily removed during the packaging process. Control should be carried out in a targeted manner, in order to preserve the population of natural enemies. Emulsifiable mineral oil at 1%, either alone or in combination with phosphated insecticides, provides effective control.

## Diseases

Various diseases caused by viruses, fungi, bacteria, and physiological disorders affect citrus, causing serious problems for producers. Among the main diseases are: tristeza, exocortis, gomosis, and fruit stem rot.

## Diseases caused by viruses

## 1. Tristeza

Plants affected show reduced growth. This disease is sometimes observed even in the nursery stage. In adult plants, a reduction in the diameter of the trunk compared to the rootstock is generally observed. It is transmitted by grafting and by insects. The main vector is the *Aphis citricidus* aphid.

Branches, cuttings, and even the trunk generally show symptoms of "stem pitting" (Stempitting), which are visible, and sometimes it is necessary to remove the peel to observe these channels. Branches of plants infected with the virus also have shorter internodes than normal, and new leaves generally have pale veins. In the productive phase, a large number of small fruits are commonly observed.

The control of tristeza is based on the use of scion material from virus-free matrices and on the use of resistant or tolerant rootstock varieties, such as: *Mandarina Cleopatra, C. Swingle, C. Volkameriana, C. Carrizo*, among others. *Orange* is extremely susceptible.

## 2. Exocortis

Plants infested show reduced growth, the vegetation is on the sides, and the foliage has little shine. From the fourth year onwards, in susceptible rootstock varieties, scale-like formations appear at the base of the plant, generally accompanied by gum exudation. Lemon clones infected with exocortis show cracks or depressions in the trunk and thick branches.

This disease is transmitted by grafting through scion material from trees infected with exocortis, as well as by contaminated tools such as pruning shears and grafting knives.

Therefore, control should be based on a program of scion material from exocortis-free trees; use of resistant and/or tolerant rootstock varieties such as: *Mandarina Cleopatra, C. Volkameriana, and C. Swingle*. Susceptible rootstocks are: *Rangpur Lime, Poncirus trifoliata and its hybrids, Troyer and Carrizo*.

## Main diseases caused by fungi

1. Gomosis. Gomosis is one of the diseases that cause many problems in citrus cultivation in El Salvador. It is responsible for the death of many trees.

The initial symptoms are characterized by the appearance of dark lesions at the base or neck of the plant, in the roots, in the lower branches, and with the presence of gum exudation, from which its name comes. In a more advanced stage, necrosis of the tissues occurs, which are exposed to the penetration of other secondary agents, and yellowing of the foliage in branches corresponding to the affected area. When the lesion reaches the entire periphery of the trunk, the plant dies rapidly depending on the interruption of the sap flow.

The causative agents are fungi of the genus *Phytophthora* ( *P. citrophthora* and *P. parasitica*). In favorable conditions, the fungi affect the parts of the plant in contact with the soil, or the higher parts of the trunk, through splashing of water, or also, through the tools used in the cultural practices. Several factors favor the appearance of gomosis, including: temperature, humidity, susceptibility of the rootstock-scion combination, heavy soils, fertilization, irrigation, and cultural practices.

Control

The disease affects both young and adult plants. Some recommended preventive measures are:

Use of resistant and/or tolerant rootstock

Planting at a height of 0.20-0.30 m from the ground

Facilitate air circulation at the base of the trunk to reduce humidity

Apply copper paste or Bordeaux mixture annually to the trunk and main branches

Applications of Fosetyl-al (Alliete) have shown excellent results in controlling the healing of advanced lesions, both in foliar sprays and in paste applied with brushes to the trunk and main branches.

## 2. Fruit Soft Rot

Soft rot in Persian limes is a physiological disorder that occurs after harvest and affects fruits that are very close to ripening, or fruits that are already ripe.

The loss and waste of fruits due to soft rot can reach up to 40%. The symptoms are caused by the rupture of juice vesicles, which are located on the periphery of the fruit lobes. The released juice invades the peel through the central axis, causing decay of the tissues. The small lesion that forms initially has a dark color and tends to spread, occupying a considerable area. The fruit, once damaged, is rejected for commercial sale.

Due to the association of this problem with the stage of ripening and with heat (factors that are correlated with the incidence of soft rot), the following control measures are recommended:

- Harvest the fruits before the ripening phase begins (at a stage where the peel is rough and the color is dark green).
- Control the temperature of the fruits during and after harvest, keeping the fruits stored in the shade and avoiding contact with the soil or damp leaves.
- Carry out the harvest during the cooler hours of the morning or evening, but without the fruits having water droplets from spraying or rain.

## Floral Induction

This practice is used in Persian limes to promote lime production during the period when lime prices are highest (January-April), so it starts from the time it takes for a lime to ripen, which is 100 to 120 days.

One of the floral induction practices being used is pruning, which involves cutting the tips of the productive branches (0.10 - 0.15 m), and then applying nitrogen fertilization to the soil, which will promote the growth of new shoots with flowering 5 or 6 weeks later. To ensure maximum flowering, it is necessary to accompany the shoot growth with foliar applications of mineral salts such as potassium nitrate 2%, zinc sulfate and boron 1 kg/100 liters of water, respectively. It should be noted that the success of this work depends largely on the plant not being subjected to water stress.

## Harvesting

As the final stage of cultivation, harvesting is extremely important, as small oversights can expose the entire investment made. The percentage of rejection, the durability of the fruit, its shelf life in supermarkets, the taste, and the quality of the industrial juice all depend largely on the care taken during harvesting.

The practice of harvesting and transporting Persian limes requires attention to avoid damage that can damage the fruit, cause cell rupture, oleocelosis and fruit decay. For this reason, in addition to the precautions mentioned, the use of a minimum of materials and equipment is necessary, consisting of:

- 20 kg canvas bags with a false bottom and hook and strap closure, so that the fruits can be removed from the bottom without causing damage.
- 27 kg plastic baskets.
- It is important to avoid harvesting fruits that are still wet from spraying or morning dew, and to place them directly on the ground, for which the use of baskets is necessary to avoid the appearance of stains, lesions and bruises.

Limes in baskets

<!-- image -->

It is common to observe in the field that the harvesting of Persian limes is carried out by people without the proper preparation, using extremely damaging methods to the fruit and the plant itself. The ideal would be to carry out the harvest using scissors, cutting at the base of the fruit.

Among the most common errors observed in the harvesting phase are:

- Cutting the fruits in an improper way, with the help of sticks or hooks, is a practice that can not only damage the fruit, but also tear branches or cause leaves, flowers and small fruits to fall that have not reached their commercial stage.
- Harvesting fruits that are wet, which facilitates the appearance of dark stains, diseases or decay.
- Placing the harvested fruit directly on the ground, which causes the appearance of stains, lesions and bruises.
- Exposing the fruit to the sun for too long, which causes requem in the peel and alters the taste.

- Harvesting fruits that are excessively ripe or unripe.

It is important to avoid using ladders leaning against the tree, as the branches of the Persian lime are not very strong, so the use of double-legged ladders is recommended.

Persian limes for export should be harvested, following certain indicators, such as:

- The fruit should be of medium size (not smaller than 47 mm in diameter).
- The peel of the fruit should have a slightly rough appearance.
- The color of the peel should be dark green.
- The harvested fruits should not be exposed to the sun for too long, to avoid requem and alteration in taste.

Like all crops, limes can be fully industrialized, as the foliage that is cut when pruning the trees can be used to extract essential oils (aromatherapy).

Fruits intended for juice processors should have reached their physiological maturity, that is, have a color between dark green and light green, with a smooth peel, and should also be completely healthy and without any bruises, as this reduces the quality of the juice to be extracted. It is important to consider indicators such as juice extraction levels (90%).

The final byproducts of the process, such as the peel, can be utilized in the production of pectin.

## Post-harvest handling

The exported lemons must be handled properly from harvest, with a more rigorous pre-selection in the field, considering the quality requirements of the buyer and the time of transport to their destination market.

For classification and packaging, a series of activities are carried out, of which we will mention the main ones.

- a) Washing the fruit with chlorinated water (to prevent field pathogens from damaging the fruit and lower the field temperature)
- b) Drying the fruit with forced air using fans.
- c) Selecting the fruit, discarding those that do not meet the required standards (fruits with white or yellow spots, insect damage, scratches from rubbing against branches, etc.).
- d) Homogeneous paraffin coating of the fruit
- e) Drying the fruit with forced air using fans
- f) Selecting and filling boxes according to size, of fruit. This phase is carried out by trained personnel. The final selection is made; the boxes can range from 10 lbs, and the number of fruits per box depends on the requirements of the buyer.
- g) The boxes are packed in wooden pallets, and sturdy corner protectors to keep them stable during transport. Each pallet can hold 180 boxes of 10 lbs.
- h) Storage in refrigerated rooms at an average temperature of 10°C and 85-90% humidity.
- i) Transport in refrigerated containers under the same temperature and humidity conditions.

## Marketing

The price of Peruvian lemons is not always attractive, due to the concentrated supply during the rainy season. This situation should encourage the search for alternatives that can alter the flowering period, forcing production during the dry season, mainly during the months of January to May.
