# Tobacco cessation — fictive patient chats

Illustrative dialogues between a person who smokes (or is trying to quit)
and a health assistant. Derived from the QuitNowTXT message library
(Annex 2 of WHO *Be He@lthy Be Mobile — mTobaccoCessation handbook*) and
the *Gambia cessation clinical guidelines* (2016). All names and
specifics are invented; numbers and recommendations are illustrative.

---

## Chat 1 — Thinking about quitting

> **Ebrima:** I smoke about 15 a day. My wife wants me to quit. I don't
> know if I can.

That's a normal feeling. Almost everyone who has ever quit had that
exact thought. The fact that you're even thinking about it means you've
already done the hardest part — deciding to consider it.

Let me ask you two things: do you want to quit for yourself as well, or
just to keep peace at home? And how soon after waking do you smoke your
first cigarette of the day?

> **Ebrima:** Within 30 minutes of waking. And, honestly, I'd be glad
> to quit — I can feel I get out of breath climbing stairs now.

That early-morning cigarette tells me your nicotine addiction is in the
moderate-to-strong range, which means medication (like nicotine patches
or gum) would probably help a lot. The breathlessness you mentioned is
worth taking seriously — it usually improves within weeks of quitting.

Here's how I'd suggest starting:

1. Pick a **Quit Day** in the next 1-2 weeks. Choose a day with low
   stress.
2. Between now and then, **identify your triggers** — what times of day
   and which situations make you reach for one.
3. Tell your wife the date, and ask her to support you (not nag — there
   is a difference).
4. Talk to a clinic about nicotine replacement (patch or gum). It
   roughly doubles your chance of staying smoke-free.

Want me to help you build your trigger list and a substitute for each?

## Chat 2 — Quit Day morning

> **Mariama:** Today is my Quit Day. I'm already wanting one and it's
> only 7am.

You're stronger than you think. The first morning is the worst — you've
trained your body to expect that wake-up cigarette for years, and it's
asking for it now. **Cravings feel huge but they pass in 5-10 minutes.**

Try right now:
- Pour yourself a glass of cold water.
- Take 10 slow, deep breaths.
- Step outside for 2 minutes if you can.
- Do something with your hands — wash dishes, fold a shirt, anything.

Have you eaten breakfast? Hunger sharpens cravings.

> **Mariama:** Just had tea.

Tea is one of the most common pairings with cigarettes — if your
morning tea has always come with a smoke, the tea by itself will
trigger the craving for a while. Try changing the ritual: switch to
coffee, switch to a different mug, drink the tea standing up or in a
different room. Anything that breaks the old script.

Throughout today: keep busy, keep moving, keep your reasons-for-quitting
list nearby. You can do this — millions of ex-smokers have done it, one
day at a time.

## Chat 3 — A slip on day 5

> **Lamin:** I caved. I had a cigarette with my friend last night. I
> feel like I ruined everything.

You didn't ruin anything. One cigarette is a slip, not a relapse. The
4 days you were smoke-free still count. **The question now isn't
whether you slipped — it's what you do next.**

Two questions:
1. What was happening right before you smoked? Who were you with, what
   were you feeling, where were you?
2. How many cigarettes did you have — one, or the whole pack?

> **Lamin:** Just one. I was with my friend who smokes and he offered
> me one. After a few beers I just took it.

Two big triggers there: a smoking friend, and alcohol. Both are very
common relapse triggers, especially together. That doesn't mean you
need to avoid your friend forever — it means for the next few weeks,
when you're with him, you either don't drink, or you ask him to not
offer.

Here's what to do today:

- **Don't smoke today.** That's all you need to commit to right now.
- **Recommit to your Quit Day.** Pick this morning as a fresh start.
- **Reach out** to whoever is supporting you — tell them you slipped,
  what triggered it, and what you're going to do differently.

Most ex-smokers had several slips before they quit for good. You're not
back to square one — you have more information about your triggers than
you did a week ago. That's progress.

## Chat 4 — Asking about hookah / shisha

> **Aminata:** I don't smoke cigarettes but I do shisha with friends on
> weekends. That's safer, right?

Unfortunately, no. A typical shisha session lasts about 45 minutes to
an hour, and during that time you inhale about as much smoke as 100
cigarettes worth — sometimes more. Plus you get extra carbon monoxide
from the charcoal.

The risks are the same family as cigarettes: heart disease, lung
disease, mouth and throat cancer. The water in the pipe does not
filter out the harmful chemicals — that's a common misconception.

> **Aminata:** But I only do it twice a month.

Twice a month is much better than weekly. The damage from any tobacco
exposure adds up over years, so reducing how often you do it matters.
The lowest-risk option is to stop, but if you're not ready to do that
yet, two things to think about:

- Could you go to the same social gatherings without the shisha — drink
  juice or tea with your friends instead?
- Could you make it once a month for the next few months, and see if
  you can let it go from there?

There's no need to be all-or-nothing today.

## Chat 5 — Withdrawal symptoms

> **Ousman:** I'm on day 3 and I want to bite someone's head off. Is
> this normal?

Completely normal. **Irritability peaks around day 2-3 and starts
easing by the end of week 1.** This is your brain rebalancing without
nicotine. Other things you might be feeling: trouble sleeping,
difficulty focusing, hunger, mild headaches.

These are signs your body is healing, not signs that quitting is wrong
for you.

A few things that help in the next 4-5 days:
- **Drink lots of water** — it helps your body clear the nicotine and
  reduces the headaches.
- **Move your body** — even a 15-minute walk lowers irritability
  noticeably.
- **Sleep early** if you can — sleep is your best ally this week.
- **Avoid coffee** if it's making you more jittery. Switch to herbal tea
  or water for a couple of weeks.
- **Tell people what you're going through** so they don't take the
  irritability personally.

Have you been using nicotine patches or gum? If not, and the cravings
or irritability are unbearable, talk to a pharmacist or clinic this
week. It is much more effective to use medication than to rely on
willpower alone.

## Chat 6 — Relapsed and back

> **Binta:** I quit for 3 months but I've been smoking again for 6
> weeks. I'm so disappointed in myself.

Three smoke-free months is a real achievement, not something to throw
away. Many people who eventually quit for good went through several
cycles of quitting and relapsing first. That's not failure — that's
how this works for most people.

Let's think practically. What happened around the time you started
smoking again? Was it a stressful event, a social situation, a
particular trigger that came up?

> **Binta:** My father was sick and I started just to cope.

Stress and loss are the most common reasons people relapse. You found
something that helped you cope at a hard time — that's understandable.
And now that the worst of that period has passed, you can decide what
to do next.

If you're ready to try again, here's what is different this time:

- **You know you can do 3 months smoke-free** — that's proof you can do
  it.
- **You know one of your big triggers is stress.** Next time, you can
  plan ahead: who will you call, what will you do, instead of reaching
  for a cigarette?
- **Medication helps a lot** if you didn't use it last time. Talk to a
  health worker about patches, gum or tablets.

Want to set a Quit Day for the next 1-2 weeks?

## Chat 7 — Family member smoking around children

> **Modou:** My uncle smokes in the house when he visits, and my baby
> son is just learning to crawl. I don't want to be rude.

Your son's health comes first — being respectful to your uncle and
asking him not to smoke inside are not opposites.

A way to frame it: "Uncle, I love when you visit. I have to ask one
thing — could you please smoke outside, even just on the porch? The
baby is breathing whatever's in the room, and I want him to be okay."

Most people, asked kindly, will respect that. Second-hand smoke is a
known cause of asthma, ear infections, slower lung development and
cot death in young children. It's not a small thing.

If your uncle smokes outside but then comes back in, his clothes, his
hair and his breath still carry the smoke — what's called "third-hand
smoke" — and it lingers in soft furnishings. Air the room out and ask
him to wash his hands before holding the baby.

Would your uncle be open to thinking about quitting himself? Often, a
family member's reason — a baby, a grandchild, a sick parent — is the
strongest motivation.
