## INTRODUCTION

There are a variety of diseases that affect the bee *Apis mellifera*, but it is necessary for the beekeeper to learn about some of these diseases, especially those that affect the brood, as failing to treat them in time can lead to losses of live material and economic losses.

None of the bee diseases are transmitted to humans, but some of the products used to control them can affect human health if not used according to the instructions.

This manual will cover the most common diseases and pests that cause economic damage to beekeepers.

Bee diseases can be described in different ways, but for the purposes of this manual, in Chapter I, the diseases that affect the brood will be defined, and in Chapter II, the diseases that affect the adult bees will be defined. Each chapter includes subdivisions related to the agent that causes the disease, how to diagnose it, and finally, how to prevent or solve the problem if evidence of its presence is found in the hives.

Experienced beekeepers can identify diseases in the brood, but diseases in adult bees can only be detected through laboratory analysis of samples, and it is recommended to do this monthly.

A healthy hive should have a vigorous population of bees, with different stages of development in the brood. The queen bee should lay eggs in a uniform, concentric pattern, with larvae that are white and plump, without any offensive odor. The sealed brood should be consistent, not disturbed, and not isolated. If there is a sudden decrease in the number of bees, the absence of laying, lack of uniformity, multiple eggs in a cell, eggs in the walls of the cells, dark larvae, or offensive odors, or if the opercula are protruding, or if the bees are inactive or nervous, these can be signs of disease or disorder in the bees.

## GLOSSARY

BEEKEEPER: Worker bee that collects nectar from flowers, transports it in its honey sac, and initiates the process of reducing the sugar content with the enzyme invertase.

CAUSAL AGENT: Refers to viruses, bacteria, parasites, fungi, responsible for the onset of a disease.

*Apis mellifera*: The honeybee species *Apis mellifera*.

ROBBER BEES: Bees that steal products from a hive that is not their own.

CONSANGUINEOUS: Refers to the relationship between bees that descend from the same ancestor.

BROOD SEALED: When the larva enters the pupa stage.

SPIRACULUM OF THE PROTORAX: The respiratory opening in the exoskeleton of insects that connects the exterior with the tracheae.

EXOTOXINS: Toxins produced by microorganisms that can be excreted into the surrounding environment.

HEMOLymph: The blood-like fluid in insects.

IICA: Inter-American Institute for Agricultural Cooperation.

LARVA ECLOSIONED: Newly hatched larva.

MYCELIUM: The vegetative part of a fungus, consisting of a network of fine hyphae.

MUTAGEN: A physical or chemical agent that alters or changes the genetic information (usually DNA) of an organism, such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and other organisms.

OIE: World Organisation for Animal Health.

OPERCULATED: Refers to the closure of cells where the queen, bees, and drones will hatch, and the cells containing honey.

PACKAGE: A group of bees consisting of a fertile queen and a group of worker bees, excluding larvae.

PRONAGRO: National Programme for Agricultural and Food Development, SAG.

PYMERURAL: Rural Micro-Enterprise Development Programme in Honduras and Nicaragua.

SYMPTOMS: A useful warning sign of health threats.

## 1.1 Bacterial Diseases of the Brood

## 1.1.1 American Foulbrood

- There is no scientific evidence of the presence of this disease in Honduras.
- It is a disease known in other countries as "brood rot," "viscous disease," and "deformed brood."
- According to the OIE, the causative agent is a bacterium called *Paenibacillus larvae*.

<!-- image -->

A dead larva from an infestation with American Foulbrood, which will subsequently dry out and form a hard scale that is difficult for bees to remove.

<!-- image -->

When the disease develops widely, scales can form within the cells from the dead larvae.

## SYMPTOMS

- Spores enter the hive through: bees that carry them on their bodies, bees from other hives that steal honey from a hive (robbing), through the use of improperly disinfected tools in suspect hives, and feeding with contaminated honey.
- When the spores are inside the hive, they are carried by nurse bees to the cells, where they are deposited along with the food; the larvae ingest the spores, which, once in the intestine, adopt a vegetative form under the conditions of pH and oxygen.
- When the larval stage of the bee is in the pre-pupal and pupal stages, the bacteria that have not been eliminated by feces can migrate to the hemolymph, potentially causing death in the larval stage.
- It affects both worker larvae and drone larvae, and occasionally queen larvae. In a severe infection, the population of worker bees decreases significantly.
- Spore formation in the larva occurs in the presence of oxygen, and they are resistant to desiccation, chemical disinfectants, and high temperatures.
- Spores remain dormant in the combs of colonies that have been affected by the disease, which constitutes a source of contamination.
- It is a non-seasonal disease, caused by stress factors and improper management, including lack of pollen, exposure to harmful substances, imbalances between nurse bees and adult bees, and hive relocation.
- Worker bees fill these cells with food (honey and pollen), and the infection occurs when the nurse bees provide this contaminated food to the larvae.
- A newly hatched larva (eclosion) can be infected by a spore, but after 2 days, the larva's susceptibility is practically zero.

## DIAGNOSIS

- The brood appears spotty and discontinuous in the cells.
- Cells are dark, sunken, greasy, and have some perforations.
- The smell of the diseased comb is foul, similar to wood glue.
- Dead larvae remain in the cells.
- If an opening is made in a cell, a mass of yellowish-creamy and then black material can be seen, depending on the degree of decomposition.
- The scale left by dried larvae is difficult to remove (different from European Foulbrood).
- If the brood dies at the end of the pupal stage, it is common to see the larva's tongue on the scales. The remnants of the tongue are characteristic of American Foulbrood, as are the scales that are strongly adhered and have a characteristic odor (different from European Foulbrood).
- To differentiate from European Foulbrood, consider the age of the affected brood: American Foulbrood affects young brood, while European Foulbrood affects older brood.
- For diagnosis, it is safe to insert a toothpick into an affected cell and gently remove it. If a viscous and gelatinous thread is formed when removed, it will stretch up to 2 cm.
- If there is any doubt, send the comb with dead brood to a laboratory, which should be at least 10 cm long and 8 cm wide.

## PREVENTION AND CONTROL MEASURES

- Identify areas in the country where the disease is endemic or emerging.
- Suppliers of wax and live bees must have a certificate from the competent authority in their country of origin, certified by SENASA.
- Suppress the queen's laying.
- Sterilize the floor, boxes, and frames of the hive, using a powerful disinfectant that can destroy spores (1% hydrogen peroxide, plus 0.5% formic acid, or use a 4% sodium hydroxide solution).
- When cleaning, it is advisable to use gloves and a metal brush to remove debris, then the equipment should be flamed with a torch or by stacking the boxes on a floor and applying alcohol, then lighting them until they are well flamed, then place a lid on the boxes with and the fire will go out. This procedure should be carried out with the boxes inverted so that no spores remain in the rebate where the frames rest.
- To carry out this procedure and not harm the colony housed in this equipment, it is necessary to place an empty hive in the place of the diseased one to provide it with space for the bees to relocate. Provide some frames with wax foundation (WITHOUT BEES, WITHOUT HONEY, WITHOUT LARVAE), to prevent the bees from depositing contaminated honey in the new combs.
- Do not provide artificial feed with contaminated raw materials, and regularly monitor the hive and prevent robbing.
- Burn affected hives, but this is not the most feasible option. Remove and burn all combs from the hive, without discarding them in the apiary to avoid contamination of other hives. Residues that cannot be burned should be buried.

## TREATMENT

- Use antibiotics authorized by SENASA. (In some countries, oxytetracycline is used, but in Honduras, it should only be used if authorized by the competent authority).

## 1.1.2 European Foulbrood

## CAUSAL AGENT

- There is no scientific evidence of its presence in Honduras.
- It is an infectious disease of bee larvae, known as "benign brood," "yellow brood," and "rotten brood" in other countries.
- It is caused by a variety of bacteria, including *Melissococcus pluton*, also known as *Bacillus pluton*, which infects the larvae and weakens them, making them susceptible to other germs.
- It is the second most common bee brood disease and is sometimes called "benign" because it causes less damage than American Foulbrood.
- The bacteria *Melissococcus pluton* can survive in the walls of cells, in bee droppings, or on the floor of the hive for several months.
- The bacterium *Bacillus alvei* is useful for laboratory diagnosis, as it is the only organism that can be distinguished from the spores of American Foulbrood.
- The disease poses no threat to human health.

## SYMPTOMS

- This condition affects larvae, drones, and, occasionally, queen larvae.
- It can occur at any time of year, but is more prevalent during the flowering season.
- Larvae are highly susceptible to infection until they are 48 hours old.
- Colonies that are resistant to the disease are those with good hygienic behavior.
- The mode of transmission is similar to that of American Foulbrood.
- Young larvae ingest *Melissococcus pluton*, either through the food provided by the nurse bees or by being contaminated when present in the cells where the larvae are housed.
- This bacterium reproduces in the digestive tract, using the nutrients provided to the larvae.
- Before the larva is capped, within a period of 3 to 5 days, the bacteria has reproduced to the point where it occupies most of the intestine, passing to the epithelium and causing death.
- Death often occurs when the cells are still open and the larvae are still curled up.
- After 4 days, the larvae dry out on the floor of the cell, leaving a scale that is easily removed by the cleaning bees.
- Stressful conditions and poor management can cause the disease, including a lack of pollen, exposure to harmful substances, imbalances between nurse bees and adult bees, and colony transfers.

## DIAGNOSIS

- The larvae appear stunted, with round or elongated dead larvae, with the uncapped larvae being the most affected. This is a difference from American Foulbrood, which has a sour odor, similar to vinegar or rancid fat.
- As the larvae dry out, they change color and become darker over time. These are found curled up inside the cell, and the tracheal system is easily visible.
- Uncapped larvae have different colors, ranging from cream to white.
- In addition to observation, laboratory tests are recommended. This allows for the identification of the different bacteria present in the affected colony.
- When the infection is mild and the populations are healthy, the colony can recover on its own. However, there is always some loss of population.

## PREVENTION AND CONTROL MEASURES

- The measures to be taken are similar to those recommended for American Foulbrood. It is necessary to clean and disinfect equipment, to avoid feeding artificial feed with contaminated material, to regularly monitor the colony, and to prevent robbing.
- Suppress queen laying.
- Reduce stress on the colony by implementing a feeding program, transferring colonies to appropriate temperatures, and maintaining a balance between young and adult bees.
- Suspect colonies should be quarantined for more than 15 days.
- By implementing apicultural management practices, it is possible to stimulate the cleaning behavior of bees and the selection of genetic lines based on this characteristic.
- Simply remove the affected frames, and in more severe cases, use the package or brushing method.

## TREATMENT

- If antibiotics are used, only those approved by SENASA should be used.
- Apply the treatment according to the manufacturer's instructions.
- The current trend is to avoid the use of antibiotics due to their presence in honey, which can affect people who are allergic to these antibiotics. The best approach is to implement preventive and control measures.
- It is necessary to disinfect and flame the equipment used.

## 1.2 Fungal Diseases of the Brood

## 1.2.1 Chalkbrood or Whitebrood

## CAUSAL AGENT

- This disease is caused by the fungus *Ascosphaera apis* (Maassen claussen).
- This is an infective and contagious disease. Previously, the technical names *Ascosferiosis* or *Ascophaerosis* were used, and in other countries, the common names Chalkbrood, Whitebrood, Tiza, Gis, and Calcárea are used.
- The contaminating form of the fungus is through the formation of spores, which are dark in color.
- The disease can affect larvae of all three bee castes, but it is more common in drone larvae.
- The disease is more common during rainy and cold weather.
- The fungus itself does not cause significant damage, but its proliferation is favored by humidity, low temperatures, poor ventilation within the hive, and its presence in weak colonies, as well as in colonies that have been exposed to antibiotics. Colonies with inbreeding seem to be more susceptible to this disease.

<!-- image -->

<!-- image -->

## SYMPTOMS

- Larvae are most susceptible to the disease between 3 and 4 days of age. The agent enters the hive carried by foraging bees.
- It has also been shown that varroa mites can carry fungal spores. However, the presence of the fungus in the hive does not necessarily mean that the disease will develop. For chalkbrood to manifest, the predisposing factors must be present, primarily humidity and temperature that favor the growth of the fungus (between 20 and 30ºC).
- Spores reach the larva's digestive tract through food, or they adhere to the larva's skin when present in the brood cells, influenced by predisposing factors. The fungal mycelium begins to grow from the spore in the larva's intestine or on its skin. For their development, they require oxygen, so they break the posterior end of the larva. They penetrate the intestinal walls and pass through the larval body tissues, enveloping it completely as if they were roots, and also envelop the larva from the skin, giving it a mummified appearance.
- The larvae can die in an open cell or in a capped cell, as well as on the floor in front of the brood cells after death. They dry out and harden, acquiring the consistency and color of a piece of chalk or tiza.
- The mortality rate of larvae is generally low, but can reach up to 30%.
- The predisposing factors are:
- Humidity: provides a suitable environment
- Poor Ventilation: favors the presence of humidity
- Low Temperatures: facilitate the diffusion of oxygen (aerobic environment), and provide the ideal temperature (20-30 degrees C in the brood nest)
- Weak Colonies: cannot maintain the temperature of the nest above 30 degrees
- Abuse of antibiotics: destroys the normal bacterial flora of the bee's digestive tract
- Lack of protein reserves

## DIAGNOSIS

- When larvae bodies resemble pieces of plaster (chalk), they are called "mummified larvae."
- Occasionally, hardened larvae of a brownish (dark green) color are observed. This occurs when larvae are covered by fungi in their reproductive state. The dark color is due to the color of the spores.
- Most affected larvae are found on the periphery of the hives, with drone larvae being the most affected. Beekeeper can observe the dead larvae.
- When the infection is severe, shaking the hive sounds like a maraca, as the mummies are not firmly attached to the cells and hit the walls of the cells.
- The diagnosis can be made in addition to observation, through laboratory testing.

## PREVENTION AND CONTROL MEASURES

- Keep hives elevated (on stands), at a height of at least 30 cm from the floor. Regulate the space within the hive to avoid moisture condensation and achieve the optimal temperature.
- Do not install hives in flood-prone areas, and ensure good air circulation.
- Keep entrances open to promote proper ventilation.
- Slightly incline the hives towards the entrance (front) to prevent water from entering during rain and to avoid cold winds.
- Protect hives with wooden and sheet metal roofs, using wooden barriers.
- Reinforce or unite weak colonies, provided they are healthy.
- Avoid inbreeding through a good queen breeding program.
- Control predisposing or triggering factors.

- Replace the queen annually and replace queens in affected hives with new queens, and strengthen the colonies with sealed brood.
- Replace old frames in hives, at least every 2 years.
- Do not overuse antibiotics.
- Burn the mummies found in front of the entrances with a smoker. Burn affected frames and remove mummies from the floors and entrances to prevent a new fungal infection cycle.
- Take measures to reduce robbing.
- Avoid bad practices that can infect healthy hives, disinfect the scraper with the internal heat of the smoker, avoid indiscriminate transfer of frames between hives, among others.
- Avoid chilling the brood: Do not place capped brood frames in weak or slightly affected hives, do not remove adult bees from sick and weak colonies, nor give them extra brood to develop. Do not exchange frames between sick and healthy colonies.

## TREATMENT

- Use the product authorized by SENASA and in accordance with the agency's specifications.

## 1.2.1 Chalk Brood

## CAUSAL AGENT

- Caused by the fungus Aspergillus clavus and, in some cases, Aspergillus fumigatus. Larvae die due to intoxication caused by aflatoxins and damage caused by the mycelium.
- Very similar to chalk brood, its technical name is Aspergillosis and in other countries it is known as chalk brood or stone brood, of fungal origin and affects both the brood and the adult bees.
- This is a very common fungus to find in the environment and in the soil.
- Its contagious nature is caused by spores, which are greenish in color.
- It affects bee larvae and, in some cases, adult bees, as well as other insect and mammal species.
- There is a greater dependence on predisposing factors than in the case of chalk brood.
- The disease is more frequent during the rainy season and is favored by poor management practices and robbing.
- The most affected brood is drone brood

<!-- image -->

## SYMPTOMS

- Similar to chalk brood.
- These are also pathogens for other animals, insects and humans, as when the fungus is reproducing, and a person inhales it, it can cause diseases.

## DIAGNOSIS

- Only a few larvae are affected, the mummies have a grayish-green or greenish-yellow color, especially in the head area.
- The mummies are attached to the bottom of the cells, so the worker bees can only extract them in pieces, which they discard in front of the entrance. The remaining debris are covered with propolis.
- To differentiate from chalk brood, the mummies are dark and take on the consistency of stone. When the hive is shaken, there is no sound because the sick larvae are attached to the bottom of the cells.

## PREVENTION AND CONTROL MEASURES

- These are similar to those taken for chalk brood.
- Avoid chilling the brood: Do not place capped brood frames in weak or slightly affected hives, do not remove adult bees from sick and weak colonies, nor give them extra brood to develop.
- Burn affected frames and remove mummies from the floors and entrances to prevent a new fungal infection cycle.
- Orient the entrance to avoid cold winds.
- Do not exchange frames between sick and healthy colonies.

- Replace the queens of affected colonies with new queens.

## 1.3 Viral Diseases of the Brood

## 1.3.1 Brood Cells

## CAUSAL AGENT

- The technical name for the disease is viral brood disease, also known as "cell brood" or "viral brood disease" in other countries.
- It is an infective and contagious viral disease that affects bee larvae.
- Caused by the Morator Aetatulas virus.
- The virus prefers certain tissues of the larvae, such as the cuticle, muscles, fat, and nervous tissue, where it reproduces.
- It can be cultivated in both larval tissues and chicken fibroblasts.
- It typically affects worker larvae and less so adult bees.
- It can occur throughout the year, but is more common before flowering and during the rainy season, especially in weak or stressed colonies.
- The way in which the larvae are affected has not been clearly determined.
- It is believed that the virus enters the larva through contaminated food, such as royal jelly or pollen with nectar, as worker bees store the virus in the hypopharyngeal and salivary glands through contaminated eggs.
- Some studies suggest that the queen lays infected eggs.
- Poor beekeeping practices, as well as robbing, favor the development of the disease.

## SYMPTOMS

- The virus enters the larva through the food provided by nurse bees. Larvae are more susceptible to infection up to 4 days of age. Nurse bees become contaminated when they come into contact with the fluid resulting from the decomposition of a dead larva.
- The virus passes from the digestive tract to the hemolymph and then to its preferred tissues for proliferation.
- The death of the larvae occurs when the cell capping begins or a few days after capping. This happens when the process of skin shedding is interrupted, without the old cuticle detaching from the larva, which then serves as a sac that fills with fluid rich in viral particles.
- The cuticle becomes pigmented and hardened, especially in the head area. When the larva dries, it forms a scale that is easily removed.

## DIAGNOSIS

- Sunken, perforated, and greasy-looking opercula can be observed in affected cells, similar to American foulbrood.
- It is characteristic to observe dead larvae inside a sac, which then takes on the appearance of an inverted cone. This part of the body corresponds to the larva's head, which darkens and hardens and points upwards.
- As the larva dries, it takes on a darker color, until it forms a crust that is easily removable from the lower walls of the cell. The crusts or scales are free of the virus, so they do not constitute a source of contagion.
- When inspecting the brood nest, irregularities in egg laying may be observed. There may be a vinegar-like odor or the absence of odor. Larvae with advanced development in the form of sacs can be observed, with a decolorization from white to transparent.
- The diagnosis can also be made through laboratory testing. The virus cannot be seen in normal microscopes, so special tests must be performed.

## PREVENTION AND CONTROL MEASURES

- Avoid stress in the colonies.
- Use bee breeds that are less susceptible.
- Prevent the introduction of other causal agents, such as the Varroa mite.
- It has also been found that feeding the colony with a concentrated syrup of 2 parts sugar to 1 part water (2:1) helps to control the disease.
- It is ideal to replace the queen.
- Destroy contaminated brood frames if the case is severe.
- A brood nutrition program is recommended.

## TREATMENT

- There is no specific medication for this disease, but it can be prevented with control measures.

## 2.1 Parasitic Diseases of Adult Bees

## 2.1.1 American Foulbrood

## CAUSAL AGENT

- Acarapis woodi. This mite lives in the bee's trachea.
- The technical name for the disease is Acarapisosis of honeybees.
- It affects all three castes of honeybees: queen, worker, and drone. The infestation begins in bees younger than 6 days old, and older bees are immune. The highest levels of infestation occur during the rainy season, cold weather, poor flowering, and during long periods of confinement of the bees.

## SYMPTOMS

- It usually occurs through the dispersal of swarms, the purchase of sick queen bees, or robbing.
- Young bees are infested with the female mite when it comes into physical contact with older, parasitized bees. It moves from the hairs of the thorax of the infested bee to those of the susceptible bee, guided by the air currents produced by the bee's breathing movements. It enters through the spiracle of a trachea in the thorax.
- It feeds on the hemolymph of the bees.
- Bees lose the ability to fly, and the lifespan of an infected bee is 30% shorter than a healthy bee.

## DIAGNOSIS

- The bees appear agitated, exhibit difficulty flying, show restlessness, and frequently groom their abdomen.
- Bees are observed with dislocated wings, flapping them without being able to fly, with an extended abdomen, dead or dying bees near the hive entrances, or bees walking on the ground, some with a black and shiny abdomen, and some losing their stinging instinct.
- In cases of high infestation, bees are unable to fly.
- Beekeepers often do not pay attention to this pest, as it is not visible to the naked eye.
- Low temperatures in the shade, in colonies that have been confined for a long period and have been infested, favor the development of this pest.
- These symptoms are also observed in cases of poisoning, sudden changes in ambient temperature, or consumption of fermented foods, and are also similar to the symptoms of nosemiosis, paralysis, and amebiasis.
- It is recommended to perform laboratory analysis, taking 3 adult bees per apiary annually. The bees are collected from the entrance of each hive and placed in a 70% alcohol jar, along with the owner's information.

## CONTROL AND PREVENTION MEASURES

- Varroa mites have primarily spread through migratory beekeeping. It is important not to mix hives with highly infested bees with those that are not infested.
- If there is a suspicion, refer to a technician for confirmation in a laboratory.
- Other methods include breeding queens from less susceptible lines and keeping young queens in the colonies.
- Avoid robbing and handling errors.

## TREATMENT

- The treatment should be directed at adult Varroa mites, as larvae and immature forms are less affected by acaricides due to their immobility.

## 2.1.2 Varroa Mite

## CAUSAL AGENT

- It is an ectoparasite with the scientific name Varroa destructor. It is a mite similar to a tick, with a reddish color, elliptical and flattened horizontally, belonging to the class Arachnida.
- It affects both the brood and adult bees.
- It is caused by the mobilization of populated hives, bee biological material, by wild swarms, by robbing, or by queens from other locations during commercialization.
- It affects all three bee castes and their offspring, with a preference for drones.

## SYMPTOMS

- The female Varroa mite lays eggs on the adult bee, abandons it, and moves on to the bee brood 5-6 days later, penetrating into the cell and completing its life cycle there.

<!-- image -->

<!-- image -->

## DIAGNOSIS

- In the early stages of infection, no symptoms are observed, so the infection remains hidden from the beekeeper. However, as the infestation increases to significant levels in the hive, general weakening of the colony and associated diseases are observed, until the colony eventually collapses.
- When it manifests, it is because the case is becoming serious.
- The bees appear nervous.
- The mite can be counted visually, and the presence of one or more mites in the bees' bodies can be observed. There is mortality in the brood, and malformations are observed in the wings, legs, abdomen, and thorax.
- Diseased bees are observed with malformations and crawling on the hive floor.
- The size of the bees' bodies decreases.
- Parasitized worker bees are observed grooming their legs in the areas where the parasites are located.
- They rub their bodies against the walls of the cells, inserting their head and thorax.
- If a cell is opened, the mites can be seen.
- Diagnoses should be performed before and after treatment. To perform the analysis, we can use a method that consists of collecting a sample of 200-300 bees from both sides of three frames of brood from each hive in a jar.
- For a correct analysis, it is necessary to sample at least 15-20% of the hives in the apiary.
- Once the sample is obtained, add water, a little detergent, and a spray of alcohol to prevent excessive foaming.
- Then shake well and pour the contents onto a sieve or filter that retains the bees and allows the mites to pass through. We will retain the mites with another finer sieve or simply with a white cloth. Then count the bees and the mites.

<!-- image -->

<!-- image -->

- The number of mites multiplied by 100 and divided by the number of bees will give the percentage of infestation.
- After the treatment, this percentage should not exceed 1%.

## PREVENTION AND CONTROL MEASURES

- It is important to find a treatment that is suitable for each beekeeper, and to aim to maintain the lowest possible presence of this pest, as a tool to minimize the stress of the colonies and the entry of new diseases.
- Several chemical treatments have proven to be effective in controlling Varroa, but they must be supported by the health unit of each country.
- There are some biological methods that aim to reduce the load of the mite within the hives, and that, when combined with chemical methods, can reduce the percentage of Varroa in the hives.
- None of the products used to combat them have proven to be 100% effective, and many chemicals are toxic, carcinogenic, and mutagenic.
- Increase the amount of organic products in the hives, not only reduces the amount of Varroa, but also contributes to reducing the resistance of the mites to chemical products. On the other hand, they do not contaminate the hives.
- It is important that all beekeepers perform treatments at the same time in order to control the sources of re-infestation and increase the effectiveness of the mites.

<!-- image -->

- Maintaining healthy, vigorous colonies with a good population contributes to keeping Varroa populations under control.
- The extent of the disease will depend primarily on the ecological conditions of each region and the mobilization of hives, which generally advance the reproduction of the mite, in tropical climates with abundant bee reproduction, there are periods when the colonies seem to heal themselves. It is important to know that the mite is still present within the brood, reducing the lifespan of the bees and therefore the effective days of foraging. This leads to a lower honey yield and a lower production, although the colonies may appear healthy.

## TREATMENT

- The global trend in the treatment of Varroa is to perform at least one strategic treatment at the end of the year, and then, depending on the climatic and ecological conditions of the populations, one or two additional applications are recommended.
- Any chemical product recommended must be authorized by SENASA.
- The following recommendations are based on four fundamental pillars that are necessary to ensure the success of control strategies:
- a. Rotation of active ingredients (acaricides);
- b. Increased use of organic acaricides;
- c. Assessment of the infestation level before and after applying the treatment.
- d. Coordinated zonal treatments.

## A. ROTATION OF ACTIVE INGREDIENTS

- It is essential to prevent the phenomenon of resistance to acaricides currently used, the mandatory rotation of products each year.
- To achieve this, it is necessary to request from the supplier specifications in addition to the dosage to be used, the forms of use, and the expiration date of the product, the name of the active ingredient with which it was formulated. Remember that all veterinary products are made with excipients, vehicles, and an active ingredient (e.g., amitraz, fluvalinate, flumetrina, oxalic acid, formic acid, etc.)
- Improper use of products and continuous use of the same product can lead to the phenomenon known as chemical resistance.
- Although organic acaricides, by definition, do not cause resistance, it is not advisable to always use the same organic acaricide, in order to avoid resistance mechanisms in Varroa, which can reduce the effectiveness of acaricides.

## B. Organic Acaricides - Avoiding Residues

- To avoid residues in honey, it is essential to know the application time of each of the drugs to be used. All acaricides to be used must be authorized by SENASA.
- There are drugs that must be administered mainly after the harvest, to avoid contaminating the honey. (For example: cimiazol, coumaphos or amitraz)
- In other times, it is advisable to use organic acaricides (oxalic acid, formic acid, thymol) to avoid the risk of leaving residues.
- Keep in mind that acaricides should stop being applied at least eight weeks before the honey harvest. Use the recommended doses and the application method that have been studied.

## C. ASSESSMENT OF INFESTATION LEVEL.

- In general, once treatments have been carried out, many beekeepers wait for the next inspections to see the state of the hives.
- Since Varroa is one of the main causes of hive losses, it is essential to know how the acaricide used worked, as changes in the climate, high infestation levels, nearby apiaries without treatment, active ingredients that are not effective enough, or improper application can lead to a high load of mites in the treated apiary.

## D. COORDINATED ZONAL TREATMENT

- As a fourth pillar, coordinated treatment between beekeepers for simultaneous treatment in all apiaries with the same active ingredient can be considered. This helps to prevent re-infestation through nearby apiaries and eliminates as many mites as possible.
- Contact the Association, Center, Society, or Beekeeping Cooperative in your area to start coordinating treatments in terms of dates and products to be used.
- The number of treatments will vary depending on the cycle of each group of hives and in the areas where they are located.

## OTHER RECOMMENDATIONS TO CONSIDER IN TREATMENT

- Avoid homemade formulations, as they are dangerous and can damage the quality of honey in a country, and in most cases, they are not very effective against mites.
- Another option to keep mite populations low, especially in small operations due to the complexity of the method, is the use of frame-based treatments. Studies confirm the elimination of 60% of Varroa by incorporating and subsequently removing two frames with mites. It is important to pay special attention to hives where this method is used without leaving the frames inside the hive for more than 15 days, as this would result in a very high number of mites, compromising the viability of the colony. Therefore, it is recommended to use this method only in small-scale operations and in easily accessible apiaries.
- Several other actions contribute to reducing the Varroa load, so consult with a specialist and remember that combating Varroa is an indispensable task for every beekeeper and always results in a better harvest.

## 2.1.3 Nosemosis

## CAUSAL AGENT

- Also known as Nosemiasis.
- The causal agent is a specific intracellular parasitic fungus, including two species: Nosema apis and the more recently identified Nosema ceranae.
- This is considered the most widespread disease in the world, and it is found in all countries with beekeeping.
- Highly contagious and easily spread, it is found in all countries with beekeeping.
- The parasite resides in the digestive tract of adult bees, where it multiplies and causes lesions that prevent bees from digesting food and assimilating nutrients properly.
- It is latent throughout the year in hives and appears during the rainy season, cold weather, or windy conditions, or when bees are fed with sugar syrup.
- It is present in all three castes of adult bees.
- Infection is greatly increased by close contact between bees.
- Apiaries located in damp, cold, or shaded areas have a higher risk of infestation.
- Honeycombs and hive materials contaminated with bee excrement are the main source of infection, and this is why the disease persists from one season to the next.
- Contaminated equipment, robbing, and acquiring queens from an infected nursery are causes of transmission and spread of the disease.

## SYMPTOMS

- The agent is always present in hives, multiplying and causing the disease when the hives' defenses are affected by factors that cause stress to the colony, leading to a negative impact on their defense system, facilitating the infection by this parasitosis, as well as the development of other diseases.
- These factors are known as pre-disposing factors. They are events of climatic, health, nutritional, or management origin that cause stress to the hives and lead to a negative impact on their defense system, facilitating the infection by this parasitosis, as well as the development of other diseases.
- The disease can usually be identified when there are bee excrements (similar to diarrhea) in the hives. However, the disease can also be present even when this sign is not observed.
- With the bee excrement, spores are released, which are forms of resistance and dissemination of this parasite.
- When cleaning the hive, young bees ingest these spores and acquire and develop the disease.
- After ingestion, the spores reach the ventriculus or true stomach of the bee, where they attach and enter the epithelial cells.
- If the infection of the cell is not stopped, the parasite multiplies and infects new digestive cells. In this process, the ventriculus suffers irreversible damage, with a decrease in digestive function of the bee. This failure in nutrient assimilation leads to premature death of the bees.

- Once the infection has spread to a large part of the population, the death rate in bees is higher than the reproduction rate that the newly born colony can achieve, resulting in a reduction in the colony's population.
- This decrease in the number of individuals negatively affects both the production of live material and honey. In cases of severe infection, the entire bee population of the colony may be lost, with only the queen and a small group of young bees surviving.
- While the symptoms are similar for both agents (Nosema apis and ceranae), there are certain differences in the disease's dynamics.
- While Nosema ceranae has recently been identified, its distribution is widespread and dates back to several years. This agent has been recognized as more virulent, meaning it has a greater capacity to infect and damage bee colonies. Its multiplication cycle is shorter than that of Nosema apis, resulting in a faster development of the disease. As a result, the spread of the disease among the bees in the colony and the reduction in population size occur in less time. Despite this, the diagnostic, control, and treatment measures are the same.
- While some researchers in the world have associated Nosema cerane with the "colony depopulation syndrome," there are still controversies regarding this. The opinions found among different researchers make it necessary to conduct further research.

## DIAGNOSIS

- The disease does not manifest clinically, and when symptoms appear, the situation is serious.
- The observable signs are not specific and can be confused with varroa mites or other diseases.
- Nosemosis can be suspected if there is a lack of development in the colony's population, excessive food consumption, and repeated replacement of sick queens with bees.
- To avoid confusion, laboratory testing is essential.
- Individually, bees may exhibit an enlarged abdomen, initial irritability, followed by weakness. After lethargy, they lose the ability to fly, the stinger becomes ineffective, they suffer from noticeable paralysis, and eventually die.
- Due to the decrease in digestive and absorption functions, the nurse bees are unable to produce adequate amounts and quality of royal jelly to properly feed the brood and the queen.
- In the queen bees, this leads to atrophy of the ovaries, with a decrease and even interruption of the laying activity, followed by subsequent replacement.
- Due to the damage to the digestive system, the consumption of reserves increases by 20% to 30%.
- There is an imbalance in the bee population.

## PREVENTION AND CONTROL MEASURES

- Annual or biannual replacement of queens.
- Removal of old combs and disinfection of materials.
- A nutrition plan for the period of nectar and pollen scarcity.
- Place apiaries in well-drained areas without excessive shade, avoid puddles.
- Combine weak colonies, ensuring they are not infected.
- Routine sampling of infected colonies, as well as in queen bee breeding areas.
- Routine sampling of colonies during periods when the disease triggers are present, as well as in queen bee breeding areas, laboratory monitoring and pharmacological control, if necessary, should be carried out.

## TREATMENT

- While several compounds have proven to be effective, many are highly toxic to bees or contaminate honey, and should be discarded as therapeutic agents.
- Currently, the only drug that has proven to be effective without contraindications or side effects is fumagillin. This active ingredient is registered in several countries where it is marketed under different trade names. However, in Honduras, it is recommended to consult with SENASA regarding its authorization.
- Pharmacological treatment should ALWAYS be accompanied by the application of GOOD SANITARY MANAGEMENT PRACTICES, which include replacing old combs and disinfecting the inert materials of the colonies.
- For the control of this and all diseases, only products specifically for bees, registered with the health authority (SENASA), should be used.

## 2.1.4 Amebiosis

## CAUSAL AGENT

- It is caused by the protozoan Malpighamoeba mellificae prell.
- Also known as amebiasis, it is a contagious disease.
- It is characterized by the formation of cysts as a state of resistance.
- It is a disease almost exclusive to worker bees; the transmission from the queen and drones is difficult.

## SYMPTOMS

- The parasite lasts between 22 and 24 days.
- The cysts survive for more than 6 months in the feces of bees in the combs.

## DIAGNOSIS

- Laboratory testing is required to establish the presence of the disease.

## PREVENTION AND CONTROL MEASURES

- Sampling of infected colonies.
- Good management practices, cleaning, and disinfection of materials.

## TREATMENT

- Use only those authorized by SENASA

## 2.2 Bacterial Diseases

## 2.2.1 Septicemia

## CAUSAL AGENT

- Its technical name is pseudomoniasis and is caused by the bacterium Pseudomonas apiséptica burnside.
- It exclusively affects adult bee colonies. The way bees become infected is not very clear, but it is related to the wounds caused by Acarapis woodi in the trachea. The mite can be a transmitting vector for the bacteria.

## SYMPTOMS

- From the trachea, the bacteria passes to the hemolymph, where it multiplies, causing septicemia and the death of the insect.
- Young mites that feed on the hemolymph of sick bees can transmit it to healthy bees. Once infected, they die within 1 to 2 days.

## DIAGNOSIS

- Dead bees are observed resting in the brood chambers or in the combs, they are unable to move, they do not feed, and they cannot fly.
- Dead bees easily crumble in the hands when lifted. The color of their hemolymph is observed as milky and turbid. Sometimes, the affected bees emit a rotten odor.
- Laboratory tests are recommended to detect the disease.

## PREVENTION AND CONTROL MEASURES

- Implement good management practices and avoid humidity.

## TREATMENT

- Apply treatment authorized by SENASA

## 2.3 Viral Diseases

## 2.3.1 Paralysis

## CAUSAL AGENT

- Also known as the "black bee syndrome," it is caused by two types of viruses: the acute paralysis virus and the chronic paralysis virus.
- It reproduces in warm weather, especially in hives where the queen is highly inbred.
- It is transmitted through the ingestion of contaminated food.

## SYMPTOMS

- Once the virus is ingested, it passes from the digestive tract to the nervous and fatty tissues. This is particularly concentrated in the head area. Bees die 3 to 7 days after infection.
- The disease may be triggered by high temperatures inside the hive.

## DIAGNOSIS

- Presence of black bees, with no spots on the thorax and trembling.
- Laboratory analysis is required.

## CONTROL AND TREATMENT MEASURES

- A feeding plan can prevent it and changes in the queen. There is no identified medication.

## CHAPTER III Preventive Measures and Disease Sampling

## 3.1 Preventive Measures for Diseases

- a) Never use queens or combs that have been infected with a disease in healthy hives.
- b) Combine weak colonies, ensuring they are not sick.
- c) Provide supplemental feeding (protein and energy) during periods of scarcity.
- d) Replace queens annually, which helps maintain strong colonies, as they respond better to diseases.
- e) When replacing queens, ensure they come from SENASA-certified breeders.
- f) Perform sampling in hives consecutively, monthly is recommended.
- g) Most diseases are transmitted from country to country, due to unscrupulous individuals importing biological material without proper health controls.
- h) Avoid exchanging combs between hives.
- i) Avoid humidity, wind, and high temperatures.

## 3.2 Disease Sampling

Sampling is an activity with a specific purpose: to determine the disease causing losses in the hive.

It is a valuable tool in the control and prevention of diseases, however, it cannot be performed "as a recipe," as each disease has unique characteristics, whether due to the causal agent, the type of sample to be taken (tissue, secretions, feces, etc.), or the characteristics of the diagnostic test.

## 4.1 Pests

## 4.1.1 Wax Moth

## CAUSAL AGENT, SYMPTOMS AND DIAGNOSIS

- o These are insects of the order Lepidoptera. Tropical climates promote the development of the pest.
- o They only attack weak or abandoned colonies, or in stored combs that have not been fumigated.
- o This is due to poor practices by the producer.
- o The moth that causes the most damage is the greater wax moth or Galleria mellonela, and Achroia grisella.
- o The larva leaves tunnels, silk, and fluff as it advances. These are easily detected when examining the combs. They are white-gray in color. After the larva, it weaves a thick, white cocoon in the wood of the boxes, to transform into a chrysalis. The cocoons leave impressions on the wood that damages the equipment.

## CONTROL, PREVENTION AND TREATMENT MEASURES

- Good beekeeping practices to ensure strong colonies.
- Do not store frames, especially those used for brood rearing, and if frames are stored, use frames racks in a shed. The spaces between the racks should be sealed with plastic or masking tape.
- Before re-hanging frames in the hive, leave them exposed to the air for 2 days.
- Expose frames to temperatures of 7 degrees Celsius for 5 hours, or 15 degrees Celsius for 2 hours.
- Frames can be stored in bags in the freezer, and can be retrieved after a considerable time, as long as the bags are not opened.

## CAUSATIVE AGENT, SYMPTOMS AND DIAGNOSIS

## 4.1.5 Aethina Tumida or Carpenter Bee

## CAUSATIVE AGENT, SYMPTOMS AND DIAGNOSIS

- Its scientific name is Aethina tumida, a beetle belonging to the order Coleoptera, family Nitidulidae.
- Also known as the small carpenter bee.
- This beetle does not cause diseases in bees, but its importance lies in the damage it causes to the hive.
- Both the adult and larval stages of this beetle are found in active hives or in stored material, where they feed on honey and pollen. The larvae are elongated, whitish, with small rows of dorsal spines along the posterior, resembling wax moth larvae, but the larvae of the carpenter bee have larger and more pronounced legs.
- Native to southern Africa, where it takes 38-81 days to develop from egg to adult, and may develop five generations per year. It can easily fly and walk on frames. The adults congregate at the back of the hive floor and feed on pollen, also consuming eggs.
- The pupae are white and are found only on the ground outside the hive.

## PREVENTION AND CONTROL MEASURES

- Maintain cleanliness around the honey extraction site and apiaries.
- Do not leave honey-filled combs for too long before extraction.
- The beetle can quickly accumulate in stored honey, especially when it is away from protective bees.
- Maintain meticulous cleanliness around susceptible hives or colonies.
- Do not stack or store infested combs on strong colonies.
- Do not exchange combs or materials between infested colonies.
- Select colonies with a high level of cleanliness, the ability to actively eliminate Aethina tummida larvae is a type of resistance (select and propagate these lines).
- Experiment with cultural control measures. It may be possible to trap larvae while they are trying to reach the ground and pupate. Mobile colonies may be recommended to maintain a low population of the beetle. The beetle's ability to complete its development can vary depending on different soil conditions; beekeepers may find some natural locations that are less prone to beetle infestation. Fire ants can be a beneficial insect in this context if they are found to hunt pupating beetles.
- Bees will not normally clean infested combs with fermented honey. However, bees can complete the job if the beekeeper first removes as much honey as possible with a high-pressure water hose.
- Avoid placing hives in sandy or areas that facilitate A. tummida pupation.
- Design traps to prevent A. tummida larvae from pupating.
- Place corrugated cardboard to detect their presence.
- Soil-based control can significantly help.
- Remove the hive in case of a severe infestation to prevent its spread.

## TREATMENT

- Chemical control involves a significant risk of contaminating the honey, which could render it unsuitable for human consumption. It is recommended to use only products registered with SENASA and to follow the technical specifications for use.

## 4.2 Pesticides

## CAUSAL AGENT

Chemical products, generally agrochemicals.

## DIAGNOSIS

Observation of massive bee death on the ground of the hives and a foul odor.

## CONTROL MEASURES

Prevent by keeping hives away from areas where there are regular applications of agrochemicals, covering the hives at night or moving them when applications are taking place, promoting the use of chemicals that are harmless to bees, such as Bt. (Bacillus turigiensis).
