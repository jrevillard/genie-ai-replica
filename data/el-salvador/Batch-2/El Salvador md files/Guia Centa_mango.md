## INTRODUCTION

The National Center for Agrifultural and Forestry Technology (CENTA) is the institution responsible for executing the national policy for agro-pastoral and forestry technological development.

The Institutional Vision aims to ensure food security for the Salvadoran population, reduce imports and increase exports of agricultural products by introducing technological innovation processes that increase productivity, competitiveness and profitability in various areas. In the fruit sector, significant efforts have been made in mango cultivation, which is considered one of the most profitable, but also challenging, due to its high incidence of pests and diseases. These efforts have resulted in the development of technologies for identifying better genetic materials and managing the crop in general.

The present technical guide is the result of the research, experience and dedication of the CENTA technical and administrative team. It aims to serve as a tool for dissemination and consultation for professionals in agriculture, fruit growers, students and the general public, on the most recommended techniques in the current situation, generated or validated by CENTA for the successful production of this crop, taking into account the possibility of acquisition and adaptation to the climatic, edaphic and cultural conditions in our country.

## General Information

<!-- image -->

Mango is one of the most widely distributed fruits in the country, due to its ability to adapt to different adverse conditions. As a result, most of the national production comes from backyard orchards, but there are also commercial orchards.

The origin of the mango is located in the Asian continent, in the geographical area between the northeastern part of India and the northern part of Burma, very close to the Himalayas.

The cultivation of mango was initially spread throughout Southeast Asia and later to the Malay archipelago; thus, the Portuguese first introduced it to the African continent and subsequently to the coasts of Brazil, from which it spread to the rest of the Americas.

Globally, approximately 16.127 million metric tons are produced annually. This is distributed as follows: Asia produces 79%; America 13%; and Africa, Europe, and Oceania, 8%.

Different varieties of mango have demand in both local and international markets due to their excellent taste and presentation. They are consumed as fresh fruit or processed into products such as jams, preserves, sauces, pickles, salads, ice cream, and canned juices.

Potential planting areas:

Mango prefers a warm and dry climate, which is found from the coastal areas of the east, center, and west of the country, to mid-altitude areas (800 m above sea level) with a temperature of 26-32°C and an average rainfall of 1000-1500 mm.

## Botanical Aspects

## Root

The main root penetrates from 6 to 8 meters, while the superficial roots extend in a radius of up to 10 meters from the trunk. This distribution allows the plant to withstand conditions of low humidity.

## Stem

The branching pattern of the tree depends on whether it is propagated by seed or grafting, and on the type of pruning applied.

In trees propagated by seed, the branching is abundant, and the height can reach over 40 meters.

In grafted and pruned trees, the branching is less pronounced, leading to fewer flowering branches and a more symmetrical shape, with a more or less spherical crown.

## Leaves

The leaves appear at the ends of the branches. Their spacing is very irregular and is determined by the growth periods; at the beginning of these periods, the leaves appear very close together, and at the end, they are more spaced. The petioles, which are swollen at the base, have a channel on the upper side and measure between 5 and 25 mm in length. The lamina is generally oblong or lanceolate, with a sharp base and apex, rarely elliptic. Its size varies from 5 to 35 cm in length and 2 to 10 cm in width; the edges are usually wavy. The central vein and the 15 to 30 lateral veins are very prominent, and the petiole is hard and glossy, dark green in color, while the underside is greenish yellow.

## Flower

The inflorescence is a panicle that usually appears at the end of a branch. In some cases, lateral inflorescences may also appear. A mango tree has a large number of flowering branches, and each of these branches bears thousands of flowers. The abscission occurs at night or in the early morning. The petals open shortly after the perianth and turn blue due to the pollen; they require bright and warm conditions to open. The stigma can be receptive even before the flower opens, and it continues to do so for two days. Pollination occurs exclusively by insects, which are attracted by the nectar secreted by the disc, and they transfer the pollen grains to other flowers. Self-incompatibility is predominant, but self-compatible varieties are known.

## Fruit

The shape, size, and color of the fruit vary greatly depending on the variety. The basic color is yellow in ripe fruit, which is uniform or has areas of red or green.

## Seed

Each mango fruit contains a single seed, which is oval or oblong in shape, and is surrounded by a fibrous endocarp when ripe; the testa is thin and permeable. There are two types of seed: monoembryonic seeds, which contain a single zygotic embryo, and polyembryonic seeds, which contain several embryos, usually only one is zygotic and the others are derived from the nucellar tissue. In Mangifera indica, there are two distinct types that can be distinguished by their origin:

- a) Subtropical group: with monoembryonic seeds (Indian type)
- b) Tropical group: with polyembryonic seeds (Southeast Asia)

## Propagation

## Preparing Seedbeds for Planting

It is recommended to use seeds from polyembryonic trees (criollo varieties) for planting, as they will produce uniform plants with greater development.

In addition, these trees should be vigorous, productive, adapted to the ecological conditions of the region, and have good phytosanitary conditions.

The preparation of the seedling bed for mango is similar to that of other fruit trees. The seed should be sown 8 days after harvesting the fruit, as it loses its germination power. The seeds should come from ripe, well-developed, and healthy fruits. To accelerate germination and facilitate the emergence of straight seedlings, it is recommended to remove the husk (endocarp) or, in its place, to cut off part of the seed's dorsal side. These should be disinfected and sown with the dorsal side facing up and slightly exposed on the soil surface (1/3 of the seed). The spacing between seeds is 0.05 m and between rows is 0.20 m. The depth of sowing is 0.05 m. The seedlings are ready for transplanting into polyethylene bags in 3-5 weeks, as soon as the differentiation of the first pair of leaves begins.

From polyembryonic seeds, several seedlings emerge during germination. When transplanting, when the seedlings are about 10 cm tall, the best ones are selected, and the weaker ones are discarded. When sowing, care should be taken not to damage the roots, and the seed should be sown with its seedling at the same level as it was in the seedbed.

## Nurseries

The nursery can be kept in the ground with 50% of the soil level below or at ground level, depending on the irrigation system to be implemented. They are planted in blocks of two to four stories, with 0.75 meters between each block to facilitate grafting and cultural operations. Although the nursery can be grown in full sun, seedlings develop better under uniform artificial shade, between 40-60% at the time of transplant. If weak and discolored plants are observed, apply foliar fertilizer with minor elements. After one month of transplant, when the plant has recovered, it can be exposed to full sun. Fertilization in the nursery is generally done monthly, alternating 10 g of Urea or 20 g of ammonium sulfate and 10 g of the 20-20-0 formula.

## Method for Grafting

The scions are ready for grafting when they have a diameter of 6-10 mm and a height of 0.30 m. It is preferable for the scions to have a tender stem, in this case they acquire a reddish color, both the stem and the terminal leaves.

The most commonly used grafting method is side grafting. This consists of making two oblique cuts on opposite sides of the stake, resembling a wedge. The first cut should be between six and eight centimeters long, while the second should be 0.75 to 1.5 centimeters.

The cut on the scion is made between 0.20 and 0.25 meters above the ground level, and for this, a flat area is selected. The cut is made tangentially inwards and downwards through the wood in a single movement of the knife. The length of the cut should be slightly longer (6.5 to 8.5 cm) than that made on the stake, leaving a small notch at the base of said cut where the stake wedge will be placed, so that there is a union in the cambium of

<!-- image -->

the scion and the stake; immediately tie with plastic strips, starting from the bottom. Once the graft is established, it is recommended to cut the apex of the scion between 0.10-0.20 meters above the union to eliminate apical dominance and accelerate the growth of the graft. Subsequently, the rest of the scion is removed when the graft is completely sprouted.

The best time to graft is from February to April, when a take rate of 90-95% is achieved. When grafts are made during the rainy season, it is important to place plastic caps to prevent water from entering and its subsequent death due to fungal diseases.

The stakes to be grafted must come from healthy, vigorous trees with good production and from selected varieties. The stakes should have between three and five lateral buds. In addition, if the nursery is grown in full sun, provide the saplings with a partial and uniform shade of around 50 percent (or use a shade cloth). When the grafts reach a height of around 40 cm, expose them to the sun for 4 weeks to harden them before planting in the field.

## Preparation and Storage of Stakes

As soon as the desired variety is selected, the material for grafting is prepared; for this, branches with more than three months of age, with a diameter of 0.5 centimeters, with dark green leaves and with a terminal bud are used. The branch should be between 10 and 20 centimeters long; subsequently, all the leaves of the selected branch are removed, leaving at least one centimeter of stem, and it is left on the tree for eight to fourteen days.

A defoliated stake is more efficient, as defoliation causes an immediate increase in the sugar content in the phloem of the bud, which, in turn, increases the osmotic pressure, causing solutes to move towards the apex of the bud, and increases the meristematic activity that helps in the formation of the callus, due to the stimulation of cambial division; in this way, a better union of the graft is favored.

The selection of the stakes will depend on the thickness of the rootstock; furthermore, the stakes must be removed from the tree with the buds turgid and dormant. It is recommended to prepare a greater number of stakes than the ones that will be needed.

## Establishment of the Plantation

After cleaning the land, the layout and staking are carried out according to the most appropriate planting system; then, the soil is leveled. The planting holes, depending on the soil texture, the following dimensions are recommended, which can vary from 0.40 x 0.40 x 0.40 meters to 0.60 x 0.60 x 0.60 meters; in excessively poor soils, the dimensions are recommended to be larger. The planting hole should be filled with a mixture of soil and organic matter.

## Planting Time

The mango plant should be transplanted with care to avoid damaging the root; the practices of extraction, transfer and transplant should be carried out with caution, especially when removing the plastic bag, ensuring that the root ball is not damaged. The planting should be carried out when the graft is between four and six months old, preferably at the beginning of the rains or at any time of the year if there is easy access to irrigation.

## Planting Spacing

The conditions of the soil and the variety to be used determine the spacing between plants, in addition, other factors such as soil fertility, climate and water availability must be considered. Modern fruit cultivation tends towards solutions that favor the early fruit production, by using plants with enanizing characteristics, in addition to increasing the planting density, it facilitates the carrying out of the different cultivation practices. Medium-sized varieties can be established from 5 x 5 meters, while large-sized varieties can be established from 7 x 7 m, in rows, with a population of 400 and 204 plants/ha, respectively. Pruning is recommended at least every two years, when the branches of the plantation begin to cross, which can occur after eight years of establishment.

## Planting Systems

The planting system refers to the spatial distribution of the plants in the final location, for which it is necessary to consider the conditions of the land (topography, texture, fertility, depth, rockiness, etc), variety, pattern and climate. The most recommended systems are triangular, in rows and rectangular.

It is possible to cultivate other fruit species during the mango growth period, such as pineapple, guava and papaya or annual crops such as corn, beans, cassava and vegetables.

## Pruning

Young trees develop a strong framework and a well-formed crown, requiring little pruning. It is recommended to carry out formative pruning during the first three years, removing the buds or branches that grow inwards (chupones).

The pruning that is carried out regularly is the maintenance pruning, limiting it to the removal of attacked branches by pests.

It is important to obtain a low crown that facilitates the cultural operations and the harvest.

## Fertilization

The mango can adapt well to various edaphic and fertilization conditions, however, although it is tolerant to soils with low fertility, its production levels increase significantly in fertile soils.

In studies carried out in other countries, analyzing fruits in a large number of varieties, it is observed that nitrogen and potassium are the nutrients extracted in greater quantities in a harvest, with sulfur having a similar content to calcium, which occupies the third place, followed by magnesium and phosphorus.

It is recommended to carry out a soil analysis to design the fertilization plan.

## Timing

The first flowering is typically in May, at the beginning of the rainy season, and the second flowering in June.

## Floral Induction

Various methods can be used for floral induction in mangoes, but the most commonly used method has been spraying foliage with chemicals such as: Ethrel, ethylene, auxins, naphthaleneacetic acid, maleic hydrazide, gibberellic acid, potassium nitrate, and urea. The best results are obtained with sprays of salts, and to a lesser extent, with triazole growth regulators (Paclobutrazole-uniconeazol).

In this country, promising results have been achieved with potassium nitrate sprays at 2 and 4% in three applications, with intervals of one week. This has allowed for an anticipated harvest of 30-60 days earlier.

## Factors Affecting Flowering

Growth pattern: Generally, mangoes grow in spurts, with several occurring throughout the year. This characteristic varies depending on the variety and environmental factors. Shoots of different ages have the ability to produce flowers. The ability to flower can be influenced by the previous year's harvest. Nitrogen, carbohydrate, and other substance reserves in the shoots on the tree:

Most varieties have high levels of stored starch, total carbohydrates, and a high carbohydrate/nitrogen (C/N) ratio in the shoots, which promotes flowering. The C/N ratio can be increased by pruning (accumulating carbohydrates from photosynthesis, by interrupting their passage from the leaf apex to the roots). These wounds also increase the production of ethylene and other hormonal factors associated with flowering.

Harvest: High yields in one year can reduce flowering in the following year in varieties with alternating harvests.

Hormonal effect products: It has been proven that substances promoting flowering are produced in the leaves. Auxins have a direct relationship with flowering, while high levels of gibberellins in shoots are associated with low flowering.

Environmental factors: It is known that reduced soil moisture and low temperatures induce flowering in mangoes. Very high temperatures and excessive drought reduce flowering and production. Similarly, excessive humidity can prevent flowering, as the terminal buds of the tree become vegetatively active.

## PRACTICES THAT FAVOR FLOWERING

## Flower and Fruit Thinning

The effect of thinning depends on the variety, but in general, it is not very significant. The best results are achieved by thinning fruits when they are only the size of a small bean. In some countries, thinning flowers with Ethephon (500 ppm) and Cycloheximide has given good results.

Pruning: There is a direct relationship between the ethylene levels in shoots and flowering, and different pruning practices.

## Pest Control

## WEED CONTROL

On flat terrain, weed control can be done with a cultivator, and a band of soil should be cleared at the base of the tree. Weed control is important in the first three years of cultivation, after which the trees have developed enough foliage to reduce weed incidence around them.

On uneven terrain, it is done manually. Chemical control should be carried out with post-emergence herbicides, preferably based on glyphosate, while protecting the mango seedlings.

## INSECTS

## FRUIT FLIES

(Ceratitis capitata and Anastrepha spp.)

These flies are the most important pest of the crop, attacking other fruits and having an endemic character. The female lays eggs on the ripening fruit, from which larvae emerge, feeding on the pulp, and then move to the soil when the fruit falls, where they continue to develop (pupa), before emerging as adults and repeating the cycle. For control, the integrated pest management (IPM) approach is used. This starts with collecting fallen fruit, which is buried to a depth of 50 centimeters and treated with an insecticide in granules or powder; it is recommended to keep the soil clean of weeds and apply an insecticide once a year. When the fruits are young, Malathion 57 CE is applied.

Another way to control is to use traps with ripe fruit as bait. The traps can be made manually, with plastic containers from soft drinks, to which windows are made so that the insect can enter; in the bottom, the bait is applied, which can be fruit juice, hydrolyzed protein, etc.

## DISEASES

Among the most important diseases affecting mango cultivation are: anthracnose (Colletotrichum gloesporioides), rots (Elsinoe mangiferae), powdery mildew (Oidium mangiferae) and trunk cancer (Ceratocystis fimbriata). The best way to control is through good agronomic management at planting, with sanitary pruning and pruning that facilitate air circulation and sunlight. If necessary, fungicides such as Mancoceb and Benomil can be applied to protect before flowering, and after fruit set. Depending on the climate conditions, two additional applications should be made.

## ANTHRACNOSIS

(Colletotrichum gloesporioides Penz)

This disease is the most important in the crop. As in other fruit crops, it affects leaves, shoots, inflorescences and fruits. On leaves, it causes irregular brown spots of variable size. In young leaves, it also causes deformities or rupture of the damaged part of the leaf, which becomes brittle.

When attacking the first leaves (not developed), the infection spreads to the green tissue of the shoot and causes downward death.

In other cases, it attacks the middle part of the shoot, causing bending and ultimately death.

In the inflorescence, it causes hyperplasia (excessive growth in the division phase), causing small dark spots on the flowers and branches, causing the fall of flowers or partial death of the inflorescence.

Fruits are attacked at any stage. When newly formed, they cause spots near the peduncle or on the surface, causing premature fall. In more developed fruits, they cause circular and dark spots that crack in the center. Late attacks generally do not manifest, remaining latent until the ripening process or in storage. In this state, a series of small dark spots appear, slightly depressed, which can merge, necrosing large areas. These lesions are generally superficial, as the peel can be lifted to reveal healthy pulp.

## Control of Anthracnose

DACONIL, 1 kg per hectare, at the beginning of flowering and when the fruit is growing. PROCHLORAZ 45 EC, 50-100 cc in 100 liters of water. Intervals of 15 days.

AZOXYSTROBIN. Dosage of 200 grams diluted in 100 lts of water per hectare at intervals of 10 days.

The application of fungicides should be alternated, never using the same fungicide consecutively.

## Rust

(Elsinoe mangifera)

The damage manifests in the fruits with the presence of spots that are spreading, and cracks are beginning to appear on the lesions with growth.

<!-- image -->

The growth is spongy, rough to the touch, and has a grayish-brown color. In some cases, cuts larger than the lesions appear, exposing the pulp, and other secondary infections begin. On leaves, there are small, angular, grayish-green lesions, and in extreme cases, leaf deformation.

Control: Remove damaged fruits and apply copper hydroxide + Mancozeb 1.5 kg/ha of each.

## Powdery Mildew

(Oidium mangiferae)

This disease is characterized by a white powdery coating on the inflorescence, newly formed fruits, and young leaves, causing deformation in fruits and leaves, which then fall. The fungus attacks during dry periods and with cloud cover of 3 days or more.

Control: Before the first flowers appear, apply Elosal 72 SC 0.75 - 10 l/ha (sprayable powder) and alternate with Benlate at a rate of 0.7 1 kg/ha.

## Trunk Canker

(Ceratocystis fimbriata)

This affects branches that have gummy exudates (resin) and their leaves wither with burns at the tips or edges. Generally, these lesions coincide with old wounds where the wood was exposed; in other regions, it is known as the "machete disease" because this tool is used to propagate the fungus. Insects that carry the fungus also contribute to the damage.

## Control

It is recommended to remove and burn damaged trees, disinfect tools, avoid dense plantings, and avoid wounds or treat them with a copper-based fungicide.

## Irrigation

Water is essential for young, newly established trees, before they begin to produce. After that, irrigation is essential for fruit growth. In producing trees, strong irrigation should be suspended two months before flowering and continued after fruit set until maturity.

## Systems

Drip and micro-sprinkler irrigation systems are recommended for mechanized fruit orchards for efficient water use, enabling fertigation, and because they allow fertilization with water, which means saving labor. Gravity irrigation will depend on the conditions and facilities of the land.

## Harvesting

The mango fruit requires 105 to 140 days to reach physiological maturity from flowering. In our region, the local varieties are precocious and produce from December to April, and the improved varieties produce from April and August. The harvested fruit is perishable, and this process accelerates with poor fruit handling.

## Harvesting index

The cutting at the right time is of great importance, as it affects the quality of the fruit and the post-harvest life. The harvest should be carried out before it reaches the climacteric point, as fruits that ripen on the tree have a lower aroma and are less resistant to transport. There are some visual characteristics that can be used to determine the cutting point in some varieties and types:

* Beginning of yellow coloration at the lower end of the fruit.
* Formation of a cavity at the base of the peduncle.

In most mango varieties, when they reach physiological maturity, the pulp turns yellow, and it can be easily determined by slicing a few fruits, from which you can determine if they are ready to be harvested.

## Collection systems

Collection should be carried out with a ladder type A and a pole with a blade at the end to cut the fruit stem. It is advisable to cut the stems attached to the fruit, not to pile the fruits, to wash them, to dry them and to arrange them carefully in wooden, plastic or cardboard boxes previously ventilated. For varieties susceptible to anthracnose, apply Benomil 15 days before harvesting.

## Post-harvest handling

The losses in post-harvest mango are significant, mainly due to the microbial decomposition of fungal and bacterial origin.

The most destructive disease of mangoes is anthracnose, Colletotrichum gloeosporioides penz, which appears as dark brown or black spots on the fruits.

There are other fungi that cause rot or develop black spots on the mango's surface, and some bacteria also cause necrotic spots on the fruit.

The deterioration of the fruit's quality due to fungal attacks can be prevented or controlled under certain conditions, through the application of fungicides before or after harvest. However, recently, it has been found that treating the fruit with hot water after harvest is very effective for controlling anthracnose, as well as other fungal diseases.

## Industrialization

In India, Brazil, the United States, England, and Pakistan, various studies have been conducted on mango industrialization. In some places, mango industrialization is limited to producing slices in syrup, nectars, and juices. Additionally, it is used to produce jams, jellies, juices, frozen cubes, canned goods, or concentrated products.

It is possible to obtain products such as dehydrated mango slices or mango rolls, which have already been achieved at the experimental level. These products have acceptable organoleptic characteristics and have yielded an average of 18% by weight using varieties such as Kent, Keitt, and Manila. In addition, mango rolls can be stored for up to a year.

In other countries, high-quality wines have been produced. However, not all varieties are suitable for this purpose, so it is necessary to evaluate many of these to produce wines on a large scale.

The different mango varieties have industrial demands, so the quality of the raw material for a specific process should be as follows:

For canning, the slices should have a firm and juicy texture, a pleasant taste, and a natural, appealing smell. In addition, the seed should be small, and the fruit should ripen uniformly, remaining fresh in the store for a long time and retaining its texture when processed.

Soft, fibrous, and sour mangoes are considered of inferior quality for canning, but they can be used in pickles and in cooking. The fruit for juice or nectar production should have a good pulp consistency, taste, and smell, and some varieties, although fibrous and soft, have sweet juice and can be used for this purpose.

<!-- image -->

Immature but fully developed mangoes can be used to make pickles.

Juicy varieties are suitable for making dehydrated slices. For freezing, it is necessary to use ripe and firm fruits, as green fruits can give a bitter or sour taste.

## Commercialization

The domestic market absorbs almost 100% of the production as fresh fruit, as in our country, commercialization is done with fresh fruit, as there are no suitable industrial culture and processes. The fruit is commercialized in an unripe, seasoned, and ripe state, reaching prices at the beginning of the harvest of up to $0.40 per unit, starting in December.

Criollo mangoes are the most abundant, although improved varieties are also commercialized. These varieties remain on the market for a longer period due to their longer harvest period. There is potential in our country for establishing commercial plantations for export, as the potential of the international market is practically unlimited, as most producing countries have a local consumption market and, on the other hand, a small percentage of the world's production enters the international market. Therefore, knowledge of international markets is of great importance for planning the establishment of orchards, with these purposes in mind.
