## INTRODUCTION

Historically, organic beekeeping has its roots in naturalists who approached nature in a productive and philosophical way. Justus von Liebig (1803-1873), considered the inventor of organic beekeeping, wrote that "the rational farmer must examine whether his methods are in harmony with certain natural truths and laws, or whether they, on the contrary, damage them; he must constantly keep in mind that the true purpose of agricultural practice should not be solely focused on obtaining the highest yields, but also on making those high yields sustainable."

In recent decades, in contrast to the development of industrial beekeeping, organic beekeeping has focused, in part, on ensuring the production of healthy food for consumers. In parallel, it has been integrated into a broader framework, which is also the framework of agroecology, incorporating ideas about a more environmentally and socially sensitive approach to beekeeping, focused not only on production but also on the environmental and social sustainability of the production system.

In beekeeping, these concepts take on special significance, considering the particular relationship that bees have with the environment. Honey production depends on a healthy environment for bees and abundant flowering. At the same time, through honey production, bees contribute to the valorisation of the environment and biodiversity, and become a support for social development.

Organic beekeeping, on its own scale, is therefore a particularly well-suited tool for tackling two major problems that humanity seeks to reduce: the loss of biodiversity worldwide and social and economic poverty.

This context motivated the holding of the Second World Conference on Organic Beekeeping, in San Cristóbal de las Casas, from March 19 to 25, 2012.

In the same context, the present "Manual of Organic Beekeeping" aims to bring together important elements for beekeepers who want to embark on the path of organic production. In the first part, it gathers comments on the technical requirements of organic beekeeping. In the second part, it presents the Internal Control System for beekeeping organizations. Finally, it presents some selected texts from the lectures given at the conference.

## PART 1: ORGANIC PRODUCTION STANDARDS

This first part discusses the main technical requirements for organic honey production. It refers to the European Union's regulation for organic production (Annex 1), which is based on several previous private regulations, to essentially have the same criteria. The following combines new and previously cited texts.

## Apiary Location and Forage Areas

The most difficult aspect to understand and apply in organic beekeeping is regarding the forage area near the apiaries. The European regulation states that "the location of the apiaries must be chosen in such a way that, within a radius of 3 kilometers, the sources of nectar or pollen are fundamentally ecological crops, wild vegetation, or crops treated with methods with a low environmental impact."

The main problem in qualifying the forage area in organic beekeeping is the particular nature of this activity, where bees feed on a very large surface, almost completely outside the control of beekeepers. Data show that bees forage within a radius of 1 km when there is an abundance of nectar, and up to 3 km in scarcity, i.e., a surface area of 300 to 2800 hectares (see Fig. 1). However, a recent study suggests that they can forage up to 12 km. The standards that define organic beekeeping are based on a recommendation, which states that this forage area should preferably contain natural vegetation or organic crops. But this does not allow for a strict definition, since, for example, if all sources of contamination, particularly agricultural origin, are rejected, the possibility of producing organic honey in regions with intensive agriculture is eliminated. This means that there must necessarily be some tolerance for areas where pesticides are used or there is other type of contamination within the forage area. The difficulty for the inspector and the technicians of the organizations is therefore to understand the agriculture of the area being evaluated and the ecology of the bees, i.e., their interest in foraging in these areas where pesticides have been used.

Experienced beekeepers argue that "we don't certify the environment, but we seek quality." The central idea is that it is not possible to consider a corn or coffee producer who has complete control over what is applied in their plot, and a honey producer, who does not have this control, in the same way. This calls for strictness in what is under the control of the beekeeper (particularly in the application of treatments against bee diseases), i.e., in their practices. For everything else, i.e., what is done around the apiary, the principle is to limit oneself to defining whether there are practices that put the quality of the honey at risk. These include, among others, the application of compounds on crops when the bees visit them.

This interpretation is compatible with the ethics of the organic movement, following the same reflection at two levels. Therefore, the producer is required to be consistent between their activities and opt for a fully organic system. And, since it is not in their power, and to avoid being unable to produce organic honey with a strict approach, the requirement is to ensure that undesirable practices in an organic practice do not affect the quality. This allows recommending that apiaries be located:

- In areas with sufficient natural nectar and pollen sources, as well as clean water sources for the dry season.
- Away from roads and dirt roads to avoid dust contamination and to prevent bees from attacking people or animals.
- As far as possible from cities or settlements.
- Away from dumps or contaminated areas or wastewater treatment plants.
- At a good distance from crops where pesticides and herbicides are used, in regions with intensive agriculture.
- Away from high-voltage power lines.
- Away from vandalism and theft, floods and landslides.

These recommendations are made qualitatively, without specifying distances, because it will be up to the beekeepers or inspectors to assess the risks in the field. The same dump or plot can present risks in conditions of nectar scarcity, but no risk in other conditions, which can only be determined with experience and practice.

Regarding the use of pesticides, including herbicides, it is possible to distinguish crops according to the risk they pose to organic beekeeping. There are crops that pose a risk to organic beekeeping because pesticides are applied in an area attractive to bees at the time of application. Other crops pose no risk or low risk, because pesticides are not applied, or they are applied at a time when bees do not visit them.

To illustrate this classification and to use it in practice, we take the case of organic beekeeping in the southeastern states of Mexico. It is mainly practiced in coffee-growing areas, where the crops susceptible to receiving pesticides are mainly coffee and corn.

1. In both cases, coffee or corn, herbicides are used, mainly during the rainy season, when there are no flowers that the bees can visit.
2. The period of application is in a time when there are no flowers that the bees can visit.
3. Other pesticides may be applied, particularly for certain pests of corn, but due to their application method (basically in the soil), it is also unlikely that bees will come into contact with these products.

## Bee Origin

In organic beekeeping, it is expected to work with local bees, or at least with bees adapted to the local environment.

Ideally, the bees should come from swarms that occupy the hives themselves. However, the beekeeper can create suitable conditions by placing a small hive in a suitable location. All that is needed is an empty hive, aged 3-6 years. Old hives attract swarms due to their smell. It is also permitted for the beekeeper to create swarms artificially at the appropriate time (when the hives are ready for swarming).

In addition, for the annual renewal of the hives and to promote genetic improvement, regulations require that up to 10% of the bees or swarms in organic apiaries come from outside the year.

## Bee Selection

Most beekeepers are looking for a bee that is very productive, with minimal investment, very docile, does not swarm, resistant to diseases, etc. Unfortunately, such a bee does not exist. The current problems in beekeeping, such as the Africanized bee, the presence of Varroa, and other diseases, as well as the instability of the international honey market and the deterioration of the environment, have led beekeepers to focus more on obtaining bees with high performance, low defensive characteristics, and resistance to the main diseases, although other traits that may be useful to some beekeepers, such as low tendency to swarm, compact brood, and bee color, are also desirable.

It is possible to import queens from other regions or even from other countries. However, it is important to remember that bringing bees from other origins always carries risks. In particular, these queens may not perform well where they are introduced. In addition, any introduction carries the risk of introducing new diseases that are not present in the area.

<!-- image -->

For all these reasons, the most sustainable and recommended approach is to select the best hives in the production area and breed queens from them. We propose selecting based on the following criteria:

- Honey yield per hive per year, in number of frames or kilograms.
- The docility of the bees. This is a difficult trait to quantify numerically, so it is proposed to be evaluated by observing the bees' reaction when they are checked daily, giving a rating on a scale of 1 to 5, as follows: very docile, docile, regular, or slightly docile, defensive, and very defensive.

- Resistance to pests and diseases. It is desirable that the bees have fewer health problems, so that they do not depend on medications, and because healthy colonies produce more. Resistance to diseases can be assessed by simply observing that the colonies do not show signs of disease for a long time. Another method that is currently widely used is to explore their hygienic behavior, as this behavior is directly related to the health of the colonies and can be measured by different tests.

Other characteristics can also be evaluated. The tendency to swarm, which is favorable in European bees due to their ability to form new colonies, is easily excessive in Africanized bees, due to their tropical habits. When this is the case, the tendency to swarm can be measured by marking the queens and selecting lines with an appropriate level of swarming.

It is important to mention that the more traits that are selected, the more complicated the selection becomes, so it is recommended that any beekeeper who wants to do so, only work on the traits that are most suitable for them.

## Bee Feeding

In organic beekeeping, the main source of food should be the bees' own honey. Therefore, at least one super of honey should be left in each hive for the last harvest, so that during the rainy season, a critical period due to the scarcity of nectar and pollen, the bees have food.

Artificial feeding of the hives will only be allowed when the survival of the colony is threatened due to extreme weather conditions. In these cases, it is permitted to feed the colony with organic honey, preferably from the same beekeeper. This requires that the beekeeper has sufficient reserves of honey and pollen that can be used during periods of food scarcity. If the beekeeper does not have reserves of honey and/or pollen from the same beekeeper, it is possible to feed with organic honey or sugar, provided that the operation is documented.

Feeding bees with non-organic products is strictly prohibited. The use of honey, pollen, or other products that have been fermented is also not allowed, as this compromises the health of the bees and the safety of the products produced by the hive. Providing inputs that come from sick colonies is also prohibited to prevent the spread of diseases. If any of these points are not followed, the honey produced cannot be sold as organic honey.

Feeding is not allowed during the 2 weeks leading up to the nectar flow period.

## Disease Prevention

In organic beekeeping, the main focus is on preventing bee diseases. The selection of bees described above is an important part of this prevention. Here are some other measures.

Above all, good beekeeping practices (whether organic or not) mean applying consistent and effective preventative measures, rather than treating the symptoms, as these indicate the bees' health problems. This approach is based on reflecting the natural behavior of bees, as they were able to overcome pests and threats without human intervention for millions of years. The beekeeper can be seen as the worst enemy of the colony, as they damage a very balanced structure every time they open the hive, not only by stealing honey, pollen, propolis, but also for inspection, propagation, migration, feeding, and applying medications.

This is compounded by additional stressors, such as the use of pesticides, the reduction of pollen diversity, pests, predators, monocultures with genetically modified crops, exposure to electromagnetic radiation, or climate change, which can cause premature flowering and overload the bees' defense mechanisms, leading to their death.

Therefore, today, the most urgent task for a beekeeper is to restore and support the internal balance of the colony, and to minimize stress for the bees as much as possible. While organic regulations address critical points such as the location of the apiary, the origin of the bees, the quality of the materials used, tools, and equipment, the quality of the wax, feeding, treatments, and other operational measures, the beekeeper must go beyond these purely technical instructions.

Reducing stress, in this sense, means:

- Selecting bee strains adapted to the local environment.
- Regularly replacing the queen bee, preventing the loss of pheromone signals after 2 years.

## Disease Control

All of the above measures are within the reach of the beekeeper to strengthen the health of the bees and allow them to mobilize their energy to cope with stress. However, even with these measures, diseases and pests can still occur in the hives. In these cases, the state of the art in good beekeeping practices is divided into two parts.

The main disease is the Varroa mite. This not only causes physical damage to individual larvae, but also by infecting them with a list of viruses that is still incomplete. The problem is still waiting for a truly acceptable solution. The level of Varroa infestation is determined using special scales, and if it exceeds a certain level, treatment is necessary.

Applying formic acid, although effective, is extremely stressful for the bees. Furthermore, there is a small difference between the effective dose against Varroa and the dose that is harmful to bees, and there are no recommendations for application that are suitable for all individual circumstances. Varroa.

Oxalic acid requires precautions for the user, but it is one of the compounds that gives the best results.

An advantage of these substances is that they are found in bee products naturally, and are supposed to be harmless in food products. However, their use is restricted to periods without nectar flow, to prevent the formation of levels higher than those found naturally. The essential oils or their compounds, such as thymol, can be effective, but the wax, being a fat, accumulates the residues of the agent, and its taste may be found in the honey. Therefore, its use should be avoided in the 6 weeks before the harvest. Some organizations such as BioSuisse and Demeter do not allow the use of such compounds, so it is necessary to verify the authorizations before using them.

There is a treatment against Varroa using heat, in which larvae are treated with heat. The success of thermal treatment is due to the fact that bee larvae are more resistant to heat than Varroa. Thermal treatment can be carried out at any time during the brood period. Varroa.

Finally, traps can be used to collect Varroa, by leaving the bees to build cells for drone larvae, which is very attractive to Varroa. If these cells are removed, the Varroa population will be controlled, and new wax will be obtained for melting and stamping. Varroa.

The other important disease for bees is American foulbrood. Once a brood is infected, the epidemic character can lead to catastrophic losses. As there is no cure, conventional beekeepers give antibiotics (tetracycline, sulfonamides) as a prophylactic measure, although this practice is prohibited in many conventional operations. The use of antibiotics is prohibited in organic production. The damage can be reduced by treating the adult bees in an artificial swarm, while the wax and brood are burned. The hive itself must be carefully disinfected with soda.

## Hive Management

The ideal number of hives in a beekeeping operation depends on the availability of forage and water. We can provide some recommendations for hive placement:

- Place hives near a water source and a short distance from good food sources, as well as at recommended distances from critical activities.
- Avoid direct exposure to sunlight, selecting a shaded area.
- Verify the abundance of forage plants, vegetation, fruit trees, and agricultural crops, with different flowering times in the vicinity of the hive to ensure the bees' food supply and the duration of honey production.
- Place hive entrances on the opposite side of prevailing winds.

## Honey Treatment

During the extraction and filtration process, the following conditions should be observed:

- It is recommended to extract honey near the apiary to avoid contamination with dust during transport. Extraction sites should be kept clean and equipped with clean water. The floor of these sites should be covered with a plastic tarp.
- During transport, the hives should be completely covered to prevent contamination with dust.
- Only stainless steel extractors are allowed. The use of fats in extractors is not permitted.
- For decapping, only stainless steel knives are allowed.

## Conversion Period

The conversion period refers to the transition from conventional beekeeping to organic beekeeping, which involves incorporating the necessary inputs, materials, and practices into the production unit in accordance with the guidelines for organic honey production. This period must last for at least one year.

The start of the conversion period for conventional to organic beekeeping will be considered when the beekeeper signs the cost proposal and the inspection and certification contract with the certifying body. For a group of small beekeepers, the transition will begin when the beekeeper signs the commitment letter to the internal standards for the production of honey in their organization and is registered in the group of organic beekeepers.

During the conversion period, all beeswax must be replaced with organically produced beeswax. If the analysis of the beeswax shows the presence of residues, such as from conventional acaricides, the beeswax must be replaced. If it is not possible to replace all the beeswax during the one-year period, the period can be extended, which will be decided by the certification body. The beeswax can be beeswax from the caps of the beekeeper, from beekeepers in the same organization, who are also in the process of becoming organic, or from other organic operators.

The frames with replaced beeswax must have a clear marking to distinguish them from frames with still contaminated beeswax.

If, for any reason, non-approved practices or products are used in organic production, the organic production units must undergo a new conversion period in accordance with the guidelines for organic honey production.

During the conversion period, the products from the hives cannot be marketed as organic.

## PART 2: INTERNAL CONTROL SYSTEM IN BEekeeping

## Introduction

One of the main challenges faced by small producers in the world when certifying their products is the high cost of individual certification. To address this problem, and to take advantage of the economies of scale, they have developed their own internal control systems for all their members.

Internal control originated in small producer organizations with the aim of self-regulating production processes and implementing the organic program within them. In this way, organized groups have focused on having a clear understanding of the progress of the organic project within their own organization, which in Mexico is called "visiting," where the responsible organic personnel (now internal inspectors) evaluate the level of compliance of a producer with their work plan related to organic coffee.

From the EU Organic Farming Regulation 2092/91, certification bodies adopt the internal control system as an equivalent method for organic regulation at the level of small producer organizations. This was analyzed in workshops on internal control systems in small producer organizations organized by the Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements IFOAM in 2002 and 2003, based on the systematization work carried out by Naturland and IMO Control of Switzerland in their Quality Assurance Manual: Guide for Internal Control Systems (SCI) in smallholder farms since 1999. This topic was revisited and approved by the European Union's agricultural ecology commission in 2006, publishing the evaluation guide for internal control systems by certification bodies.

Initially, the internal control system was very simple, with some cases only having records of each producer and a list/register of associated producers in the organization.

This control system has functioned as an equivalent to the control and certification systems of organic producers in Europe, the United States, Japan, Canada, etc.

This document aims to be a supportive guide, but it is necessary to clarify that certification bodies and competent authorities constantly make modifications to the rules, procedures, and formats, therefore, organizations interested in using this important tool must be attentive to the recommendations of their certifying agent.

## The small producer organization

It is important to mention that for there to be a discussion of an internal control system, the first thing that must exist is a small producer organization. To do this, each certification body has its own requirements regarding small producer organizations, for example, some certification bodies have established that a small producer organization can be made up of a minimum of 20 small producers, others have said that 10 producers, this depends on the type of product or crop that you want to certify.

In our experience in Mexico, for coffee or honey, the requirements for considering a small producer organization are as follows:

a) Legally organized people with agricultural, livestock, and/or wild collection activities, processing, transformation, and/or commercialization.

b) People legally defined according to the Law of Commercial Societies and the Mexican Agricultural Law as a community, cooperative, association, society, or union.

c) Excludes merchants or individual stockers.

d) Excludes individual producers whose income exceeds USD 20,000 per year for organic product sales or that have more than 25 hectares of the crops they intend to certify for commercialization.

But the requirements of section d) are different for the case of citrus, mango, vegetables, livestock, in which the criterion of 25 hectares does not match reality, for the case of fruit trees, the number of hectares must be greater and for the case of vegetables, the number of hectares must be less than 25, for the case of livestock, the criterion of surface also remains exceeded, in this case, the criterion of the number of livestock heads is more appropriate, 40 or 50 livestock heads can be considered as a small producer in southern Mexico. In summary, the requirements for classifying a producer as a small producer will depend on the product, the region or country in which it is located.

## Certification requirements

For small producer organizations, we recommend that you review the specific requirements established by your certification body, as these generally differ from one certification body to another, but in general, the requirements are as follows:

1. The small producer organization must be legally constituted in accordance with Mexican or national laws.

2. The group must be able to manage and maintain a reliable internal control system.

3. The group must be dedicated to agricultural, livestock, and/or wild collection activities, processing, transformation, and/or commercialization.

4. The small producer group must be within a production system and a relatively consistent agricultural method.

5. The group must have coordinated production and commercial management for the benefit of the collective.

9. The groups must have centralized processing, distribution, and commercialization systems.

## Definition of Internal Control System

IFOAM defines the internal control system as a documented quality assurance system that allows an external certification body to delegate the annual inspection of individual members of a group to an identified entity/unit within the certified operator. (As a result, the main task of the certification body is the evaluation of the functioning of the internal control system.)

## Necessary Personnel

The minimum staff required for a small producer group to establish its internal control system is: the internal control system officer, internal inspectors, and an evaluation committee for questionnaires or internal control reports.

To perform their work, all personnel involved in the internal control system must meet certain minimum requirements: be able to read and write, have experience in ecologically sound crop and process management, to be inspected. The following describes the functions they perform.

The internal control system officer is the person responsible for organizing internal inspections, as well as ensuring that internal control is carried out on 100% of the group's members. They coordinate the evaluation committee and are responsible for creating lists of producers with their respective qualification categories.

Internal inspectors carry out the internal inspection, visit the plots, verify that producers are working in accordance with the group's and the certification body's internal rules and official regulations such as NOP, EU, JAS. They document the inspection by completing an internal inspection questionnaire or checklist for each of the inspected partners.

The evaluation committee is responsible for reviewing and evaluating the internal inspection questionnaires prepared by the internal inspectors. The evaluation consists of determining to what extent beekeepers are complying with certification standards. Based on the level of compliance with the standards, they determine whether to approve or sanction the partners. The committee can be chaired or coordinated by the internal control system officer. Members of the committee should not evaluate the internal control reports of producers or groups with whom they have a conflict of interest.

We have mentioned, within the requirements, that "the group must be able to manage and maintain a competent Internal Control System," however, in reality, there are small organizations with limited influence to a single locality that do not have sufficient staff, and therefore cannot meet this requirement.

In these cases, both organizations hire the services of internal control with experienced organizations or consultants with a presence in the region.

## Execution of Internal Inspection

The way to organize internal inspections and the designation of internal inspectors is specific to each group. The assignment of internal inspectors can be done randomly, by rotation, or by knowledge of the areas. However, the following conditions must be met during execution:

The internal inspection must be carried out at least once a year.

It must include 100% of the beekeepers and apiaries. If extraction, equipment, and storage are carried out at the level of each beekeeper, these must also be inspected.

Internal inspectors must carry out the inspection impartially.

Internal inspectors must not inspect the apiaries of producers for whom they have provided technical assistance, they must not inspect beekeepers or communities where they have a conflict of interest, i.e., they must not inspect the hives of their friends, enemies, and family.

Internal inspectors must complete an internal inspection questionnaire for each apiary they visit.

## From misunderstanding, nothing good can come

The alternative market for organic production methods emerged from the refusal of a small minority of small producers in the primary sector to adapt to a production method that has and has the support of a large part of the scientific community, the agro-industry and public authorities.

An alternative model of production, and which seems to be gaining more space, is emerging directly from the bottom (a unique phenomenon) and is presented as a recognized and recognized tradition of trade.

While we consider the recent developments in defining what constitutes a better organic approach, these elements may still be sources of confusion and misunderstanding.

It's not surprising that the "birth" of a harmonized organic certification has involved a lengthy process, with significant processing times and mediation. A key factor is the increasing openness to the issue, as expressed by consumers in the face of major food scandals, which have created a widespread sense of distrust in conventional production methods.

Today, most consumers, and perhaps organic producers, attribute a number of requirements to the term "organic," such as:

- Higher quality
- Produced in a clean environment
- Produced using "clean" agricultural practices.

In fact, through the relevant EU regulations on agriculture and organic farming, there is no mention of these "key" elements in terms of quality and distinctive characteristics, nor is there any emphasis on environmental protection (with the exception of a small reference to grazing activities).

The legislation defines "biological" (and it must) as the farmer's or beekeeper's business.

Of the three requirements mentioned above, the only one that truly corresponds to the last – "organic" – is the ability to produce and the power of the farmer and beekeeper.

In the growth of the organic movement, and its formalization, confusion and uncertainty about what it is, and what it is, are inherent in this unique and alternative form of food production, which has created, and continues to create, many misunderstandings and misconceptions.

Many of the actors in the field do not see any need to expend energy clarifying things, simply proceeding and benefiting from common misunderstandings.

We believe, on the contrary, that clarifying misconceptions is beneficial.

In the communication and promotion of biological certification, it is important to avoid implying that organic certification is essential for quality (as is the case with "bio") and for a favorable environment, as a whole, distinct, and therefore exempt from the effects of pollution.

It is important to support the dignity of organic farming, and to communicate, for a number of reasons, that organic farming has, and should have, the maximum possible impact on environmental compatibility and positive, varied production.

## The peculiarities of beekeeping.

In this context, it is appropriate to highlight how beekeeping is exploited by a number of unique elements:

- Until the discovery of transit spaces and the subsequent ability to manipulate hives (during the 19th century), humans were unable to penetrate the basic aspects of bee life, and therefore, unable to develop techniques for primitive agriculture. Beekeeping, in other words, was, until recently, more like an instinctive and opportunistic activity, related to livestock farming.
- Human activity cannot significantly affect the way bees are managed, nor can it induce genetic changes similar to those produced by all other forms of "domesticated" life. To such an extent that, until a few years ago (until the spread of the Varroa mite), bees could live, either in the wild or "as a breed".
- And one of the rare forms of agriculture, resulting from the sacrifice of education, but which, in turn, allows humans to benefit only when the bees are kept in optimal conditions. In fact, consuming a single hive for its biological needs requires 220/250 kg of honey and dozens of kilograms of pollen (rich in egg yolk protein), while the average yield of a single hive in many Italian regions is 15 to 40 kg per year.
- The care provided by humans is not primarily dedicated to the care of livestock, but to maintaining the delicate balance of the organic system (in development, controlling the swarm, producing the surplus, wintering).
- The displacement of pastures, practiced since antiquity, depends on the variables (climatic, economic, etc.) that make some areas only suitable for nomadic grazing.
- The number of hives managed is closely related to the high level of professional qualification required for the beekeeper. Therefore, the average size of the farms is a measure of the active unit. Beekeeping, in other words, is easily adaptable to "industrial" activities.
- The area of pasture is approximately three times the area of a large beehive compared to other types of livestock.
- Beekeeping is the rearing of bees without any negative impact on the environment. Instead, it works with a great social value, and is essential for the environment and the production of agricultural and forest products.

- The product of beekeeping is better preserved than other products from the effects of environmental pollution due to the extreme fragility of bees, which are among the most sensitive insect species to any chemical attack.

In conclusion, for many of its aspects, beekeeping is, in comparison with other forms of livestock farming, already very close to organic production methods.

Of course, there are exceptions, especially for some that are consistent with a model of North American management, but they are certainly not the rule at the global and European level, and are, in particular, in the Italian beekeeping sector.

The only aspect that has triumphed with conventional logic, with its limitations and defects, is the aspect of disease control. Antibiotics and sulfonamides, in the "prevention" of bee diseases, and pyrethroids, etc., have been used.

The real distinction between ecological beekeeping and conventional beekeeping is in the methods of combating health problems.

The legislation for ecological beekeeping was developed by a committee composed of the community, mainly of people without specific technical knowledge and knowledge of beekeeping (and so on).

Therefore, for beekeeping and bee breeding, the attention has been focused on the aspects of trust, without understanding the discriminatory legal aberration, such as the environment and the quality of the pastures, regardless of the activity and the power of the beekeeper and by the filter that is applied in the case of important environmental contamination.

The European legislation will focus on these aspects.

In practice, it is essential to take into account the environmental limitations, remembering that "essentially" the limits specified and defined as the qualification and distinctive aspect are very well defined, in accordance with a biological approach, and are, above all, aimed at combating health problems.

- Concentrating and focusing activities on the real discriminating factor in relation to conventional care (health, feeding, quality of the wax, etc.) that the beekeeper undertakes in the care of the hives.

A more rigorous quality certification in beekeeping is an absolute necessity for the future of this type of production.

A serious application of the requirements for the transition to organic farming can be a stimulus for the growth of a productive sector, such as beekeeping, which is very small, but of great importance for the quality of the environment and the production of many agroforestry crops.

For industrial processes, where the product undergoes a change, the Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) are applied. Animal health, plant health and food safety are then based on risk mitigation programs, which, when properly managed and verified, provide the guarantees that the Integrated MSF Health System (SSIMSF) functions properly and that the country, for that product chain, is reliable.
