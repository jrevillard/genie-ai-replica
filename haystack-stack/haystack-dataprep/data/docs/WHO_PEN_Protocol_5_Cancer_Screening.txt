WHO PEN Protocol 5 — Early Detection and Referral of Cancer

PROTOCOL 5: CANCER SCREENING AND EARLY DETECTION

1. PURPOSE
- Cancer is increasingly common in The Gambia due to aging population and lifestyle changes
- Early detection dramatically improves survival for many cancer types
- Health workers at primary care level can identify warning signs and refer promptly
- Focus on cancers that can be detected early: cervical, breast, oral, and common childhood cancers

2. GENERAL CANCER WARNING SIGNS (Red Flags)
Any of these should prompt referral for investigation:
- Unexplained weight loss (more than 5kg in 1-2 months without trying)
- Persistent fatigue not explained by other conditions
- Unexplained pain that persists for more than 2-3 weeks
- Lump or thickening anywhere on the body that is new or growing
- Wound or sore that does not heal within 3 weeks
- Unusual bleeding or discharge
- Change in bowel or bladder habits lasting more than 2 weeks
- Difficulty swallowing that persists
- Persistent hoarseness or cough lasting more than 3 weeks
- Change in appearance of a mole or skin lesion
- Night sweats (drenching, not related to hot weather)

3. CERVICAL CANCER

Risk factors:
- HPV infection (human papillomavirus) — main cause
- Early sexual debut (before age 18)
- Multiple sexual partners
- HIV infection
- Smoking
- Not being screened previously
- Immunosuppression

Warning signs (refer immediately):
- Post-coital bleeding (bleeding after sexual intercourse)
- Abnormal vaginal bleeding (between periods, after menopause)
- Persistent vaginal discharge (especially foul-smelling or blood-stained)
- Pelvic pain (persistent)
- Pain during intercourse

Screening:
- WHO recommends screening all women aged 30-49 (can extend to 25-65 in high-risk populations)
- VIA (Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid): available at health centre level
  * Apply 3-5% acetic acid to cervix, wait 1 minute
  * White areas (acetowhite) suggest pre-cancerous changes
  * Positive VIA → immediate cryotherapy or LEEP (if available) or refer
- HPV testing: more sensitive, available at some facilities
- Pap smear: requires lab processing — available at regional hospitals

Prevention:
- HPV vaccination: WHO recommends for girls aged 9-14 (before sexual debut)
- The Gambia has introduced HPV vaccination in its national immunization program
- Screening every 3-5 years for VIA-negative women
- Condom use reduces HPV transmission risk

Treatment of pre-cancer:
- Cryotherapy: freezing treatment at health centre, done same day as VIA (screen-and-treat approach)
- LEEP: loop electrosurgical excision — available at some hospitals
- Early invasive cancer: needs surgery and/or radiotherapy at EFSTH or abroad

4. BREAST CANCER

Risk factors:
- Age (risk increases after 40)
- Family history (mother, sister with breast cancer)
- Early menarche (first period before age 12) or late menopause
- Never breastfed
- Obesity (especially after menopause)
- Hormonal contraceptive use (slight increase)
- Alcohol use

Warning signs (refer immediately):
- New lump in breast or armpit (painless lumps are MORE concerning than painful ones)
- Change in breast size or shape
- Skin changes: dimpling, puckering, redness, orange-peel texture
- Nipple changes: inversion, discharge (especially bloody), rash on nipple
- Persistent breast pain (especially if one-sided)

Clinical Breast Examination (CBE):
- Should be performed by trained health worker at every visit for women 30+
- Systematic inspection and palpation of both breasts and axillae
- Patient examined in sitting and supine positions
- ANY suspicious finding → refer for further investigation (ultrasound, biopsy)

Breast Self-Examination:
- Teach all women to examine their breasts monthly
- Best time: 5-7 days after start of period (when breasts are least lumpy)
- Look in mirror: arms at sides, then raised, then on hips — check for changes
- Feel: use pads of three middle fingers, circular motion, cover entire breast + armpit
- Report any change to health worker promptly
- Most lumps are NOT cancer — but all should be checked

Treatment:
- Depends on stage — surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormonal therapy
- Early-stage breast cancer has 5-year survival > 90% with treatment
- Late-stage: much lower survival — emphasizes importance of early detection
- Treatment available at EFSTH and referral abroad for complex cases

5. ORAL CANCER

Risk factors:
- Tobacco use (smoking AND chewing)
- Betel nut chewing (less common in Gambia)
- Heavy alcohol use
- HPV infection
- Poor oral hygiene
- Sun exposure (lip cancer)

Warning signs:
- White or red patch in mouth that doesn't go away in 2 weeks
- Sore or ulcer in mouth that doesn't heal in 3 weeks
- Persistent sore throat or feeling of something stuck
- Difficulty chewing or moving tongue
- Numbness in mouth or lips
- Lump in neck
- Loose teeth without dental cause
- Jaw swelling

Action: Any of these lasting > 2-3 weeks → refer to hospital for biopsy

6. CHILDHOOD CANCERS
Common childhood cancers that present at primary care:
- Burkitt lymphoma: rapidly growing jaw or abdominal mass (common in Sub-Saharan Africa, linked to malaria and EBV)
- Retinoblastoma: white pupil reflex (cat's eye), squinting — check with torch/flashlight
- Wilms tumor: abdominal mass in child, often painless
- Leukemia: persistent fever, pallor, bruising, bone pain, enlarged lymph nodes

Red flags in children (refer urgently):
- Unexplained weight loss
- Persistent fever > 2 weeks not responding to malaria/antibiotic treatment
- Unusual bruising or bleeding
- Persistent bone/joint pain
- Growing lump or mass
- White reflex in eye (check with torch)
- Persistent headache with vomiting (especially morning)
- Night sweats

7. CANCER AND NCD OVERLAP
- Diabetes increases risk of: liver, pancreatic, colorectal, breast, bladder cancer
- Hypertension increases risk of: kidney cancer
- Obesity increases risk of: many cancers (breast, colorectal, endometrial, kidney, liver)
- Smoking increases risk of: lung, bladder, cervical, oral, pancreatic, kidney cancer
- NCD lifestyle interventions (healthy diet, exercise, no tobacco) also PREVENT cancer

8. PAIN MANAGEMENT IN CANCER
WHO Pain Ladder:
Step 1 (mild pain): Paracetamol 500-1000mg every 6 hours, Ibuprofen 400mg every 8 hours
Step 2 (moderate pain): Add Codeine or Tramadol
Step 3 (severe pain): Oral Morphine (needs prescription, may be limited availability)

IMPORTANT: Cancer pain should be treated. Patients should not suffer in silence.
Refer to hospital if pain is not controlled with Step 1-2 medications.

9. PSYCHOSOCIAL SUPPORT
- Cancer diagnosis causes significant psychological distress
- Acknowledge fear and grief — listen without rushing
- Involve family with patient's permission
- Connect with cancer support groups if available
- Spiritual support (Imam, pastor) if patient requests
- Address practical concerns: cost of treatment, transport, childcare
- Palliative care discussion when appropriate — focus on comfort and dignity

10. REFERRAL PATHWAY
- Health post/CHW → Health centre (VIA, CBE, general examination)
- Health centre → Regional hospital (ultrasound, basic lab tests, biopsy)
- Regional hospital → EFSTH (surgery, pathology, chemotherapy)
- EFSTH → Abroad (radiotherapy, complex surgery — if funding available)

GAMBIAN CONTEXT:
- Cervical cancer is the most common cancer in Gambian women
- Breast cancer is the second most common
- HPV vaccination program introduced for girls aged 9-14
- VIA screening available at some health centres — expanding
- EFSTH has surgical capability and basic chemotherapy
- Radiotherapy NOT available in Gambia — patients referred abroad (Senegal, Morocco)
- MRC Gambia at Fajara conducts cancer research — may have clinical trials
- Cancer Society of The Gambia provides some support services
- Traditional healers often first point of contact — encourage patients to ALSO seek medical evaluation
- Late presentation is common — community education is critical
