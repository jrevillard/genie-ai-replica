## Introduction

Significant advances in plant nutrition have made it possible to currently access a wealth of knowledge and studies on the inputs provided to the soil, particularly fertilizers, as well as the nutritional needs of different crops. Despite all of this, it is clear that the soil remains a key factor in crop productivity, as it is not entirely predictable, as are weather conditions. Undoubtedly, this adds uncertainty to the results of various agricultural activities. Therefore, measures are needed to help rationalize the management of fertilizers, machinery, seeds, water management, and phytosanitary aspects, among others.

## The trend in fertilization plans

The effect that fertilizers have on the quantity and quality of harvests has been extensively studied. However, the specific needs of crops, application techniques, and times, as well as the sources of nutrients, vary, and must be fine-tuned over several crop cycles. In more specific terms, the goal is to develop perfectly tailored plans, considering the overall conditions of the land in the farms.

## Importance of Soil Fertility

Soil fertility is a matter of great importance in global food production, as it is the soil that supports the production of most crops. For a long time, this concept

<!-- image -->

was considered solely as the content of nutrients in the soil, but today it is known that it includes many more factors. Undoubtedly, the content of nutrients is one of the most important for production, but it is important not to forget aspects of physical fertility (soil depth, compaction, aeration, drainage, soil water variables) that are often more limiting to crop growth and can make it difficult to absorb nutrients. It is also important to consider the chemical fertility of the soil (cation exchange capacity, carbonate content, salinity, sodicity, pH). To improve soil fertility and the productivity of any crop, it is not enough to simply apply nutrients; all of the factors mentioned above must be considered, otherwise, only unnecessary expenses will be incurred, and the problem may even be exacerbated.

## Importance of Macronutrients in Plant Nutrition

Macronutrients (N, P, and K) are required by crops in considerable amounts. The soil, on the other hand, often has a reduced supply of these nutrients. These nutrients have been the focus of fertilization practices, and they have achieved the most spectacular increases in yields in most crops. Both the sources that supply them in the soil, and their retention or solubility, are very different. For these reasons, fertilization and techniques should present differences that help to improve or manage each nutrient more efficiently. The nutritional needs are defined by the species, where it is necessary to consider quantity, quality, form, and timing of application.

## Potash Fertilization

This essential nutrient for plants is abundant in many soils, and in some cases, it may be present in surface or subsurface waters. It is common to find potassium in the soil minerals (clays). Because of the abundance of potassium in the soil, one might think that it is not a limiting factor for production, but unfortunately, only a small portion of the total potassium in soils is available or potentially usable by plants. To describe this in more detail, there are three situations in the soil: 1. Potassium in minerals with very slow release (negligible); 2. Potassium that is exchangeable and releases quickly; and 3. Potassium in soil solution that is immediately assimilable. These two forms of potassium in the soil are those that supply the plant. Potassium minerals are highly soluble; however, the soil often retains potassium well, except in very sandy soils. In very clayey soils, a phenomenon of potassium immobilization can occur, where this is bound to mineral forms for a long time.

## Aspects for Potash Fertilization

The solubility of potassium in fertilizers is not a problem. The most economical one is potassium chloride, and it is highly soluble. Therefore, the potassium provided by mineral fertilizers will be immediately assimilable. Although, potassium does not evaporate, and the risk of loss by leaching is low, the ease of assimilation of potassium can be lost, in this case, by fixation to the clay minerals of the soil. The fixation of this potassium can last for a long time until a change in the minerals occurs.

Organic sources have a significant amount of potassium, a portion in soluble form and another portion bound to organic structures (not immediately assimilable). The release of the portion bound to organic structures will occur at the same rate as the mineralization of these materials. Potassium remains largely in the plant's structures, so if the plant's remains are returned to the soil, the most important part of the potassium extracted will be replenished.

In the soil, potassium presents antagonisms with other nutrients, particularly magnesium, therefore high levels of potassium affect the absorption of magnesium. For potassium, it is particularly important the phenomenon known as "luxury consumption", which occurs when plants that develop in soils with abundant potassium extract and incorporate much more than they need.

## Importance of Soil Analysis in Potash Fertilization

Soil analysis allows you to know the status of available potassium in the soil. This data, along with the needs of the crop, is essential for designing fertilization programs. There are various methods for determining available potassium in the soil. The most common is the "extraction with ammonium acetate" in which the information on exchangeable potassium and potassium in solution is obtained. This method is the one recommended by NOM-021 for analyzing potassium in Mexican soils.
