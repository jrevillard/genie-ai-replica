## DESCRIPTION

Adults are beetles, weevils or caterpillars. They lay their eggs, which are white or pale in color, at a depth of three to more than three centimeters in the soil.

Larvae or worms are robust, in the shape of a 'C', and measure three to three and a half centimeters. They are soft, white, with a brown head, and have no visible eyes. They have strong mandibles.

Pupae are found in the soil, in cells constructed at a depth of 15-20 cm. They remain inactive during the summer. Their life cycle can be one to two years, depending on the species.

## DAMAGE

Larvae feed on the roots, completely destroying them and, in almost all cases, causing the death of the plants. The attack usually occurs in localized areas of the crop, so there are patches of damage in the field.

This pest causes more frequent damage in soils where there were grasslands or where the soil was covered with grass.

## CONTROL

Critical level: One larva per three soil samples of 30 × 30 cm and 20 cm deep.

Cultural: In soils where there were grasslands or soils severely infested, remove grassy weeds (e.g., couch grass, foxtail). Deep plowing of the soil buries and exposes the larvae and pupae to the sun, birds and other predators.

Biological: Fungi such as Metarrhizium and Beauveria effectively control this pest.

Chemical: Granular insecticides can be used in the soil.

## DESCRIPTION

Adults are gray or brown. They have an elongated body. They have no legs, are soft and moist. When moving, they leave a sticky, shiny secretion. The eggs are laid in masses in moist places such as trunks, stones, soil or under garbage.

Young snails have the same appearance as adults, but are smaller. They reproduce very easily. They are hermaphroditic and very prolific. Their life cycle depends on the humidity of the place where they live. During dry periods, they remain inactive, with a reproductive explosion when the rains begin (late season in beans).

## PESTS

Throughout Central America, the snail is the main pest affecting bean crops. Both adults and young snails feed on the leaves of plants, creating irregular holes. They also feed on the shoots of newly emerging plants. In severe infestations, they can cause the complete loss of leaves, significantly reducing yields.

Snails cause damage on cloudy and humid days, or during the night. In the summer, most snails die due to the dry environment.

## CONTROL

Critical level: One snail per square meter in the first planting, and one snail per two square meters in the second planting.

Cultural: Remove weeds, especially broadleaf weeds, and other host plants from within and around the crop. Destroy crop residue. Maintain good drainage.

Manual: Use traps to manually collect and destroy snails. Killing one snail in the first planting reduces the population by 50 in the second planting.

Chemical: Apply metaldehyde-based baits throughout the field in the late afternoon. Do not use insecticides, as they are ineffective.

## 3. Agrotis ipsilon, A. bilitora, Feltia experimentalis, F. subterranea (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae): BLACK CUTWORM, GROUNDWORM, NIGHTMOTH, CORN BORER

## DESCRIPTION

Adults are robust, greyish moths. Their wings have a transverse band of black spots. They lay their eggs individually or in small clusters in the moist soil or on the underside of young leaves. The eggs are globular and white.

Larvae are thick, brownish with pale yellowish dorsal markings when young. They later turn greyish-black and can reach up to five centimeters in length.

Larvae are nocturnal. During the day, they hide in the soil, pupating and turning a bright brown color.

## DAMAGE

Damage is significant in young plants. The larvae cut the stems at ground level, killing the plants. Smaller larvae graze on the stems, weakening their growth.

Crops with a high presence of grassy weeds are more susceptible to this pest.

## CONTROL

Critical level: Before planting, one larva per five soil samples of 30 × 30 cm and 20 cm deep. After planting, one damaged plant per 20 plants sampled.

Cultural: Remove weeds and prepare the soil 15 days before planting. Continuous watering affects the development of larvae.

Biological: There are ichneumonid or braconid wasps that parasitize the larvae and pupae. There are also tachnid flies that attack the larvae.

Chemical: Apply insecticides at night, either contact or ingestion; baits with molasses, chaff, and granular insecticides at the base of the plant; and seed treatments with insecticides.

## 4. Elasmopalpus lignosellus (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae): MINOR SHOOT Borer, CORN BORER, HOLE MOTH

## DESCRIPTION

Adults are small, greyish-brown moths. They lay their eggs in stems, leaves, and soil. The eggs are white-green.

Larvae are thin and move quickly. They are green to greenish-yellow, with reddish-brown bands or rings. They are two centimeters long.

Pupae are enclosed in a silken cocoon, one to two centimeters from the soil surface. Their life cycle lasts 30 to 35 days.

## DAMAGE

Larvae cause significant damage by boring into the stems of young plants, killing them. Inside the stem, the larvae eat from the bottom up.

Damaged plants become wilted or fall over. The attacks are more severe in sandy soils and in dry conditions.

## CONTROL

Critical level: One larva per two soil samples of 30 × 30 cm and 20 cm deep.

Cultural: Rotate crops. The field should be free of weeds before planting. Watering with a large amount of water kills larvae and pupae. If there are isolated cases of plants with damage, they should be uprooted and replanted.

Chemical: At planting, apply systemic or contact granular insecticides. It is very difficult to control this pest after planting.

## DESCRIPTION

In the adult stage, they are moths with a rounded thorax. The eggs are round and green. The females lay them individually on the underside of leaves.

The larvae are green with pale yellow lateral stripes. Their thoracic legs are always white and have three pairs of false legs on their hind end. They walk, measuring the surface they are on. The larvae pupate in a cocoon woven on the underside of leaves.

Their life cycle lasts 25 to 30 days.

## PESTS

The larvae feed on leaves and pods. High populations of larvae can significantly reduce yields.

A well-established bean crop can tolerate up to 30% leaf damage (defoliation).

## CONTROL

Critical level: The recommended level is 26 larvae per linear meter of bean plants.

Cultural: Using varieties with good recovery ability helps maintain crop yield. It is recommended to manage densities to control the pest.

Biological: It can be controlled with Trichogramma wasps, Telenomus remus. There are predators that destroy all stages of the pest. The bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis, the Nuclear Polyhedrosis Virus (NPV), and several entomopathogenic fungi such as Beauveria basiana control this pest.

Chemical: Do not use agrochemicals. In high infestations, use low doses of contact or ingestion insecticides.

The adults are white in color. They have black spots on their wings. Their abdomen is orange with transverse black stripes. The female lays her eggs in large masses on any leaf surface. The eggs are large, spherical, and yellow.

Newly hatched larvae are yellow and hairy. They later change from yellow-purple to dark brown. They have long, brown or black hairs that cover the entire body. They can reach up to 5 cm in length.

They pupate on the soil surface, among plant debris.

The larvae form groups of several individuals on the underside of the leaves. They eat the entire leaf, except for the veins. The attacked leaves have a skeleton-like appearance. When they attack all the leaves on a plant, they move to another. In large populations, they can leave the crop without leaves. The plants are severely attacked when the pods are ripening.

## CONTROL

Critical level: Thirteen larvae per 10 plants, or leaf damage greater than 20%.

Cultural: Remove host weeds such as Bidens pilosa and Amaranthus spp. Use living barriers or trenches to prevent larvae from other nearby crops. Biological: In their larval and pupal stage, they can be controlled with several species of predatory beetles. The bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis, the Nuclear Polyhedrosis Virus (NPV), and some entomopathogenic fungi control this pest.

Chemical: Used very sparingly. If used, use broad-spectrum contact or ingestion insecticides for lepidoptera.

## 7. Cerotoma spp., Diabrotica spp. (Coleoptera: Chrysomelidae): CRISOMELIDS, CUCARRONCITES, TORTUGUILLAS, DIABROTICAS, MAYAS

## DESCRIPTION

The adults are small beetles (0.5 cm), oval in shape. They have a variety of colors and different types of spots on their wings. They have thin legs and segmented antennae. The eggs are placed in the soil in masses of 12 to 14 eggs. They are yellowish and pointed. The larvae are very small, white, with a brown-dark head and tail. They have small legs near the head and live in the soil.

The pupae are found in the soil, are soft, and are enclosed in a cocoon. Their life cycle lasts 30 to 35 days.

## PESTS

The larvae feed on roots. The adults eat leaves and pods. In small plants, they also eat the stems. The damage is seen as round perforations.

The most severe damage is in small plants, as these are weaker and have few leaves. Some species transmit diseases caused by viruses, for example, the Common Mosaic. In severe infestations, yields can be reduced by 25 to 30%.

## CONTROL

Critical level: One beetle per two plants sampled in the same area, from germination to the appearance of two trifoliate leaves. Cultural: Remove weeds within and around the crop. The weevil reduces the pest population. Use trap crops in the surrounding area, such as cucurbitaceae, to reduce damage. Biological: Apply entomopathogenic fungi such as Bauveria basiana. Ladybugs control the pest but do not provide complete control. Insecticides, granules applied to the soil or systemic applied to the seed, control the larvae. Adults are controlled with foliar applications of contact or ingestion insecticides.

## 8. Epilachna varivestis (Coleoptera: Coccinellidae): BEAN BEETLE, MEXICAN BEAN BEETLE

## DESCRIPTION

The adults are rounded in shape and are brownish. They have three rows of black spots on their wings. They are small insects, measuring between six and seven millimeters.

The yellow-orange eggs are laid in masses on the underside of the leaves.

The larvae are yellow, oval in shape, and have six rows of branched spines with black tips on their back. They measure eight to ten millimeters in length.

The pupae are attached to the underside of the leaves and are yellow-orange.

They live for 25 to 30 days.

## DAMAGE

This insect causes damage to the crop in its larval and adult stages. It feeds on the lower surface of the leaves, leaving the upper layer largely intact, giving it a skeletal appearance.

When populations are high, these insects attack the pods and stems, sometimes causing the death of the plants.

## CONTROL

Critical level: 25% leaf loss before flowering. 15% after flowering.

Cultural: Avoid planting in high densities and destroy or incorporate crop residue. Use early or fast-growing varieties.

Biological: Eulophid wasps and tachinid flies parasitize larvae and pupae.

Chemical: When planting, use granular insecticides in the soil, taking care not to come into contact with the seeds. Contact or ingestion insecticides can be used on the foliage.

## DESCRIPTION

Adults are mottled, brownish-purple to pale yellowish-brown, with black markings. They generally have a pale, dark-edged diagonal line across the forewings and hindwings.

They lay their eggs on the underside of leaves, in groups or individually.

Larvae are slender, pale green to dark brown. They have yellow or pale stripes along the sides of the body and on the back. The segments are separated by velvety bands in the skin. They are active, wriggling and jumping from the plant when disturbed.

## DAMAGE

Larvae are voracious feeders of foliage and pods. In high densities, they can leave the crop without leaves in a short time.

They begin by feeding on the leaves at the top of the plant. Once they have removed all the foliage, they feed on the most succulent part of the stem.

The worst infestations occur during the dry season.

In their first three stages, they can be mistaken for cutworms, due to their appearance when walking.

## CONTROL

Critical level: 30% leaf loss when plants are flowering.

Cultural: Maintain high densities and varieties with good recovery from defoliation.

Biological: The bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis*, the Nuclear Polyhedrosis Virus, and several entomopathogenic fungi control this pest.

Chemical: Use contact or ingestion insecticides. For good control, the insecticide needs to penetrate well.

Adults are nocturnal, brownish-gray moths with spots on their wings. They lay their eggs in masses of 40 to 300 on leaves and stems. The eggs are initially pale green and then turn gray.

Larvae are plump and greenish-gray or dark gray. They can reach up to 3.5 cm in length. During the day, they hide in the soil.

In rows, the plants are affected, leading to a decrease in the crop population and a reduction in yields.

Biological: Ichneumonid and braconid wasps, as well as tachinid flies, parasitize the larvae of this pest.

Chemical: Apply contact or ingestion insecticides in the evening or at night. Use granular insecticides at the base of the plant and treat the seeds. Use baits with molasses, chaff, and insecticide.

These adult insects are small, reddish-green spiders, difficult to spot at first glance. They measure 0.5 mm and have four pairs of legs.

Their eggs are round, translucent, and they lay them individually, in a scattered manner, on the plants.

Chemical: Apply acaricides to the foliage. Only do this when it is economically justified.

## DESCRIPTION

Adults are very small, measuring approximately one millimeter. They have two pairs of wings. They are found on the underside of leaves. When the foliage moves, they fly quickly. The adult female lays up to 160 eggs on the underside of the leaves. Their eggs are oval and tiny. The nymphs are pale yellow, oval, and flattened. They have no legs or wings and appear like scales. Fully developed nymphs measure less than one millimeter.

The insect completes up to 15 generations during each crop cycle.

## DAMAGE

The nymphs feed by sucking the sap from the plants. Affected leaves show scattered, yellowish spots and become curled or wrinkled. In cases of high populations, the entire foliage turns yellow. When feeding, they secrete a sticky substance that covers the leaves and flowers. A black fungus called fumagina grows on this substance. The plants stop growing, lose vigor, and produce very little.

The insect causes more damage as a vector of viruses than by direct feeding. The whitefly transmits geminivirus, such as the golden mosaic virus, the chlorotic mosaic virus, and the dwarf mosaic virus.

## CONTROL

Critical Level: Manage zero tolerance due to its role as a vector for many viral diseases. Take control measures as soon as a single whitefly is found in the crop.

Genetic: Plant virus-resistant varieties.

Cultural: Remove plants with viruses, weeds, and cucumber, tomato, soybean, tobacco, and cotton plants, which attract the whitefly and may carry viruses. Avoid planting during dry periods when the attack is more severe. Use live barriers of corn or sorghum.

Chemical: Do not use chemicals long-term. If necessary, rotate the insecticides applied. Use systemic insecticides.

## DESCRIPTION

Adults and immature stages (nymphs) are the same, differing only in size. They are very small insects (2-2.5 mm), with a soft body, in the shape of a pear. They are yellow, green, pink, gray, or black, depending on the species. Some adults have wings.

These insects can be found throughout the plant, preferring the underside of the leaves.

## DAMAGE

Aphids are sucking insects. Both adults and nymphs suck the sap from leaves, shoots, stems, and flowers.

Their saliva is toxic. Leaves that have been bitten become curled and wrinkled and eventually fall off the plant. This damage weakens the plants and causes them to become stunted.

Plants severely affected by the pest turn black. This is due to the growth of the fumagina fungus on a sticky substance excreted by these insects.

They are important vectors of various viruses, such as CMV and the rough mosaic virus.

## CONTROL

Critical Level: For winged aphids, four insects per five plants sampled. For wingless aphids, a group of insects per two plants sampled.

Cultural: Plant in high density in areas where there are many aphids. Remove plants with symptoms of virus and cucumber, tomato, soybean, tobacco, or cotton plants that are within the crop. Avoid planting during dry periods. Plant live barriers of corn or sorghum.

Biological: Ladybugs (Hippodamia convergens), predatory ladybugs (Coccinellidae), and the insect called "aphid lion" (Chrysoperla externa) are voracious aphid predators. Parasitoid wasps (L. testaceipes and D. rapae) are also available commercially.

Chemical: Apply systemic insecticides to the foliage. Apply agricultural oil and soapy water (1%) or with wheat flour to the entire foliage.

## DESCRIPTION

Adults and nymphs are cone-shaped. They measure a maximum of three millimeters. Adults have wings, while nymphs do not and are smaller. They are green in color and have white spots on the head and on the front of the thorax. When threatened, they walk sideways. They move by jumping or flying.

They lay their eggs individually, leaving them embedded in the plant's tissues. These insects live for 20 to 25 days.

## DAMAGE

When they suck sap from plants, they inject a toxin that causes the leaves to curl downwards. Affected leaves turn reddish-brown, and in severe cases, they dry out.

Plants affected by this pest do not grow. If the damage occurs during flowering, the number of pods is reduced. Pods that do develop are malformed. The pest significantly reduces yields.

Summer, drought, and high temperatures exacerbate the damage.

## CONTROL

Critical level: One adult or three or more nymphs per plant. To assess the number of aphids, gently move the foliage of the plants with your hand.

Cultural: Schedule planting dates to avoid hot and dry periods. Leave crop residues in the soil. Rain and sprinkler irrigation reduce pest populations.

Biological: There are species of wasps that parasitize the eggs (Anagrus sp.) and are predators.

Genetic: Use resistant varieties.

Chemical: In cases of high incidence, use systemic granular insecticides. Apply contact or ingestion insecticides before pod development.

## 17. Epinotia aporema, Laspeyresia leguminis (Lepidoptera: Tortricidae), Helicoverpa zea, H. virescens (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae): POD AND HOLE-MAKING IN PODS

## DESCRIPTION

The adults are typical greyish moths, with a length of between 1.5 and 3 centimeters.

They lay their eggs in masses of greenish-grey color. They lay them on shoots, flowers, and young pods.

The pupae are subterranean.

Their life cycle is 20 to 30 days.

## DAMAGE

Newly hatched larvae feed on the soft parts of the plant. Once developed, they enter the pods. The main damage is to the seeds. Pods with larvae rot due to the presence of fungi.

Severe infestations significantly reduce yields.

## CONTROL

Critical level: One larva in 20 sampled pods.

Cultural: Planting away from legumes and removing weeds within and around the crop. Early planting and harvesting can help prevent high infestations of this pest.

Biological: Larvae can be controlled by applying pathogens such as Nuclear Polyhedrosis Virus, Bacillus thuringiensis, and entomopathogenic fungi. There is also a wide variety of predators and parasites.

Chemical: Apply systemic contact or ingestion insecticides during flowering and pod formation.

## 18. Trichapion godmani (Coleoptera: Curculionidae): POD BITE, POD BORER, PEA BITE

## DESCRIPTION

The adults are small beetles (2.5 to 3 millimeters). They are greyish in color. Their upper wings are hard and striated. Their main characteristic is their elongated proboscis.

The larvae develop inside the pods. They are white and curved in shape. Their head is dark brown. They measure 2 to 3 millimeters. The females lay eggs, one at a time, on the newly formed pod tissue. They can lay up to 390 eggs in their lifetime.

The pupae are naked and found inside the pods.

Their life cycle is 20 days.

## DAMAGE

The main damage is caused by the larvae that feed on the grains. When laying eggs, they cause damage to the pods, causing circular yellow scars and malformations.

These insects can significantly reduce the yield and quality of the grains.

## CONTROL

Critical level: Eight adults per square meter, during flowering and pod formation.

Cultural: Interrupt the life cycle of this insect by ensuring uniform planting dates, proper weed management, and destruction of volunteer plants or host plants.

Genetic: Use resistant varieties.

Biological: Control with predators and parasites is not effective. The entomopathogenic fungi Metharrhizium anisopliae and Beauveria basiana have shown greater effectiveness.

Chemical: Apply contact insecticides throughout the foliage, with very fine atomization.

## DESCRIPTION

The adults are small, narrow-bodied beetles (3 mm) in front and wide in the back. They are grey or brown in color, with clear spots on the upper part of the abdomen.

They lay their eggs on mature pods or on the grains. These are white, oval-shaped, and not attached to the grains.

Their larvae are white, thick, curved, and lack legs.

The pupae develop in larval cells or capsules, growing naked.

Their life cycle is 25 to 30 days.

## DAMAGE

This pest begins to cause damage at the end of flowering. By this stage, the larvae are eating the seeds. The infestation then moves to the stored grain.

In its larval stage, it attacks all types of beans, cowpeas, and chickpeas.

This pest has significant economic importance, as it can destroy the entire stored grain.

## CONTROL

Cultural: Destroy the life cycle by removing or incorporating the plant debris. Harvesting crops early can prevent infestations. Warehouses and silos should be clean and dry. Exposing the grain to sunlight reduces damage.

Chemical: Apply contact or ingestion insecticides during the formation of the pods, only when weevils are present. In storage, apply contact insecticides on the floor, walls, and ceiling of the warehouse. For curative treatment, apply phosphamine in enclosed spaces. This treatment is highly toxic and should only be carried out by trained personnel.

Physical-Mechanical: Sprinkle the grain with ash, crystalline silica, clay, magnesium carbonate, or lime. The seeds can be treated with vegetable oil.

## 20. Zabrotes subfasciatus (Coleoptera: Bruchidae): PAINTED WEevil, MEXICAN BEAN WEevil

## DESCRIPTION

The adults are similar to those of A. obtectus. They are dark brown with cream-colored spots on the wings and thorax. The males are smaller than the females.

They lay their eggs on the seeds. Newly laid eggs are translucent, and after a few days, they turn white.

The larvae and pupae are similar to those of A. obtectus.

Their life cycle is 25-30 days.

## DAMAGE

This is a pest that only attacks stored grains. The damage is caused by the larvae inside the bean. The damage is similar to that caused by A. obtectus.

## CONTROL

Cultural: To prevent damage from this insect that feeds on harvested and stored grains, it is recommended to leave the bean in its pod. Keep warehouses and silos clean and dry.

Chemical: Same as A. obtectus.

Physical-Mechanical: Sprinkle the grain with ash, crystalline silica, clay, magnesium carbonate, or lime. The seeds can be treated with vegetable oil.

Genetic: Varieties containing the arcelin protein offer resistance to this pest. This also applies to A. obtectus.

## Glossary

Predator: An insect that feeds on another insect.

Natural enemy: An organism that helps to reduce insect populations by killing them or reducing their reproductive rate.

Phenology: The study of the periodic phenomena of plant development in relation to the environment.

Entomopathogenic fungus: Fungi that parasitize and kill insects.

Host: An organism that, at some point in its life, serves as a host for another organism to develop or complete its life cycle.

Vector insect: Any organism that transmits diseases directly or indirectly to plants.

Larva: The immature stage of insects with complete metamorphosis.

Mosaic: Areas of pale green, clearly bordered by small veins, giving the foliage a distinctly angular appearance.

Nymph: The immature stage of insects with incomplete metamorphosis.

Critical level: The population of a pest below which control is not necessary, and above which control is recommended.

Parasitoid

Pest

: An insect that feeds on another insect but does not kill it until it has developed. : Any organism that causes economic damage to a crop.

Virus: A disease caused by a virus and almost always transmitted by a vector insect.
