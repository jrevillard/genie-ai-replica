## I. INTRODUCTION

In El Salvador, the majority of avocado trees planted are of criollo germplasm, selected in different parts of the country, classified within the Guatemalan and Antilles races, presenting, among other characteristics, their adaptation from sea level to 900 meters above sea level, and all areas of the country are considered to have potential for cultivation, provided that the soil conditions allow. However, with the introduction of the Hass variety, cultivation has expanded to areas from 1000 to 2000 meters above sea level. And the booth 8 variety with a range of 250 to 1250 meters above sea level.

The global avocado trade is particularly aimed at European markets; 60% of the total is consumed in France, followed by Germany, the United Kingdom, and Italy. The main producers are Mexico, the United States, China, Southeast Asia, the Philippines, Israel, Algeria, Cuba, Kenya, and Spain (Canary Islands).

El Salvador, despite being located in the origin and domestication center of the avocado, does not produce enough for domestic consumption. In 2016, a cultivated area of 1,595 hectares was recorded, resulting in a production of 360,876 kg, with an average yield of 226 kg/hectare, representing 76% of national consumption, requiring imports of 1,148,631 kg in 2016, with a value of $12,083,415 (MAG 2017); this cultivation represents a very valuable alternative for the diversification of our agriculture, both for its profitability, potential market, and nutritional value.

## II. GENERAL INFORMATION

## IMPORTANCE

The avocado (Persea americana Miller), is a fruit with growing acceptance among consumers due to its nutritional content of proteins, vitamins, minerals, and its health benefits thanks to its monounsaturated fat, which contributes to the reduction of cholesterol and total triglycerides in the body. In addition, it offers different options for consumption in fresh, processed form, and its use in the cosmetic industry (National Cancer Institute, 1992).

The cultivation of avocados is of great socio-economic importance, as it generates permanent and temporary jobs throughout the entire agricultural and commercial chain, benefiting producers, marketers, service providers, processors, and consumers.

## IV. BOTANICAL ASPECTS

## ROOT

In avocados, the root is pivot, very branched, with a radial distribution, with the majority of the secondary and tertiary rootlets being white (Cummings and Schroeder, 1942).

## STEM

It is a cylindrical, erect, woody trunk, with a rough and sometimes furrowed bark. The crown, with extended branches, is of a globose and rounded shape.

## LEAVES

The upper side of the leaves is green when young (see figure 1a), and turns green when mature (see figure 1b); the underside is green and opaque. The leaves are arranged in a spiral and sprout in clusters, with a lifespan of 10 to 12 months (Avilan et al., 1992).

Fig. 1a) Young leaves.

<!-- image -->

## FLOWER

The flowers are approximately 10 mm in diameter, with radial symmetry, are perfect, have male (stamens) and female (pistils) organs; grouped in a panicle, hermaphrodite, pubescent, with short pedicels. They have a three-lobed calyx and a three-petaled corolla, with 9 stamens (9 functional and 3 staminodes) and two groups of nectaries, the 'staminodal' and the 'true' (Avilan et al., 1992).

## FRUIT

The fruit is a berry that develops from a unicarpelar gynoecium and contains a single seed. The pericarp consists of 3 layers: exocarp (skin), mesocarp (pulp), and endocarp (thin seed coat) (Cummings and Schroeder, 1942). The habit of avocado fruit can be either solitary or in clusters (see figure 2), and its weight varies from 100-3,000 gr (IPGRI, 1995).

## V. GENETIC DIVERSITY

Mesoamerica is considered the origin and center of diversity for the avocado (Persea americana Mill.). There are three varieties of avocado:

## MEXICAN VARIETY

(Persea americana var. drymifolia)

Originating in the highlands of Central Mexico, this variety is the most resistant to cold, tolerating temperatures below 0°C (Avilan et al., 1989); the optimal temperatures for this variety are between 5-17°C.

## GUATEMALAN VARIETY (Persea americana var. guatemalensis)

Originating in the highlands of Guatemala, this variety is adapted to subtropical conditions, with optimal temperatures of 4 to 19°C. It has leaves without an anise scent, which are larger than those of the Mexican variety and are a darker green color (Ibar, 1979).

## ANTILLAN VARIETY

(Persea americana var. Americana)

Originating in the warm, humid lowlands of Central America, where there is a short rainy season (Knight, 2007).

## HYBRIDS

The avocado is a plant with high self-pollination, meaning it has a high degree of cross-pollination, which allows for the natural or artificial production of hybrids. The characteristics of hybrids vary depending on those of their parents, seeking traits for better adaptation to a specific geographic area, commercially viable fruits of medium size, modifying the harvest time, fat content, low-growing trees, resistance to pests and diseases (Ibar, 1979).

## VI. CLIMATIC AND EDAPHIC REQUIREMENTS

## CLIMATE

## WIND

Its frequency and intensity can cause the fall of flowers and fruits, and in some cases, the breakage of entire branches, which can often disrupt the balance of the plant's crown.

## PRECIPITATION

The rainfall requirement is 1,000 to 2,000 millimeters, well distributed throughout the year. However, supplemental irrigation should be provided during the dry season and during periods of drought.

## SOLAR RADIATION

Avocado branches that are too shaded are unproductive, which is why it is important to carry out appropriate pruning practices and to control the density of the plants. Full exposure to sunlight is highly beneficial for the crop, however, the trunk and primary branches are susceptible to sunburn (Bárcenas, 2000).

## SOIL

The avocado can grow in soils of a wide variety of textures, even in clayey and loamy soils, as long as there is good drainage, with light soils being the optimal choice.

## VIII. PROPAGATION

The avocado can be propagated sexually and asexually. However, sexual propagation or by seed is not recommended for commercial plantations, due to the genetic variability, resulting in plants with very diverse characteristics compared to their parents. For commercial plantations, vegetative propagation (asexual) should be used to obtain uniform plants, genetically identical to their mother plant (Rios-Castano, et al., 1977).

## ROOTSTOCK OR SCION

The rootstock and scion must be compatible with the variety being grafted, originating from healthy mother plants with good growth characteristics, excellent adaptation to the local edaphoclimatic conditions, high resilience and resistance to biotic and abiotic adverse factors.

The rootstock seeds should be selected from mature trees that have produced at least two harvests. The fruit should be harvested in the second third of the time after the harvest begins, and in the middle third of the tree's canopy. (Bender and Whiley, 2007).

## NURSERY

Fig. 8 Nursery beds and polyethylene bags.

The seeds for producing rootstocks can be sown in propagation beds or polyethylene bags (see Figure 8). For beds, it is recommended to prepare an area that is 1 meter wide and 20-30 cm deep, with the length depending on the number of plants to be produced. The substrate to be used should be a mixture of sand, clay, and loam in equal proportions, or increase the percentage of sand to favor root development. The beds should be disinfected before planting using techniques such as solarization, using hot water, and applying products such as: Cal+Copper Sulfate in a ratio of 4:0.8 kg/m3, to control insects, nematodes, fungi, and bacteria. To stimulate and accelerate seed germination, it is recommended to remove a portion of the narrowest part of the seed (see Figure 9).

## SEED SOWING

The seed should be placed with the wider end facing down and the pointed end facing up, ensuring that the pointed end is flush with the soil and not buried.

If sowing in beds, the spacing should be 10 centimeters between seeds and 20 centimeters between rows. Germination occurs 30-50 days after sowing, depending on the material used as a rootstock, the moisture level of the nursery, and the climate.

As time passes, the seed loses viability. To prevent this, its storage should not exceed two weeks.

## NURSERY

In some nurseries, the seeds are sown directly in polyethylene bags, which has both advantages and disadvantages.

## SHOOT SELECTION

The shoots of the variety to be propagated must come from productive, healthy trees without diseases or perforations, be physiologically mature,

located on the outer side of the tree where they receive sunlight, and have a thickness equal to that of the rootstock. They should be well formed. Shoots should not be collected during the pre-flowering stage (broccoli head). To ensure a better graft, the shoot should be prepared on the mother tree by cutting off the leaves of the selected shoot and leaving the petioles. This should be done 8 days before grafting (see Figure 10).

## GRAFTING

The most commonly used grafting technique in our country for avocados is the lateral grafting, but currently, terminal grafting or wedge grafting is being recommended.

## IX. ESTABLISHMENT OF THE PLANTATION

## SELECTION OF THE PLANTING AREA

The location where the avocado plantation is to be established is of fundamental importance, as it is a perennial crop with a lifespan of more than 15 years. Therefore, it is necessary to consider aspects such as:

## PREPARATION OF THE SOIL

Manual: On sloping terrain, weed, shrub, and debris control is carried out manually. In addition, depending on the slope percentage, soil conservation works are carried out.

Mechanical: If the terrain allows for the use of agricultural machinery, it should be prepared by a subsoil cultivation to break the soil, followed by a plowing to turn over the surface layer and two harrowing passes to loosen the soil.

## PLANTING SYSTEM AND SPACING

The planting system or layout is the way in which the plants are arranged in the field. Some of the most commonly used systems in avocado cultivation are:

Square: trees are planted at equal distances between plants and rows.

Rectangular: the distance between rows is greater than the distance between plants.

Triangular or hexagonal: trees are planted in equilateral triangles, with all sides equal, this layout allows for 15% more plants per unit area than the square layout. (Godínez et al., 2000).

Table 3. Planting density for avocados.

## PLANTING SEASON

It is recommended to plant in the final location at the beginning of the rainy season (May-June) to achieve good initial growth of the plants; however, it is possible to plant at another time if irrigation is available, although the costs increase.

## STAKING

This is done to correct the growth of trees whose trunk is crooked, to prevent damage from wind, and to ensure upright trunk growth.

## METHODS OF WEED CONTROL

## MANUAL CONTROL

This can be done using tools such as a hoe or machete and a brush cutter, as a complement to the pruning of trees. In areas with sloping terrain, control is carried out using this method.

## MECHANICAL CONTROL

Wide planting distances allow us to use machinery to control weeds in the rows. Since the root system of the avocado is very susceptible to fungi, the use of mechanical cultivators or rotary tillers is recommended for weed control in the rows.

## CULTURAL CONTROL

Mulching or covering: moderate use helps to conserve moisture in the planting area and provides protection against potential pest attacks. It can also be done by spreading material over as much of the area as possible, depending on the availability of material. In sloping terrain, the material should be spread along curves.

## COVER CROPS

Fast-growing crops such as legumes can be used to quickly cover the ground and also provide nutrients through their decomposition.

## CHEMICAL CONTROL

This is recommended in plantations where there is a shortage of labor. Apply systemic or contact herbicides to the rows of the plantation and supplement in the tree's planting area manually.

## XI. FERTILIZATION IN AVOCADO

The goal is to increase the concentration of nutrients in the soil solution, when the plant does not have the amounts it needs for its production.

The best yields and quality are achieved when the necessary amounts of nutrients are applied in a balanced way, at the appropriate time, according to the plant's absorption rate and with the appropriate source of fertilizer.

A nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium (N, P, K) ratio should be established (N = 1:0.5:1). High or low levels of potassium do not favor production, and a high level of phosphorus tends to reduce yields (Avilan et al., 1989).

Balanced fertilization programs, specific to each cultivar, are essential for improving yield and fruit quality. An appropriate avocado fertilization program should include an analysis of the nutrient content in the fruit, soil and leaf analyses to better estimate the fertilization requirements of the plantation.

## XII. MAIN PESTS

## STEM AND BRANCH BORER (Stenoma catenifer Walshingam)

## DAMAGE

This is detected by the food waste expelled through the penetration hole, which remains attached to the fruit's epidermis (see figure 11). In addition to infesting fruits at all stages of development, it also affects branches during vegetative and flowering periods, where it builds longitudinal tunnels that can cause the death of the branch (Manrique et al., 2010).

Fig. 11 Fruits damaged by S. catenifer W.

<!-- image -->

## CONTROL

It is recommended to implement a phytosanitary monitoring program through sampling of fruits, branches, or the presence of adults in 10% of the planted trees per hectare.

## Cultural control:

- Collect infested fruits from both the tree's canopy and the ground and bury them (Carabalí, 2011).
- Apply a layer of organic matter greater than 5 cm over the tree's planting area.

## Biological control:

- Beauveria bassiana (144 gr/ 200 lt Water).
- Bacillus Thuringiensis 6.4 WG dose 15 grs. / 4 gallons of water.

## STEM AND BRANCH BORER (Copturus aguacatae Kissinger)

## DAMAGE

The larva bores into the stem, mature or young branches in different ways, sometimes limiting the attack to the bark when it is thick enough, or digging deep to reach the central cylinder, and when the branch is young and thin. The galleries are covered with the larva's food waste, which has the appearance of sawdust (Bernal et all, 2014).

Its damage can also extend to the fruits. The adult (see figure 12) prefers to lay eggs on the thinner branches, especially at the ends. Branches attacked by this pest dry up, and when the damage progresses, the tree dies (ICA 2012).

Fig. 12 Adult state of Copturus aguacatae K.

<!-- image -->

## CONTROL

## Cultural control:

* Identify symptoms of damage, then scrape the affected areas with white exudates and look for the insect larvae within. Once the pest and its damage are detected, use a disinfected pair of scissors to cut off the branches 40 cm below the last affected area. When the larvae are located, remove them and seal the stem with a paste containing a fungicide (Carabalí, 2011).

* Branches that have been pruned should be removed from the plantation and burned.
* Eradicate the trees when the infestation is severe.
* Paint the trunk and branches with a copper and lime-based paste once a year.

## Biological control:

* Beauveria bassiana (720 gr./ 1000 lt. Water).

## RED SPIDER MITES OR ACARS

( Oligonychus punicae Hirst.)

## DAMAGE

This involves piercing the leaves with their rasping-sucking mouthparts, sucking out the sap, which causes chlorosis, followed by a bronze-brown discoloration and the formation of a corky layer on the leaves, weakening the plant nutritionally, which causes the leaves to dry out. Severely affected foliage can dry out and fall prematurely. When the population is high, defoliation and reduced yields can occur (Bernal et all, 2014).

Fig. 13 Adult Oligonychus punicae.

<!-- image -->

## CONTROL

## Cultural control:

* Monitor the crop during the dry season and periods of drought.
* Control weeds and other hosts.
* Remove suckers at the base and from within.
* Provide balanced nutrition and sufficient watering.
* Use a bio-rational product evaluated by CENTA: 75 gr of flour + 75 gr of salt + 45 gr of detergent for 4 gallons of water.

## Chemical control:

* Botanical insecticides such as: 80 EC Nim extract (170 ml/4 gal water).
* 80% Sulfur WG (200-300 gr/100 lts water).
* Green leaf mites acaricides.

## TRIP (Scirtothrips, Frankliniella and Leptothrips sp)

## DAMAGE

The nymphs and adults (see figure 14) feed on leaves and fruit by perforating the epidermis with their rasping-sucking mouthparts, causing deformities in the leaves, damage to the fruit, and the formation of dark-colored spots caused by their excrement on the leaf surface. In severe cases, the leaves of the trees fall completely (Bernal et all, 2014).

## CONTROL

## Cultural control:

* Keep the plantation free of weeds both inside and outside.
* Remove host plants.

## Biological control:

Apply Bacillus thurigiensis to the foliage.

<!-- image -->

## Chemical control:

* Azadirachtin 40 EC (170 ml/4 gal water).
* Thiamethoxan 25 WG (175-280 gr/mz)

## XIII. MAIN DISEASES

ROOT ROT AND WILT CAUSED BY Phytophthora (Phytophthora cinnamomi var.

cinnamomi Rands)

## SYMPTOMS

Roots: Primarily causes root rot in plants of all ages and develops more rapidly in waterlogged soils. It affects the fine roots, which turn brown-black and eventually die. When examining the secondary roots, they show partial necrosis (Tamayo, 2005).

Foliage: Causes discoloration and wilting of the leaves. In new leaves, they are scarce, smaller than normal, and have a surface that is frequently folded towards the petiole. On the underside, the veins turn purple, and eventually the leaves wither and fall, so that the crowns of the affected trees quickly become bare (see figure 15), although some dry leaves may remain attached to the branches. In nursery plants, the pattern of ascending and descending death.

Fruits: They are smaller and thinner. Excessive flowering and fruit production can occur, but the fruits do not reach their normal size (Tamayo, 2005).

## CONTROL METHODS

## Cultural control:

- Use disinfected seeds, substrates, and soil in nurseries and greenhouses.
- Moderate watering in nurseries and greenhouses.
- Select soils with good external and internal drainage, avoiding clay soils and compacted soils.
- Eradicate diseased plants, remove them from the crop, isolate, and disinfect the area when the disease pressure and environmental factors have caused significant economic losses.
- Avoid causing wounds to the stem and roots.
- Manage weeds properly.
- Create drainage channels on the farm.
- Incorporate organic matter into the soil (planting site); this promotes the growth and multiplication of antagonistic microorganisms to *P. cinnamomi*.

Fig. 15 Tree defoliated by *Phytophthora cinnamomi*.

## Biological control:

- Periodic application in nurseries of mycorrhizal fungi (Micorrizafer) (10 g/bag) and products based on the fungus *Trichoderma sp.* (0.5 g/l) to the soil in the bag.

## Chemical control:

- Foliar sprays: Fosetil Alumium 80 WG (dose 1.5-2 kg/ha or 250-300 gr/100 lts water)
- Planting hole: moistening the walls and submerging the seedling in a solution of: Fosetil Alumium 80 WG, Fosetil Aluminium + Propomocarb 84 SL (dose 1.5 ml/lt water).

## ANTHRONY (Colletotrichum gloeosporioides)

## SYMPTOMS

In nurseries, a downward death of the crown and decay of the graft is observed, which is characterized by being dark brown, black, or reddish.

In the field, anthracnose causes a progressive, downward death of branches and shoots, which exhibit a dark brown to black coloration. In conditions of high relative humidity, this causes wilting, leaf death, and the stem is covered with salmon-colored masses, which correspond to the conidia of the fungus that causes the disease (Mejia, 1999; Tamayo, 2004).

Flowers: dark spots appear on the branches or necrosis at the ends. This causes their fall and lesions in early stages of formation.

Peduncle and fruit: Affects the union of the peduncle with the forming fruits, causing elongated lesions of brown to dark brown coloration that cause their fall in early stages of formation. The pathogen penetrates the epidermis and remains latent until its maturation, when the spots appear on the fruits, causing their premature fall from the tree. (Tamayo, 2005).

Fig. 16 Death of branches due to *Colletotrichum spp.*

## Technical Guide

In post-harvest, damage caused by *C. gloeosporioides* is observed in different parts of the fruit, such as round spots of variable size (0.5 to 3 cm in diameter), of brown or light brown color, slightly depressed in the center and without defined edges (Tamayo, 2004).

Fig. 17 Fruit damaged by *Colletotrichum spp.*

## CONTROL METHODS

## Cultural control:

- Seeds for grafting and buds must come from plants free of the disease.
- Use disinfected seeds, substrates, and soil in nurseries and greenhouses.
- Perform pruning for thinning and phytosanitary purposes.
- Seal wounds made by pruning.
- Eliminate alternate host trees.
- Remove and burn dead wood, leaves, and infected fruit.
- Control insect pests.
- Disinfect storage rooms and boxes used for selling the fruit periodically with products based on Sodium Hypochlorite at 2%.
- Carry out adequate and balanced fertilization at planting, especially with potassium and calcium.
- Disinfect tools used for any type of pruning and when moving from one tree to another.
- Disinfect equipment used in packing houses (harvesting boxes, etc.) periodically with products based on Sodium Hypochlorite at 2%.

## Chemical control:

- Contact fungicides based on: Copper oxychloride or Copper Hydroxide (2 g/l); chlorothalonil 50 SC (0.75-1.0 lt/200 lt water); folpet 80 WP (dose 2004000 gr/200 lt water).

- Systemic fungicides: carbendazim 50 SC (0.5 lt/mz); azosulfuron 50 WP (dose 100 gr/200 lt water); thiamethiram 50 SC (0.35 to 0.75 kg/mz or 8-10 gr / gal).

## SCAB (Sphaceloma perseae Jenk)

## SYMPTOMS

On the fruit, round or irregular lesions of brown or light brown color, with a corky appearance, protruding, are observed, which can merge and affect a large part of the fruit. With the cracking of the affected areas (see figure 18), the entry of other organisms is favored, affecting its commercial value.

Leaves: young leaves and tender shoots show tiny lesions (1 to 2 mm in diameter) of dark brown color, surrounded by a slight chlorotic halo. In conditions of continuous rain, the lesions and the chlorotic halo increase in size (3 to 5 mm in diameter), take on a brownish-light color, acquire various shapes and grow until they cover lateral parts of the leaf, giving the leaf blade a corky and wrinkled appearance (Ploetz et al., 1994). In severe attacks, the shoots and leaves necrosate, turn upwards and can die.

## CONTROL METHODS

## Cultural control:

- Removal and burning of dead wood, leaves, and infected fruit
- Pruning to improve air circulation and control pests
- Monitoring and timely control of trips populations
- Installing windbreaks to prevent mechanical damage to fruit and branches.

## Chemical control:

- Systemic fungicides: carbendazim 50 SC (0.5 l/ha), Azositrubin 50 WP, Tiofanate Methyl 50 SC (0.35 to 0.75 kg/ha or 8-10 g/gallon)
- Contact fungicides based on:
- Copper oxychloride or Copper Hydroxide (2 g/l)
- Chlorotalonil 50 SC (0.75-1.0 l/200 l water)
- Folpet 80 WP (200-4000 g/200 l water)
- Difenoconazole 250 EC (0.5 to 1 cc/l)
- Benomil 50 WP (0.5 g/l)

## XIV. HARVESTING

Harvesting is the final field stage of avocado cultivation and the beginning of a new phase for its commercialization. This involves planning, organizing, executing, and supervising all the activities necessary to collect and place the fruit on the market.

## RECOMMENDATIONS FOR HARVESTING

- Do not apply any type of systemic pesticides one month before harvest.
- Disinfect all tools and equipment used in the harvest.
- Do not damage the fruit.
- Separate the harvested fruit from the ground, damaged fruit, and fruit harvested from the tree.
- Cut with a portion of the peduncle between 3-4 mm to prevent rubbing and bruising between fruits.
- Use ladders and sharp pruning shears or other implements, avoiding dropping the fruit.
- Carefully empty and place the fruit in plastic crates at the minimum height, taking care to protect the loaded fruit in the collection bag.
- Use 70% of the capacity of the plastic crate and use clean material at the bottom to prevent damage from impacts and overfilling.
- Collect in the early morning hours to avoid stress and dehydration.
- Once the fruit is collected in plastic crates, they must be protected from sunlight and rain, placing them in the shade under trees and with tarpaulins.
- Transport the freshly harvested fruit to the packing plant or collection center.
