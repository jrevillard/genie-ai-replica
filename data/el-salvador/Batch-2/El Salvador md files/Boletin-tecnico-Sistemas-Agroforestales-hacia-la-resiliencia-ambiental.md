## v. CONSIDERATIONS

Traditional agroforestry systems have been an alternative, simulating the natural conditions of a balanced environment, and currently represent a prominent sustainable option in the face of climate change. Economically, these systems allow for the generation of diverse and sequential income streams. These indigenous systems are part of the ancestral forestry management culture, allowing, based on technical and empirical knowledge, the modeling of the system to be implemented, involving forest, crop, and animal components.

## I. INTRODUCTION

The country's economy is closely linked to the conservation of natural resources, and these, to the variability of climate change. In the country, droughts, floods, torrential rains, landslides, and other phenomena associated with climate change are frequent; these have direct consequences on the availability and access to food, leading to crop losses, destruction of agricultural facilities, soil degradation and water sources, as well as loss of seeds, which puts food security at risk.

Agriculture in the region must face the challenges of climate change while also ensuring sustainable agricultural production to meet the growing demand for food from a rapidly growing population. Future projections indicate an increase in average temperatures and a decrease in precipitation. By the year 2100, some scenarios project an increase in temperature between 2°C and 5°C, and a decrease in precipitation between 18% and 40%. CEPAL 2010.

Studies and the practices of farmers have demonstrated the environmental benefits of agroforestry systems for climate change resilience, considering them an adaptation strategy.

## II. WHAT ARE AGROFORESTRY SYSTEMS?

Agroforestry is an interdisciplinary area that includes interactions between trees, people, crops, and animals, managing a series of land use systems and technologies in which integrated production is possible, demonstrating a mutual ecological influence. Its different components interact biologically, both in fragile and stable ecosystems, depending on time and space on the same land area, either simultaneously or sequentially.

## III. ENVIRONMENTAL BENEFITS OF AGROFORESTRY SYSTEMS

Among the most outstanding benefits are:

- Water, soil, and fertility conservation
- Provision of food for humans and animals
- Improvement of the microclimate
- Reduction of solar radiation and temperature
- Protection of crops, animals, and humans
- Weed control through shade and cover
- Environmental and ecological services: carbon and nitrogen fixation, oxygen provision,
- Cleaning of contaminated atmospheres, conservation of biodiversity (native species, migratory birds, habitat, landscaping, ecotourism, etc.)

## Indigenous agroforestry systems

The field experience has shown that the most widely adopted agroforestry systems by farmers are the following:

## a. Trees along property boundaries.

This is a line of trees or shrubs that delimit a property or area. Depending on the development of the species, they can be established at 1.50 to 2.0 m. The most commonly used species are Madre cacao (Gliricidia) and Pito (Erythrina sp).

These can be used to delimit the property, obtain firewood, improve soil, and protect crops.

<!-- image -->

## b. Isolated trees.

In this system, the distances and species are chosen to avoid affecting food crops. The species are typically native and naturally regenerating, such as laurel (Cordia alliodora), or other native species.

The spacing would be between 10 and 12 meters, using species with a reduced crown, in order to allow sunlight to penetrate the system.

## c. Thicket.

These are small areas planted with commercially valuable species, spaced 3x3 meters apart. In the first few years, they can be interplanted with crops such as: (Tectona grandis), mahogany (Swietenia humilis), white cedar (Tabebuia donnel smithii), pine (Pinus sp), and others.

The purpose is to obtain timber and by-products such as firewood.
