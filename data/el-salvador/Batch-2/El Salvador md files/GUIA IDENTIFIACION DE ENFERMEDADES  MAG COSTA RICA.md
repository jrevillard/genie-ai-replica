## Bean Cultivation

Beans are one of the staple foods in the Costa Rican diet, not only as a source of plant protein and iron, but also as a source of fiber, folic acid, thiamine, potassium, magnesium, and zinc. These compounds are vital in preventing and treating cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, obesity, constipation, and colon cancer, among the most common. Consuming foods high in iron reduces anemia in children.

Despite the fact that 98% of the population in Costa Rica consumes beans, consumption varies between rural and urban areas. It is estimated that the average Costa Rican consumes about 10 kg of beans per person per year.

## Recommendations for using this guide

This guide is intended to be a permanent resource for farmers in the field. It quickly describes the main characteristics of the most common diseases that affect beans in Costa Rica. To accurately diagnose a disease, it is necessary to walk through the field and compare the affected plants with the symptoms described in the guide.

If what you observe in the field does not match what is described in the guide, it is recommended to collect samples of the affected plants and send them as soon as possible to the Ministry of Agriculture and Livestock office nearest to you, or to the pathology laboratories of universities or the National Institute of Innovation and Transfer in Agricultural Technology.

Samples should be wrapped in damp newspaper and placed in a closed plastic bag. A label with the following information should also be included: farmer's name, location, date of collection, and distribution of the disease in the field. This label should also be placed in the bag.

## The origin of diseases

Diseases do not always have a single cause. The symptoms observed in the field are the result of the interaction of climatic conditions, the production system, the variety used, and the presence of previous crop residues contaminated with diseases.

Variations in temperature, humidity, and soil conditions can accelerate the development of a disease. The level or magnitude of each of these factors can favor the development of different diseases. The production system also influences the presence of diseases, through the effects of different forms of land preparation, planting density, and agronomic management. As for the variety used, it is important to remember that bean varieties have different levels of resistance to diseases, adaptation to the site, growth habit, and yield.

One of the causes of diseases that has received the most emphasis is infection by microorganisms. This includes fungi, bacteria, nematodes, and viruses, but there is no characteristic symptom for each of them. For this reason, accurate diagnosis of the disease requires the collection of sufficient sick material at different stages of infection.

The progression of the disease is closely related to the type of pathogen, the susceptibility of the variety, and the prevailing environmental conditions in the area. In the early stages of plant growth, it is common to observe yellowing or stunted plants due to root damage. These diseases do not progress rapidly, and in many cases, the plants do not die. But in more advanced stages of plant development, a disease can develop rapidly because the pathogen is spread by wind, which favors its almost uniform attack in the plot. In cases of diseases that progress by rain splash, their development is slower and they are observed in patches.

The early appearance of diseases is closely related to the survival of the organism in crop residues, in the soil, or in the seed.

## Root Diseases

## Causative Agents

Several species of fungi that normally inhabit the soil are responsible for causing root rot in beans, including: Fusarium, Rhizoctonia, Pythium, and Sclerotium.

## Symptoms

Root rot is common, and its severity varies from region to region depending on prevailing environmental conditions and the agricultural history of the field.

Root rot appears from the first few weeks of plant growth, and is typically found in the field in patches of different sizes.

In these areas, small and weak plants are observed, with leaves that are smaller and yellowish (Fig. 1). In some cases, yellowing also appears on adult plants, on the lower leaves (Fig. 2). Complete or partial wilting of the plant may be observed.

The symptoms in roots vary slightly depending on the fungus that causes them. Generally, one can observe strangulation, disintegration, or sinking of the root, which is light brown or reddish-brown, near the soil level.

## Dispersal

The spread of the disease in the field is slow, but can progress rapidly if there are continuous bean plantings in the same area. The main way that the fungi that attack roots spread is through rain splash, runoff water, or movement of contaminated soil, especially when preparing the soil for planting. The use of infected seed can also be a means of spreading these fungi.

## Control

Control of root rot is achieved through agricultural practices or the application of chemicals. In the first case, crop rotation (maize, rice, cassava, grasses, tiquisque) is recommended, as well as planting in well-drained soils or avoiding waterlogging by building drainage systems, using minimal tillage, using high-quality seed, and, if available, planting resistant varieties.

Chemical control of root diseases should focus on seed treatment. Some recommended products include: Benomil, Carboxim + Captan, Chloroneb, PCNB, and TCMTB.

## Foliage Diseases

## ANTRACNOSIS

## Causative Agent

Anthracnosis of beans is caused by the fungus Colletotrichum lindemuthianum.

## Symptoms

Anthracnosis of beans attacks from the time the plant emerges until it reaches production, potentially affecting the seed. On the foliage, the symptoms initially appear on the lower part of the leaf, as small, dark reddish lesions, located along the veins.

Over time, these lesions turn dark brown to black (Fig. 3). In pods, there are round, sunken lesions with a well-defined edge and a dark center.

In rainy periods, with the presence of fog or low temperatures, small pinkish spots can be observed in the dark center (Fig. 4).

## Dispersal

Infected seed is the main means of dispersal of anthracnosis. Seed exchange, without quality control, favors the contamination of areas free of the disease, or spreads the disease to new production areas. When anthracnosis is already in the field, it spreads from one plant to another through rain splash, and also through the contact of wet plants with people, animals, or tools that are contaminated with the fungus.

## Control

The main measure to control anthracnosis is to use seed produced according to strict quality standards. It is not recommended to plant seed intended for consumption or trade. Since this fungus does not attack other crops, crop rotation is an effective practice. It is recommended to plant improved commercial varieties, as these have intermediate levels of resistance.

## ANGULAR SPOT

## Causative Agent

Angular spot is caused by the fungus Phaeoisariopsis griseola.

## Symptoms

Angular spot attacks the foliage, pods, and beans. On the leaves, the disease starts as small, bright gray spots, which increase in size and take the shape of the veins (Fig. 5).

In a more advanced stage, the spot turns dark brown. On the underside of the leaf, small, rod-like structures of dark gray color can be observed (Fig. 6).

Generally, the disease appears around the fourth week after planting. Severe attacks cause yellowing and leaf drop, which reduces yield when it occurs early in the growing cycle.

## Spread

The fungus that causes angular spot is easily spread by wind. Seeds can also be a means of spread.

## Control

Control of angular spot is achieved through cultivation practices that delay the onset and development of the disease.

It is recommended to use healthy seeds from improved varieties and crop rotation, which allows infected crop residues to decompose. Using resistant varieties is important to keep the level of disease incidence low.

## DOWNY MILDEW OR MILDEW

## Causative agent

Downy mildew, also known as mildew due to its appearance, is caused by the fungus *Thanatephorus cucumeris*.

## Symptoms

The initial symptoms of downy mildew are characterized by small, irregular, light green, watery-looking spots with a dark center (Fig. 7).

If high temperatures and rain occur, these spots grow and then merge together, resulting in a large lesion on the leaf, and joining with other leaves, giving the appearance of a web (Fig. 8).

In some regions, the fungus has the ability to produce a variant that causes slightly different initial symptoms. In these conditions, the disease begins as a small, dark, round spot with a yellowish border, a symptom known as "eye spot" (Fig. 9). However, these spots later grow and are observed as described in the previous paragraph.

On pods, the spots are dark, watery-looking, irregularly shaped, and can cover the entire pod (Fig. 10). A cottony growth can be observed on the spots under high temperature and humidity conditions. In these cases, the seeds become deformed and discolored.

When environmental conditions are favorable to the fungus, the mildew develops rapidly, destroying a crop in a few days.

## Spread

The fungus has a wide ability to survive in the soil, from which it spreads to plants through rainfall splash. It can also be spread between plants by rainwater that runs between the leaves.

Seeds are also an important means of spreading downy mildew.

## Control

To reduce the damage caused by mildew, cultivation practices should be implemented to reduce water splash and runoff. For example, planting with minimal tillage, using mulches, improving drainage in areas prone to flooding, crop rotation, and using quality seeds.

## FALSE ANGULAR SPOT

## Causative agent

False angular spot of beans is caused by the nematode *Aphelenchoides besseyi*. This is one of the few nematodes that affect the aerial parts of the plant, not just beans, but it has also been observed in other crops such as rice, strawberries, grasses, and some weeds.

## Symptoms

The initial symptoms of this disease are very similar to those described for angular spot, caused by a fungus. For this reason, it is known as false angular spot.

The disease begins as small, angular, dark spots, distributed on the leaf (Fig. 11). As it progresses, these spots merge and concentrate towards the base of the leaf, which is what characterizes and differentiates it completely from angular spot (Fig. 12).

Figure 11. Initial symptoms of false angular spot.

**FALSE MOLD (Angular Spot)**

## Symptoms

The main symptoms of false angular spot are small, circular, yellowish spots on the leaves. These spots may become brown or black and have a slightly raised edge. The disease can also affect the stems and pods, causing them to become distorted or stunted.

## Spread

False angular spot is spread by wind and water. It can also be spread by contaminated seeds or tools.

## Control

The best way to control false angular spot is to avoid planting susceptible varieties. If you must plant susceptible varieties, take steps to prevent the spread of the disease, such as using resistant varieties, practicing crop rotation, and avoiding overhead irrigation.

**ENTYLOMA LEAF SPOT**

## Cause

This leaf spot is caused by the fungus *Entyloma petuniae*.

## Symptoms

The symptoms of *Entyloma* leaf spot are small, circular, brown spots on the leaves. These spots may be surrounded by a yellow halo. The disease can also cause the leaves to turn yellow and drop off.

## Spread

*Entyloma* is spread by wind and water. It can also be spread by contaminated seeds or tools.

## Control

The best way to control *Entyloma* is to avoid planting susceptible varieties. If you must plant susceptible varieties, take steps to prevent the spread of the disease, such as using resistant varieties, practicing crop rotation, and avoiding overhead irrigation.

**BEAN RUST**

## Cause

Bean rust is caused by the fungus *Uromyces phaseoli*.

## Symptoms

The symptoms of bean rust are small, reddish-brown spots on the leaves. These spots may be surrounded by a yellow halo. The disease can also cause the leaves to turn yellow and drop off. In severe cases, bean rust can cause the entire plant to die.

## Spread

Bean rust is spread by wind and water. It can also be spread by contaminated seeds or tools.

## Control

The best way to control bean rust is to avoid planting susceptible varieties. If you must plant susceptible varieties, take steps to prevent the spread of the disease, such as using resistant varieties, practicing crop rotation, and avoiding overhead irrigation.

**Bacterial Leaf Spot**

## Cause

Bacterial leaf spot is caused by the bacterium *Xanthomonas axonopodis*.

## Symptoms

The symptoms of bacterial leaf spot are small, water-soaked spots on the leaves. These spots may become brown or black and have a slightly raised edge. The disease can also cause the leaves to turn yellow and drop off. In severe cases, bacterial leaf spot can cause the entire plant to die.

## Spread

Bacterial leaf spot is spread by contaminated seeds or tools. It can also be spread by wind and water.

## Control

The best way to control bacterial leaf spot is to avoid planting susceptible varieties. If you must plant susceptible varieties, take steps to prevent the spread of the disease, such as using resistant varieties, practicing crop rotation, and avoiding overhead irrigation.

## Dissemination

In the field, the disease primarily spreads through rain splash, insects, and the rubbing of wet leaves. The movement of people or animals between plants also facilitates the spread of the bacteria to other plants. Over long distances, seeds are the main means of dissemination.

## Control

In this case, the use of healthy seeds and crop rotation are crucial. Many commercial varieties already possess intermediate resistance to the disease.

## Viral Diseases

## Causative Agent

Viral diseases are caused by a series of viruses with a wide geographical distribution.

## Symptoms

The symptoms caused by viruses are varied and can easily be confused with other alterations such as nutritional deficiencies, toxicities, or environmental effects. The most common symptoms are: changes in the greenness of the leaves, ranging from light green to yellow, often forming mosaics; stunted growth; deformed leaves; more prominent veins; a shortened or lengthened plant life cycle; deformed pods with a lower number of grains, or no grains at all (Fig. 17).

## Dissemination

These viral diseases can be transmitted by insects (aphids, whiteflies, thrips, or mites). Depending on the transmitting insect, the distribution of affected plants can be uniform or limited to patches in the field. This distribution of diseased plants differentiates the viral attack from other factors that produce similar symptoms, because its distribution in the field is more homogeneous.

Figure 17. Symptoms of common bean mosaic virus (BCMV).

<!-- image -->

## Control

The main practice for controlling these diseases is the use of improved varieties with resistance to the viruses.

Another option is to reduce the population of vector insects by using insecticides (Table 2).

## Stunting

## Causative Agent

High concentrations of the bean common mosaic virus (CCMV) have been detected in the diseased plants analyzed.

## Symptoms

Stunting is characterized by plants with a darker green color than healthy plants, with abnormally long veins, and with varying degrees of leaf deformation (Fig. 18 A, B). The production of pods is either zero or very reduced, and these tend to be deformed.

In the field, this disease and angular leaf spot often occur on the same plant.

## Dissemination

This virus is transmitted by several species of thrips. In the field, the disease is found distributed in foci or patches of different sizes. No transmission by seed has been detected.

## Control

The strategy focuses on controlling the transmitting insects and the weeds that can host the virus. In case of rotation with corn, it is recommended to treat the corn seed to reduce the population of thrips present in the following bean cycle.

## Considerations for the use of fungicides and insecticides

1. Chemicals must be stored in appropriate conditions, locked, well-ventilated, and out of the reach of children and domestic animals.
2. Always read the instructions on the label carefully to avoid improper use.
3. Always adhere to the recommended doses and mixtures.
4. Ensure that the application equipment is in good condition, as spills can cause poisoning.
5. Always use protective equipment (mask, goggles, apron, gloves, and boots) to reduce direct contact of the product with the skin.
6. Be careful not to contaminate water sources, wells, or areas where animals typically drink water.
7. In case of poisoning, seek medical help immediately, and provide the doctor with the label or packaging.
