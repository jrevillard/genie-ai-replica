## Introduction

The jocote, *Spondias*, belongs to the Anacardiaceae family. It is a species native to Central America and Mexico, and is found throughout the Caribbean and tropical America. The majority of producers consider the cultivation of jocote to be a valuable asset for many families in rural areas, who have been cultivating it for generations, gaining knowledge of its benefits and problems without the application of technology that would allow for better agronomic management, from soil preparation to selecting seedlings, planting, fertilization, pest and disease control, harvesting, and marketing. However, some producers who carry out certain agricultural practices have been able to obtain better economic income, considering it highly profitable and also as an alternative to other crops that do not adapt to the agro-climatic conditions of jocote.

In El Salvador, it is estimated that there are between 700 and 800 hectares of jocote cultivated. In the municipalities of Ahuachapán and San Lorenzo in the Ahuachapán department, there are approximately 420 hectares of acid jocote in the hands of 850 producers. Also in the irrigation district of San Vicente and in San Miguel, jocote is cultivated. In the canton El Jocote of San Matías, La Libertad, there are cultivated areas of sugar jocote.

The National Center for Agricultural and Forestry Technology (CENTA) established a collection of jocote varieties in the San Andrés Agricultural Development Center in 1999. The objective of this collection was the conservation, characterization, propagation, and exchange of germplasm. In addition to fulfilling the objectives mentioned above, this collection is also used to support research work, to increase the quantity, and as a means of teaching for students and producers interested in cultivating jocote.

The information presented in this document corresponds to the characterization of the different jocote varieties. Regardless of the variety you wish to establish, agronomic management is of vital importance to have a more technologically advanced crop, which in turn will allow for better harvests (quality fruit) and therefore, better economic income for the producer.

## Main jocote varieties

There are approximately nine jocote varieties known in El Salvador (acid or red baron, corona, sugar jocote, pitarrillo, chapín, winter jocote, tronador, guaturca, and iguana). Of these, only the first three are of the greatest economic importance for the majority of producers in rural areas. They are considered to be of greater importance, as they are the most sought after by consumers as fresh fruit.

Among the different jocote varieties harvested during the summer season, there are: acid or red baron, sugar jocote, yellow pitarrillo, tronador, guaturca, and iguana; and those harvested in winter are: corona, chapín, and winter or water jocote.

## ACID JOCOTE OR RED BARON

Well accepted in the local market. Its morphological characteristics are: fruit weight of 21g, sweet and sour taste, depending on the degree of ripeness, red skin in maturity and green in unripe state, shape: round to flattened, size: medium to large, consumed as fresh fruit and in juice. Its nutrient content is: 0.72% protein, 0.15% fat, 0.51% ash, and 0.43% crude fiber. Harvest season: from February to March. Adapts to altitudes from 50 to 700 msnm.

<!-- image -->

## CORONA JOCOTE

Has the best price per unit in the local market due to being a particularly large and fleshy fruit. It is very common to find it in coffee plantations or in other crops. Its morphological characteristics are: average fruit weight of 30 g, very sweet taste, red skin with bright green and yellow tones, large seed and little pulp, consumed as fresh fruit, harvest season from July, August and September. Adapts between 850 and 1500 msnm.

<!-- image -->

## SUGAR JOCOTE

Has good acceptance in the local market, and in certain areas, it achieves a better price than acid jocote. The morphological and bromatological characteristics of the fruit describe it as a fruit with an average weight of 16 g, sweet and astringent taste, green skin, round to flattened shape, small fruit size. It is preferably consumed in unripe state. Its nutrient content is: 0.7% protein, 0.14% fat, 0.74% ash, and 3.7% crude fiber. Harvest season: from February to March. Adapts to altitudes from 50 to 700 msnm.

## OTHER JOCOTE VARIETIES

As mentioned above, there are other varieties of lesser importance, for their commercialization and areas of cultivation. Of these, there is no economic data. Although it is true that in some localities they contribute somewhat to the economic well-being of rural families.

These are varieties of regular acceptance among consumers as fresh fruit, except for the iguana jocote, whose main utility (of the tree) is for building fences. Among these varieties there are:

## YELLOW PITARRILLO

Has little commercial value. It is characterized by having an average fruit weight of 18 g, sweet taste, yellow skin, cylindrical fruit shape, small fruit size, not very consistent in transport, consumed only as fresh fruit. Its nutrient content is: 0.84% protein, 0.22% fat, 0.49% ash, and 0.63% crude fiber. Harvest season: from February to April. Adapts to altitudes from 50 to 700 msnm.

<!-- image -->

## CHAPÍN JOCOTE

Has little commercial value. It is consumed in ripe state. It is characterized by having an average fruit weight of 27 g, sour taste, yellow-green skin, oval shape, large size, good consistency in transport. Harvest season from July to September. Adapts to altitudes from 50 to 800 msnm.

## Winter or Water Melon

This fruit has commercial value and is characterized by an average fruit weight of 13 g, a sour fruit flavor, an oval shape, and a medium size. It is consumed in both green and ripe states. The harvest season is from July to September. It adapts to altitudes from 50 to 700 m above sea level.

<!-- image -->

## Thunder Melon

This fruit is characterized by a small size, an elongated shape, a rind with green and purple hues, a sweet and crunchy taste when consumed. It adapts to altitudes between 50 and 700 m above sea level. The harvest season is from February to April.

<!-- image -->

## Guaturcha Melon

This melon is found in the Chinamas area, Ahuachapán, and Atiquizaya, in isolated areas between cultivated areas of sour melon. It is characterized by a medium to large fruit, yellow in color, and sweet in taste. It adapts to altitudes from 50 to 700 m above sea level. It is harvested from February to April.

<!-- image -->

## Iguana Melon

This melon is found in the wild and is frequently used as a post in fences. It has no commercial value. It is characterized by an average fruit weight of 8.4 g, a very sour taste, a red rind, an elongated shape, and a small size. The harvest season is from February to April. It is found at altitudes between 50 and 700 m above sea level.

## Agronomic Management of Melon Cultivation

Any commercial orchard requires a layout, as well as the implementation of certain agronomic practices to ensure stable, profitable, and high-quality production. In this sense, although the crop has been considered wild or rustic, it requires agronomic management to improve production.

## CLIMATIC AND EDAPHIC REQUIREMENTS

The melon has been considered a wild or rustic crop, it is found in shallow soils with low organic matter and nutrient content. As for the environmental conditions, some varieties adapt better to altitudes between 10 and 800 m above sea level, and temperatures between 30 and 37 °C, while others adapt to altitudes between 800 and 1700 m above sea level.

## SELECTION OF PLANT MATERIAL

The traditional method of propagating melon is asexual or vegetative, such as branches with two or three lateral sides or also cuttings of approximately 1.0 to 1.5 m long by 0.10 to 0.12 m in diameter. The selected material for planting should be branches in a mature state and from productive trees, to harvest in less time.

## PLANTING

It is recommended to plant preferably in April.

The soil preparation should begin from the second week of March. The activities involved include: weed removal, staking, and hoeing; in addition, if there is straw, it should be incorporated to provide protection and nutrients to the soil. As for hoeing, a hole of 0.30 to 0.40 m deep, with a diameter slightly larger than the cutting, is recommended.

With the aim of giving a better layout to the plantation that will be established, it is necessary to consider the topography of the land, to trace the staking and hoeing.

The planting system can be in a square or three-row pattern, and the spacing of the plants can be 6 x 6 m, 5 x 5 m or 4 x 4 m. In the first years, melon cultivation can be grown in conjunction with legumes, cucurbits, and basic grains.

## FERTILIZATION

Melon, like any crop, requires a certain amount of nutrients that are directly related to the development of the crop, yield, and quality of the harvest. The amount of fertilizer will depend on the age and development of the plant, however, it is recommended to carry out a soil analysis before fertilizing, which determines the amount and type of nutrients that the soil needs. If you do not have a soil analysis, it is recommended to fertilize according to the program in Table 1. Foliar fertilization is also recommended to correct minor element deficiencies in the foliage and to improve the quality of the fruit.

## PRUNING

The practice of pruning the melon tree is not carried out by producers, as pruning a branch means economic losses. However, by pruning long branches or diseased branches, it is possible to stimulate the buds, which favors the tree to develop new branches, foliage, and increased flowering. Pruning in fruit trees is a practice that is very important in the life cycle and productivity of the tree.

## PEST CONTROL

## Weeds

It is very important that the melon crop is free of weeds, as this benefits the soil by increasing its temperature, which benefits the development of the tree and achieves a better harvest. It can be done manually, mechanically, or by applying a chemical product.

## Insects

In the melon crop, as in any crop, insects appear that are harmful to the good development and affect both the foliage and the fruit. Among the most known are the fruit fly (Anastrepha sp; Ceratitis capitata), the stem borer (Lagocheirus sp), aphids, and trips. For their control, it is recommended to take into account some cultural practices of the crop, such as: weed removal, appropriate fertilization, pruning practices, and others.

Anastrepha (fruit fly) is a fly that belongs to the order Diptera, and to the family Tephritidae. Its wings are translucent and have brown spots, which helps it to camouflage itself in the same bark of the tree. The damage is caused by the larva when it feeds on the central part of the stem, creating galleries, which interrupt the passage of nutrients, and the tree tends to decrease its production and die.

## Control:

- Removing dead trees (burned). It is also important to remove the damaged trees and bury them from the property, to eliminate all larvae that are inside the trunk, completing their biological cycle.
- Applying lime to the base of the trees to reduce insect oviposition.

Costs: The damage is caused by the insects when they scrape the fruit skin, when it is still forming. This scraping causes deformation of the fruit. Consequently, it reduces its quality and affects the selling price.

## Control:

Abamectin, Amitraz, at the doses recommended by the manufacturers. Applications should be carried out at the beginning of flowering, when the fruit is small, and after the harvest, as the time for fruit formation and the harvest period is very short.

Anthracnose (Colletotrichum gloeosporioides). This is a disease that affects the fruit, causing black spots that quickly cover it, causing it to fall.

## Control:

Using products such as Propineb 70 wp, at a dose of 20 to 30 grams per gallon of water (1.4 to 2.8 kg/ha); Carbendazim 500, at a dose of 4 to 8 cc per gallon of water, Copper oxychloride + Copper sulfate, at a dose of 20 to 30 grams per gallon of water.

## Harvesting

To prevent the fruit from being damaged during harvesting, it is recommended to use a harvesting tool. The maturity of the fruit, according to the buyer's preference, can be harvested at the peak of ripeness or when it starts to change color from green to red or yellow.

A harvesting tool is a tool made with a 2 to 3 meter long pole and a plastic (or fabric) container attached to the top. It prevents the fruit from being damaged when it falls to the ground, achieving a product of better quality and presentation, which allows it to be sold at a better market price.

## Uses of Jocote Fruit in the Food Industry

Jocote can be industrialized as a natural pulp, concentrated pulp; in addition, jams, jellies, jams and other beverages derived from mixtures with other tropical fruits can be made.

There are other varieties such as Jocote de azucarón, whose main use is as a fresh fruit. Jocote de corona, recognized as a table fruit, for its excellent size, flavor and color, is consumed preferably fresh, the pulp in the preparation of beverages, smoothies and desserts.

## Marketing

Jocote is marketed in the local market. The economic potential obtained by the producer is more profitable than other crops. Marketing Jocote in the early days can achieve better prices, decreasing its value as its presence in the market increases. Jocote de corona has a better price than Jocote de azucarón and Jocote ácido. In certain areas, Jocote de azucarón has a better price than Jocote ácido.
