## I. ORIGIN AND DISTRIBUTION

The origin of bananas is the southwestern part of Asia. It is believed that the Balbisiana genome originated on the eastern coast of India, and the Acuminata genome originated on the eastern coast of what is now Malaysia, Thailand, and Myanmar.

The ecological conditions where bananas originated were under the undergrowth, so this species is considered to be shade-tolerant, meaning it needs some shade for normal development. For this reason, the banana plant does not completely close its stomata during the hottest hours of the day and must retract its two pseudostems to avoid dehydration.

In 1516, Europeans introduced bananas to the Americas and the Caribbean. Today, it is a widely cultivated crop due to its adaptability, both in the tropics and subtropics. However, the largest commercial plantations are found in the humid tropics. African countries such as Uganda and Rwanda are the largest producers of bananas in the world (Figure 1).

## PROPERTIES OF BANANA FRUIT

The male banana shares certain similarities with the common banana in terms of minerals and vitamins. The main difference is in the type of carbohydrates. It is low in fat and protein. It contains 122 kcal per 100g. Its most notable properties are:

## COMPLEX CARBOHYDRATES:

in the form of starch, unlike the yellow banana, which is mainly glucose and fructose (that's why it's sweeter).

## FIBER:

contains both soluble and insoluble fiber. In the case of soluble fiber, it contains resistant starch type 2. Once cooked, most of the starch becomes digestible as a result of gelatinization. If stored in the refrigerator, the starch retrogrades and becomes resistant starch type 3, a food that is beneficial for the microbiota (a prebiotic effect), as with cooked and chilled potatoes.

## MINERALS:

is one of the most potassium-rich foods, which is important for restoring the balance between sodium and potassium and preventing high blood pressure. It also contains a good amount of magnesium, which regulates bone formation, helps with the proper functioning of the heart and muscles.

## VITAMINS:

is rich in vitamin C, an antioxidant that combats free radicals and protects against aging and helps repair tissues. It also contains vitamin A, an antioxidant that protects the skin and eyesight, and vitamin B6, which is involved in the nervous system and immunity.

## IV. BOTANY OF THE PLANT

## PLANT

It is a giant herbaceous plant, the true stem is a subterranean storage organ called a rhizome or corm, and the apparent stem is a pseudostem, which is the result of the union of the leaf sheaths. It can reach a height of 3 to 6 meters (Figure 2).

## ROOTS

They are superficial and distributed in a layer of 30-40 cm, with the majority concentrated at 15-20 cm. They are white and tender when emerging, but become hard and yellowish as they mature. They can grow laterally up to 3 m and to a depth of 1.5 m. The root's penetration power is weak, so the root distribution is related to the texture and structure of the soil.

## LEAVES

Very large and arranged in a spiral, 2-4 m long and up to 0.50 m wide, with a petiole of 1 m or more in length and an elliptical blade, slightly decurrent towards the petiole, slightly wavy and glabrous.

<!-- image -->

## STEM

The true stem is a large, starchy, subterranean rhizome, with buds; these develop once the plant has flowered and fruited.

As each rhizome section reaches maturity, its terminal bud becomes an inflorescence when pushed upwards from within the soil by the elongation of the stem, until it emerges above the pseudostem.

## FLOWERS

The flowers are yellowish, irregular, and have 5 fertile stamens and one sterile stamen.

## FRUIT

The fruit is a long, three- or six-sided stalk, with a degree of curvature and length that varies depending on the variety. It develops from the ovary of a pistillate flower. The small spots that are observed when the fruit is opened are the aborted ovules, which turn black.

## ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS

The banana plant is a herbaceous plant, which consists of three phases: Vegetative, Flowering, and Fruiting (Figure 3).

## VEGETATIVE PHASE

This phase lasts for 6 months and is where the formation of main and secondary roots begins, as well as the development of the pseudostem and suckers (Figure 4).

It occurs from the emergence of roots to 6 months after, forming main and secondary roots, reaching up to 4 meters in length horizontally. The main roots branch into secondary roots and produce absorbent hairs, which are located between 20-25 cm from the base of the plant to a depth of 10-15 cm.

## FLOWERING PHASE

This phase lasts approximately 3 months, starting from the 6th month of the vegetative phase. The flowering stem rises from the corm through the pseudostem and is visible until the appearance of the inflorescence, at which point 10-12 leaves are missing (Figure 5).

## FRUITING PHASE

This phase lasts approximately 3 months and occurs after the flowering phase. In this phase, the male and female flowers (fingers) are differentiated. There is a gradual decrease in leaf area and it ends with the harvest. The time from the start of flowering to the harvest of the bunch is 81 to 90 days; in this phase, adverse factors only affect the size of the fruits, while the quantity of fruits was given in the previous two phases (Figure 6). ## V. ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS

Among the environmental factors that influence the commercial production of bananas are: temperature, water, light, wind, and soil.

## ALTITUDE

The ideal altitude is 20-600 m above sea level. At higher altitudes, the biological cycle lengthens. At altitudes of 70-100 meters, the biological cycle can lengthen by 45-76 days.

## TEMPERATURE

The optimal temperature is between 20°C and 30°C. Temperatures below 20°C and above 30°C cause a delay in physiological development. With temperatures below 10°C, growth stops, the latex of the pericarp coagulates and takes on a light brown color; in addition, the fruits do not ripen normally. High temperatures with intense radiation can cause burns in developing plants (Figure 7).

## WATER

The plant is composed of 85-90% water, and transpiration is very high. This crop requires abundant amounts of water for proper development, so it is recommended to plant in areas with rainfall between 1,500 and 2,500 mm, distributed throughout the year. The monthly water needs are 150 to 180 mm.

## LIGHT

The plant needs high light intensity for proper development. As the intensity of light decreases, the vegetative cycle lengthens to 14 months. With 2,000 and 10,000 lux (hour of light/year), photosynthetic activity increases rapidly.

## RELATIVE HUMIDITY

It should be adequate (75-80%), although it affects the crop indirectly, as it favors the incidence of foliar diseases, especially those of fungal origin.

## LIGHT

The light available in the tropics is sufficient for the crop, but it is an important factor, among others, for the development of lateral buds or shoots, so short planting distances affect the growth of these and prolong the vegetative cycle.

## WIND

It is not recommended to establish plantations in areas exposed to winds greater than 20 km/h, as they damage the leaves, causing slight tears that do not affect the yield, but when the plant is poorly nourished, it falls (Figure 8). Winds with a speed greater than 50 km/h cause the plant to bend, causing strong tears in the leaves, causing losses of 60 to 100%. An estimated loss of harvest of 20 to 30% due to wind (global estimate).

## SOIL REQUIREMENTS

The soil must have a depth of at least 1.2 m without a water table or hardened layers, and without internal drainage problems. The most suitable soils are: a loose texture to retain moisture, as well as clay soils can be suitable if they have a crumbly or granular structure, as the texture is linked to the soil structure. The most recommended textures range from sandy loams to clayey loams. The percentage of clay should not exceed 40% or be less than 20%. The ideal pH is between 5.5 and 7.0, and rich in organic matter.

The banana varieties most commonly planted in the country are:

## CRIOL BANANA OR USULUTAN

This variety is characterized by its high vigor, reaching a height of between 3.5 and 4.0 m, with a pseudostem diameter of 0.20 to 0.22 meters, short bunches with an average number of 27 to 30 fruits and a weight of 12-16 kg (Figure 9). ## VII. PLANTING

## PREPARATION OF THE LAND

Once the land has been selected, the following recommendations can be considered: weed control (manual, mechanical or chemical), soil preparation, with plowing to a depth of 40 cm and harrowing 8 days later to break up clods and incorporate straw. Then, perform planting.

## SELECTION OF PLANTING MATERIAL

The average banana yield can be significantly improved by selecting suitable planting material (corms or rhizomes). This material should be free of pests and diseases, as well as have certain characteristics in terms of size and quality.

## TYPES OF PLANTING MATERIAL

## · ADULT PLANT RHIZOMES

These are corms originating from plants that have been harvested. This material is considered the least suitable, as handling and transport are complicated due to its weight and size; in addition, this method produces relatively weak plants.

## · SPADE OFFSPRING

This is a suitable material, preferably 1.00 meter long spade offspring, with a wide and vigorous base. The main disadvantage is the higher cost of transport.

## · YOUNG OR UNRIPENED RHIZOMES

This planting material has a high nutritional reserve. The young plant should have a pseudostem diameter of at least 5 centimeters and a height of 20 centimeters from the soil, and have 2-3 well-developed buds.

## · IN-VITRO REPRODUCTION

This is the most modern and effective method of propagation, and is the best option for a new plantation, bringing plants to the field with high genetic quality, vigor, uniformity, and a high potential for yield, free of pests and diseases, as well as easy establishment.

This reproduction originates in the biotechnology laboratory, which has developed in-vitro micropropagation, starting from initial explants from spade offspring, which are selected and collected from the nursery in the field for their outstanding characteristics such as vigor and freedom from pathogens. Once in the laboratory, the explants are isolated and artificial conditions are provided to allow the cells to regenerate a new plant; subsequently, a multiplication phase is carried out and finally an acclimatization phase of the plants from the laboratory in a greenhouse for their development and final planting in the field (Figure 11).

## PREPARATION AND TREATMENT OF THE MATERIAL

The material (rhizome), must be peeled, removing the soil attached to it, roots and any damaged tissue by pests. It must be disinfected to be free of pathogens.

Immerse the material for 5-10 minutes in a mixture of botanical insecticide - nematicide, for example: extracts of Tagetes erecta 80% + extracts of marine algae Ascophylum nodosum 10% at a rate of 16 cc / liter of water or immerse the material for 10-15 minutes in water heated to temperatures of 56-58º C.

Plant material produced in the laboratory (in-vitro) is recommended due to its health and freedom from pests and diseases. If this material is not available, the rhizomes can be used with the previously mentioned recommendations.

## PLANTING PROCEDURE

Mark the land with stakes, according to the planting density. The dimensions of the hole in sandy soils are: 0.40 x 0.40 x 0.40 meters, in clayey soils 0.60 x 0.60 x 0.60 meters.

In clay soils, add 30 lb of organic matter mixed with the soil. The suckers, offspring or plants must be of uniform size, when applying fertilizer, place it at the bottom of the hole, the rhizome must be covered with a layer of 5-10 cm, of soil and 45 days after, replant.

## Planting Seasons

Plantains can be planted at any time of the year, provided there is sufficient moisture, either through rainfall or irrigation.

It is recommended to plan staggered plantings to maintain adequate production throughout the year.

## VIII. CULTURAL PRACTICES

## PRUNING OR THINNING

This is done to maintain the plantation in optimal conditions and to obtain maximum yield. This involves selecting the suckers to be removed, leaving the rest, ensuring uniform production throughout the year. Pruning prevents the development of weak plants with small roots and poor quality. After 3-4 months of planting, the first selective thinning is carried out, leaving the largest, most vigorous, and deepest sucker. This is then repeated every two months (Figure 13).

The first productive sucker should be vigorous on the opposite side of the mother plant's slope. With a height of 0.80-1.00 m, the next generation (grandchild) is selected from the first sprouts of the selected suckers, towards the sunnier areas, and away from the suckers of neighboring plants, while also being away from the mother plant's slope. The suckers to be removed are those that have reached a height of 0.80 - 1.00 m.

## THINNING

There are two types of thinning: sanitary, which aims to remove diseased leaves due to Sigatoka, or leaves that have reached the end of their life cycle (old, yellow, or dry leaves); and protective thinning of flower clusters, which involves removing leaves or parts of leaves that could damage the flower cluster. The cutting of leaves should be done from the bottom up, taking care to leave a small portion of the false petiole, to prevent the entry of diseases through the pseudostem.

There are basically three types of suckers or sprouts, which can be easily differentiated: sword suckers, water suckers, and sprout suckers.

## SWORD SUCKERS

These are attached to the mother plant, are vigorous, have good development, have a firm, cone-shaped stem (the base is wider than the top), thin leaves that end in a point, and are lance-shaped. They remain closed until they exceed a height of 1 meter (Figure 14). When separated from the mother plant, they indicate that they are coming from the lower part of the stem, providing good anchorage, and are the best vegetative material for planting.

Figure 14. Sword suckers.

<!-- image -->

## WATER SUCKERS

These grow separately from the mother plant, have a thin, cylindrical, and uniform stem, and their leaves open before reaching a height of 1 meter. They are nutritionally deficient, are weak, and produce flower clusters and fruits of poor quality (low production), so they are not recommended for planting (Figure 15).

Figure 15. Water suckers.

## BANANA SHOOTS

These are large sprouts that already have lateral buds (buds), they regrow after pruning, grow quickly, and are often mistaken for water sprouts; the best approach is to remove them (Figure 16).

## WEED CONTROL

Weed control can be carried out mechanically or chemically, generally a combination of both is the most effective.

Common herbicides used include, for example:

- ·Fluazifop-P-Butyl 12.5 EC: This is a post-emergent systemic herbicide, effective for grasses. The dose varies from 1.0 - 2.0 liters per tree.
- ·Glyphosate 35.6 SL: This is a post-emergent systemic herbicide, effective for grasses that are difficult to control and for broadleaf weeds. The dose varies from 0.75 - 3.0 liters/plant.

## IX. FERTILIZATION

For proper fertilization, take soil samples for analysis in the laboratory. The most needed major elements are: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium.

## NITROGEN

This is related to the growth and production of plant matter. A deficiency reduces plant growth, the number and size of leaves, giving them a pale green color, reduces the distance between leaves on the pseudostem, appearing as a rosette or stunted growth.

An excess of nitrogen produces plants with very developed leaves that are dark green; however, the fruit does not fill out completely, the fruits are thinner, and the weight of the bunch is lower.

## PHOSPHORUS

This is important for root and bunch formation. Deficiencies are difficult to detect in the field; however, it reduces the rate of leaf production, which appear very green with marginal chlorosis, followed by necrosis. This can cause the plant to become stunted. Phosphorus deficiency drastically reduces growth, the number of leaves, and the interval between new leaf production, eventually leading to premature death.

## POTASSIUM

Along with nitrogen, potassium is the most important element in banana cultivation, it plays a role in the plant's and the fruit's water balance; the fruit can contain up to 350 milligrams of potassium, so a large amount of this element is extracted during harvest, therefore, it is necessary to add it to the soil through fertilizer.

When potassium is deficient, the plant's growth is drastically reduced, the number of leaves decreases, and the interval between new leaf production lengthens. In a severe deficiency, the margins of the leaves turn yellow, spreading towards the central vein, the older leaves die quickly, appearing as premature senescence, additionally, the size of the bunch decreases, the length and thickness of the fruit are affected, the number of hands decreases, and deformed fruits are produced.

## MAGNESIUM

Magnesium is a mineral constituent of plant chlorophyll, it is actively involved in photosynthesis, it helps with phosphate metabolism and the activation of numerous enzyme systems. Magnesium deficiencies appear on the older leaves, as initially yellow halos, then

<!-- image -->

bronze, and finally brown, distributed on the leaf blade very close to the edge. These deficiencies are more common in sandy soils and in acidic soils.

## X. IRRIGATION

Water is one of the most important factors for a good harvest, for its development (large leaves and fleshy pseudostems). Total water requirements per year: 1,800 mm in a humid tropics and 3,000 mm in a dry tropics.

## THE CHOICE OF IRRIGATION SYSTEM IS DETERMINED BY:

The availability of water, soil, topography, climate, labor capacity, electrical energy, and the cost of these resources.

It is important to consider the cost of the systems and water conservation. If the amount of water is insufficient, it can only moisten the surface of the soil, which is more susceptible to evaporation. Irrigation should be more frequent, and water consumption will be higher, leading to excessive moisture and potentially damaging the root zone.

## THE AMOUNT OF WATER TO APPLY DEPENDS ON VARIOUS FACTORS, SUCH AS:

## 1- THE SOIL'S WATER-RETENTION CAPACITY

Clay soils have a higher water-holding capacity, while sandy soils have a lower capacity.

## 2- THE DEPTH TO WHICH THE MAJORITY OF THE ROOTS ARE FOUND

In bananas, the root zone is found in the first 20 cm, and the vertical root extends to 60 cm; in lighter soils, it can extend to 2 meters.

## 3- THE MOST FAVORABLE TIME FOR IRRIGATION

With appropriate equipment, such as a tensiometer, which measures the field capacity, the amount of water the soil can retain after saturation, and knowledge of the plant's behavior between irrigations.

The irrigation interval is the time between irrigations. Establishment phase: every 5 days. Growth phase: every 7-9 days. Flowering and fruit development phase: every 5-7 days. Harvesting phase: every 7-9 days.

During the rainy season, only 30-40% of the water is utilized. It is important to consider evapotranspiration.

## TYPES OF IRRIGATION

## FLOOD IRRIGATION OR SURFACE IRRIGATION

This involves introducing water to the plantation and allowing it to saturate the soil completely. This method wastes water and nutrients, as the fertilizer can be carried to deep layers, beyond the reach of the roots. The efficiency of surface irrigation is typically around 60%.

## MICRO-SPRINKLER AND DRIP IRRIGATION

These methods are more efficient than surface irrigation, as they optimize the use of water and fertilizers. There are fewer variations in soil moisture, and the soil remains adequately aerated, creating favorable conditions for plant growth, earlier flowering, higher yields, and larger fruit size.

## SYMPTOMS

Begins with small spots that develop into thin, reddish-brown streaks. These streaks merge and darken, eventually turning black. The black and dead areas dry out and turn a paler color. The spots tend to be more severe at the tips of the leaves. Affected leaves die in 3-4 weeks. It is more severe in conditions of high humidity and temperature.

## SIGATOKA YELLOW

(Mycosphaerella mustacola)

Figures 19. Yellow or yellowish-brown spots and necrosis on the left side of the leaf tip.

<!-- image -->

## SYMPTOMS

Begins with yellow to brownish spots, with a greater presence and abundance on the tips and on the left side of the leaf. These spots then turn dark brown with a well-defined yellow halo, and when they merge, they cause the death of the tissue.

## CULTURAL CONTROL (For both Sigatoka diseases)

- Plant in sandy, well-drained soils.
- Fertilize appropriately and at the right time.
- Remove damaged leaves (phytosanitary pruning).
- Prune leaves every 15 days during periods of heavy rainfall and every month during periods of less rainfall (leaving a minimum of 12 leaves per plant).
- If leaves fall, spray them with 10% urea to inactivate the fungal spores.

- ·Copper sulfate 24 SC, at a rate of 10-16 cc / 4-gallon sprayer. Application interval: 15 days. 1 day,

## SYMPTOMS

- ·Attacks roots and invades the vascular system, preventing normal nutrition, and progressively dehydrating. Presents yellowing leaves, wilting, and ultimately, plant death.
- ·Begins on lower leaves from the edges towards the central vein, vascular bundles show yellow to brown discoloration.

## CULTURAL CONTROL

- ·Remove affected plants, bury them (1.0-1.5 meters deep), or burn them outside the planting area, to prevent the source of inoculum.
- ·Perform soil analysis and then fertilize.
- ·Maintain adequate drainage, as water is a source of disease spread.
- ·Use planting material from disease-free crops.
- ·Apply 1-2 kg / plant of agricultural lime or calcium sulfate.
- ·Apply periodic doses of zinc chelate.

## CIGAR TIP ROT

The fungi found are: Gloeosporium sp., Colletotrichum sp., Verticillium sp., among others.

## SYMPTOMS

Necrosis at the tip of the fruit, similar to cigar ash.

## CULTURAL CONTROL

Defoliate. This involves cutting the flower buds, 12-15 days after emergence. When the fingers are horizontal and the flower begins to necrosate with a black ring, remove each flower downwards.

## CHEMICAL CONTROL

- ·Azoxystrobin 50 WG, at a rate of 5 grams / 4-gallon sprayer. 1 day, waiting period between the last application and harvest.
- ·Tebuconazole + Triadimezone 30 EC, at a rate of 40 cc / 4-gallon sprayer. 1 day, waiting period between the last application and harvest.
- ·Carbendazim 50 SC, at a rate of 12 cc / 4-gallon sprayer. 7 days, waiting period between the last application and harvest.

## STEM AND SHOOT ROT (Erwinia sp.)

<!-- image -->

Technical Guide

## SYMPTOMS

- ·Begins in wounds on the shoot, causing the rotting of the pods and stems or shoots.
- ·Causes yellowing leaves, starting from the edge and reaching the central vein, causing the rotting of the pods of the shoot and subsequently the death of the lower leaves upwards, followed by a foul odor.
- ·Affected plants show wilting and loss of anchorage, as a result, they bend.
- ·In conditions of high humidity, the bacteria infect the seedlings, becoming the main means of propagation.

## CULTURAL CONTROL

- ·Use healthy (plants and shoots) material free of the disease, preferably vitroplants.
- ·Remove affected plants, bury them (at a depth of 1.0-1.5 m) or burn them outside the planting area.
- ·Maintain adequate drainage, as it is a source of contamination and spread.
- ·Crop rotation with grasses, chipilin, etc.
- ·Apply lime before sowing (1.5 lb per application).

## CHEMICAL PREVENTIVE CONTROL

- ·Copper sulfate 24 SC, at a dose of 20-30 cc / 4-gallon pump.
- ·Streptomycin, Oxytetracycline 16.5 WP at a dose of 20 g / 4-gallon pump.

Note: Once the plant is infected, there is no chemical control for the disease.

## XII. PRE-HARVEST ACTIVITIES

The quality of bananas is linked to their physiological maturity, the length of their fingers, and the absence of defects. To meet the quality indices and reach the optimal standards required by the destination market, it is necessary to know and implement a series of pre-harvest and harvest activities, which are the responsibility of the producer, and the post-harvest activities (post-harvest) are the responsibility of the commercial company, which defines the clusters for their final packaging.

## Table 6. Activities to be carried out according to the destination market.

The activities should be carried out by the producer in a timely manner in order to create a favorable environment for the development and quality of the bunch to be harvested.

Failing to perform some of these tasks or not doing them in a timely manner can result in low-quality products and fruit being discarded for sale.

Cleaning the environment involves removing leaves, which are typically of two types:

## CURRENT LEAF REMOVAL OR PROTECTION

The goal is to reduce damage caused by leaves rubbing against the cluster. This task uses a stick with a knife at the end.

## SANITARY LEAF REMOVAL

This involves cutting off leaves that are folded and all those with more than 50% of dead (necrotic) tissue. Folded leaves allow the growth and spore formation of fungi or the infestation of harmful insects.

The leaf remover should remove the cut leaves from the base of the plant and from the irrigation and drainage channels. If there is bacterial rot in the area, it is essential to disinfect the blade after removing each plant. Formalin at 10% can be used for this purpose. Generally, it is recommended to perform leaf removal cycles every two weeks.

## BAGGING

The benefits of bagging include:

- Increases the growth rate of the fruit by maintaining a temperature higher than the external environment.
- Allows the fruit to reach a larger size in length, weight, and diameter.
- Prevents damage from leaf folding.
- Prevents pest attacks.
- Prevents damage to the fruit caused by birds and rodents.

Figure 26. Bagging the cluster.

For bagging, the top leaf is moved or turned to the opposite side of the berry, the leaf or part of the leaves that hinder the development of the cluster is removed, then the bag is placed around the berry and secured. The bag is tied at the points where the bracts form.

## TIEING

The benefits of tying include:

- Allows for planning the harvest.
- Allows for harvesting clusters according to market requirements.
- Allows for harvesting clusters with the same degree of ripeness.

The tape can be placed by tying it to the top of the cluster along with the bag, or by tying the tape to one of the lower ends of the bag.

## FLOWER REMOVAL

Benefits: Good presentation of the fruit and prevention of phytosanitary problems such as false finger and cigar tip rot. This task is done by hand, so it is important to wash your hands and arms thoroughly before starting.

The first flower removal is done 4 days after placing the bag, making sure that the fingers of the hands are parallel to the ground. It is done from the middle of the cluster upwards. The second flower removal is done two days after the first flower removal, when the remaining fingers have reached the position of fingers parallel to the ground.

## LOWER STEM REMOVAL

This involves removing the lower stems, leaving only one stem in the side or center, called the marker stem. This task is done when the cluster has completely detached and aims to allow the stems to reach the required length and thickness for the market. The benefit of this practice is that it allows for uniform ripening of the cluster and increases the size of the stems (Figure 27).

Figure 27. Removal of lower stems that are no longer developing.

## PICHOTA REMOVAL

This activity involves removing the pichota, which should be removed between 15 and 25 cm, below the last removed stem or hand. This task is done by removing the pichota and leaving a 8 to 10 cm rachis, to avoid any rot problems that affect the commercial hand of the cluster (Figure 28). Once removed, it should be cut into small pieces to facilitate rapid decomposition, thus preventing it from serving as a host for trips and other insects that may nest in it.

This practice allows for optimal nutrient utilization towards the fruit, resulting in better filling of the stems in less time and better weight of the cluster.

Figures 28. Removal of the pichota.

## STAKING

This task involves providing support to the plants with the current clusters, as they often tend to bend or fall over, which causes significant losses for producers (Figure 29). It is important to know why the plants bend, in order to take measures and prevent it from happening. The clusters are very heavy and fall due to strong winds, due to very severe or poorly made leaf removal, and due to attack by weevils and nematodes.

Figures 29. Staking with a hook-shaped stake.

## XIII. HARVEST

This involves separating the clusters from the plants when they have reached the optimal stage of harvest according to market requirements. This task is defined by the age of the cluster, the color of the tape, the age or external appearance of the fruit.

## THE MAIN TASKS ARE:

## Cutting the Pseudostem

This involves making a cross-cut approximately 2/3 of the way up the pseudostem, so that the bunch, due to its weight, causes the plant to bend.

## Receiving the Bunch

The bunch must hang without touching the ground, with the bag still covering it. The handler should have a pad on their shoulder.

## Cutting the Stem

This involves making a cut on the bunch stem, at the top of the hand closest to the bunch, above the bag (the machete must be disinfected).

## Transport

During transport to the processing center, use padding to protect the fruit and prevent damage.

## Post-Harvest Activities

This involves a series of activities aimed at preparing and presenting the fruit, including the following steps:

## Receiving the Bunch

This is the arrival of the bunch at the processing facility, where it is hung for proper handling.

## Evaluating the Bunch

This involves measuring the diameter of the central finger of the second hand using a ruler, and the length of the central finger using a measuring tape.

## Separation

This involves separating the fingers from the stem, using disinfected tools and washed hands.

## Washing

This involves placing the bunches in a pile of water with small amounts of sodium hypochlorite for about 15 minutes to remove latex.

## Selection

This involves removing or separating fingers that have damage due to disease, mechanical damage, or scars.

## Classification

Classification is based on the requirements of the purchasing company or the general market. It can be based on sensory characteristics such as the color of the peel (light green or dark green), the color of the pulp (cream or bone color), the shape (elongated or thick), and the texture of the pulp (firm).

## Packaging

This involves placing the selected and treated fruit in wooden, plastic, and cardboard boxes. These should maintain the quality of the product and also facilitate marketing.

## XIV. Marketing

All activities carried out in the establishment, including agronomic management, fertilization, irrigation, and pest control, affect the marketing of the banana. If these are efficient, everything is worthwhile; otherwise, everything is a failure.

Some parameters of the fruit that should be considered for an efficient marketing process are:

- Firm consistency.
- Bright color.
- Free from bruises and impacts.
- Good state of maturity.
- Classification by size.
