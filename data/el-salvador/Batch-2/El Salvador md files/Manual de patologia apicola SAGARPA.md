## 2 What is IICA?

The Inter-American Institute of Cooperation in Agriculture (IICA) is the specialized agricultural organization of the Inter-American System. Its origins date back to 1942, when the Executive Council of the Pan-American Union approved the creation of the Inter-American Institute of Agricultural Sciences, based in Costa Rica.

The Institute's mission is to support the 34 member states in achieving sustainable agriculture, within the framework of hemispheric integration, as a contribution to rural development through cooperation in the following strategic areas:

- Socioeconomic Policies, Trade and Investments
- Agricultural and Food Safety
- Science and Technology; Natural Resources and Agricultural Production
- Sustainable Rural Development
- Training and Education
- Information and Communication.

## Area of Agricultural and Food Safety

## Objective:

- To ensure that countries have an optimal agricultural and food safety situation, so that their animal and plant products comply with the strictest health and hygiene standards and can compete effectively in the international market.

## Priority Action Lines:

- Practical application and enforcement of sanitary and phytosanitary measures in international trade
- Modernization of National Agricultural Safety Systems with the active participation of the private sector.
- Alert and action on emerging issues.
- Strengthening of the Inter-American and Regional approach.

## INTRODUCTION

## 1 INTRODUCTION

There are more than 20 known diseases of honey bees (Apis mellifera). However, less than 10 are of true importance. It is necessary for beekeepers to learn to recognize some bee diseases, especially those affecting the brood, as early detection can prevent significant economic losses. Fortunately, none of these bee diseases are zoonotic (transmissible to humans through natural means). In the United States, beekeepers primarily need to be concerned with 8 diseases that cause significant economic losses each year: VARROASIS; AMERICAN FOULBROOD; ASIAN VARROASIS; EUROPEAN FOULBROOD; BROOD DISEASE; PARALYSIS; AND ENSACED BROOD. There are also other diseases, such as American Nosemosis, that are less common but still important.

In addition to these diseases, beekeepers in Mexico, Central America, and South America should also be aware of Aethina tumida, the small gall wasp, which is present in the United States and can potentially enter our borders through various means, such as the import of illegal queen bees or bee colonies. Beekeepers should also be prepared to recognize and prevent the entry of pests into their colonies, such as bee lice, ants, and other less common pests.

In the United States, studies estimate that bee diseases cause an average loss of $3 per colony per year. If we consider this figure for the 2 million bee colonies in Mexico, the losses would amount to $6 million per year. This figure does not take into account the fact that beekeeping practices in the United States are generally better than in our country.

Bee diseases can be classified in several ways. For the purpose of this manual, we will classify them as diseases of the brood and diseases of adult bees. Both types of diseases can be further divided according to the causative agent (bacterial, fungal, viral, and parasitic diseases).

Diseases of the brood can be easily identified by experienced beekeepers, but diseases of adult bees require the sending of samples to a specialized laboratory, as their symptoms are often ambiguous or, more commonly, are not observed. Therefore, beekeepers may not understand the cause of low colony productivity, even when their bees appear to be healthy. It is therefore recommended to take samples from each apiary at least once a year, before the flowering season (3-4 months before), and to send the bee samples to a laboratory for diagnosis.

Typically, beekeepers fill these cells with food (honey and pollen). The infection occurs when the nurse bees provide this contaminated food to the larvae. Human practices that favor the spread of the infection include: failing to disinfect work tools after inspecting an infected colony, or through indiscriminate exchange of frames between colonies. Another way the disease spreads is through robbing by bees that carry the infection to a sick colony, including queen bees and brood nests with poor management practices.

PATHOGENESIS: The larvae acquire the infection by ingesting the spores with the food. A newly hatched larva (born) can be infected with a single spore, but after 2 days, the larva's susceptibility is practically zero:

The spores germinate one day after ingestion, and the vegetative form of the bacteria reproduces in the intestine, then moves to the hemolymph, where it continues to reproduce and produces millions of spores that initiate the release of exotoxins, killing the larva within a few days, usually when it has started its pupal stage (sealed brood), although in some cases, the larva dies before pupation. After death, the brood gradually dries out until, after approximately 30 days, only the scale remains attached to the bottom of the cell.

When the infection is severe, the worker bee population of the colony decreases dramatically, as the bees emerging are few enough to maintain the colony, potentially leading to its collapse.

CLINICAL SIGNS: Among the signs we can observe in the frames, are the following: The brood appears "jumpy," meaning there is no continuity in the scales (it looks like a load of scales has been dumped into the frame). The scales are dark, sunken, and greasy-looking, and some have a small perforation: The smell of the frames is musty and similar to the smell of "glue" used in carpentry, and some say it smells like rotten fish. If you open a scale, you will find the dead larvae with a mass of color ranging from creamy yellow to brown, and then black, depending on the degree of decomposition. Larvae that have been dead for about 30 days dry out completely, leaving the scale firmly attached to the cell, making it difficult to remove (unlike the European scale, which is easy to remove). In some cases, when the larvae die at the end of the pupal stage, it is common to see their tongue pointing upwards on the scales (this is not observed in European foulbrood).

DIAGNOSIS: To identify the disease without confusing it with European foulbrood, it is necessary to take into account the age of the affected brood. In American foulbrood, the brood will be sealed, while in European foulbrood, it will be young and unsealed (in most cases). The presence of the tongue is specific to American foulbrood, as are the firmly attached scales and their characteristic smell. Field diagnosis is very reliable and is based on the "stick test," which involves inserting a stick or thin rod into an affected cell and gently removing it. If a viscous, gelatinous thread forms and extends at least 2 cm, we can be sure that it is American foulbrood. However, it is recommended that if the beekeeper wants to be absolutely certain of the diagnosis, they send a piece of frame with dead larvae to the laboratory for identification.

In Mexico, there is the National Center for Animal Health Services, located in Jiutepec, Morelos, which develops the techniques for identifying the causative agent.

CONTROL MEASURES: The most effective measure would be to burn the infected colonies, along with the bees, as there is no medication capable of destroying the resistant spores of Paenibacillus larvae. However, when this is not possible for various reasons, the following measures are recommended:

- a) Remove and burn all the frames from the colony, taking care not to throw any pieces into the apiary, as this would favor the re-establishment of the disease in other colonies.
2. To carry out this procedure and not lose the colony housed in this equipment, it is necessary to place an empty hive in the place of the sick colony to provide it with space for the bees. In addition, it must be provided with some frames with live larvae, obtained from a healthy and vigorous colony.
- b) Sterilize the floor, hive(s), and roof. To do this, it is necessary to wash them with a powerful disinfectant capable of destroying the spores of Paenibacillus larvae, such as a 1% solution of hydrogen peroxide (water) containing 0.5% formic acid; another possibility is with a 4% solution of sodium hydroxide. When washing these pieces, it is advisable to protect your hands with gloves and use a brush to clean them. Subsequently, the equipment should be flamed with a blowtorch or torch, or by stacking the boxes on a floor, pouring a small amount of alcohol inside and lighting it until it is well flamed. Then, place an external lid on the boxes, which will completely extinguish the fire. This procedure should be carried out with the boxes inverted so that no viable spores remain in the recess where the heads of the frames rest.

TREATMENT: Currently, beekeepers in countries that produce and export honey to the European Union must comply with a series of requirements, including submitting honey to laboratory analysis to determine if it is contaminated with antibiotics (sulfates, tetracycline, streptomycin), and pesticides (phosphates, chlorinated and pyrethroids such as fluvalinate). Therefore, the use of these should be reduced and, if possible, replaced with biological treatments or non-contaminating chemicals.

However, in the United States, the use of sulfates and tetracyclines is accepted, so with proper use, it is possible to export honey from colonies treated with these antibiotics.

MEDICATION OF INFECTED COLONIES: There are two groups of medications that effectively act against the vegetative form of the bacteria: One is the group of sulfates, and the other is the group of tetracyclines. Within the first group of medications, we have TRISULFATES and SULFATIAZOL SODIUM (in some countries, these medications are no longer sold). When possible, find some of these products, the way to use them is to dissolve the amount that contains 1 gr. of pure salt of the chosen sulfa in a medium or a liter of sugar syrup. The syrup is prepared by dissolving the sugar in hot water in equal parts. It is important to wait for the syrup to cool before adding the medication. Another way to administer it is to mix it with 20 gr. of powdered sugar (sugar glass) or 250 gr. of urea paste made of sugar and a little water (or burned honey from the melting of the honeycomb). Either of these options contains enough medication to treat a bee colony.

The syrup should be administered, along with the paste or powdered sugar, inside the hive in a feeder, in a can, in a bottle or plastic bag with perforations, etc. (for the syrup), or on a piece of newspaper; or wrapped in the same or sprinkled on the frames (for the paste and sugar glass). The reason for administering it inside the hive is to prevent robbing by other bees and to prevent sunlight from inactivating the product. This treatment should be repeated 3 to 5 times with a period of 8 to 14 days between treatments.

In the second group of medications, we have TERRAMYCIN (TM-10, TM-25, TM-50) and other types of OXITETRACYLINES. These products are applied in a similar way to the sulfates, varying only the dose. It is required 300 mg of pure salt per treatment and per colony; therefore, 15 9. of the commercial product should be administered for TM-10, 6 gr. when using TM-25, or 3 gr. if the choice is TM50.

It is important to mention that the continuous and indiscriminate use of an antibiotic to treat the disease can favor the development of resistance of Paenibacillus larvae, so when this happens, the medication no longer works, therefore, it is recommended to rotate the type of antibiotic used year after year, for example, using Terramycin one year and sulfa the next year. It is the beekeeper's responsibility not to use antibiotics during flowering, so that the honey is not contaminated with them, as it is a product for human consumption.

Furthermore, excessive doses of the medication per colony can promote the development of diseases such as scale and stone brood.

## 2.1.3. DUSTY SCALE

Also known as dusty brood, this is an infectious disease of bee larvae, of bacterial origin, caused by Bacillus pulvifaciens (Katznelson). It is a seemingly rare disease, perhaps because the average beekeeper is unable to identify it.

The disease was first described in 1950 by Katznelson, who found the germ in scales that turned to dust when extracted from the cells where the larvae had died.

ETIOLOGY: Bacillus pulvifaciens (Katznelson) is a gram-positive bacterium that forms spores and has an optimal growth temperature of 45°C. However, the etiology is still questioned by some authors.

## 2. EPIDEMIOLOGY

Except for its presence in the United States, the worldwide distribution of this disease is unknown.

The disease exclusively affects bee larvae. It is likely that *Bacillus pulvifaciens* is a saprophytic microorganism in the hive, which becomes pathogenic under certain stress conditions (such as low brood temperature, lack of food for larvae, etc.). The disease is spread through spores that remain latent in the honeycomb and contaminate the food that the larvae consume.

PATHOGENESIS: It is believed that the larvae ingest the bacteria's spores with the food, and subsequently, the germ multiplies and kills the larva; however, this has not yet been scientifically proven. The larva dies when the cell has been sealed; it dries out inside, leaving a scale that is difficult to remove from the bottom walls of the cell; which can be mistaken for American Loque.

SYMPTOMS: The larvae may appear "jumped," with the affected larva having been sealed. Sunken and perforated cells are observed, along with the presence of scales where larvae have died. Some of the characteristic features of the disease that help to differentiate it from American Loque include the lack of any special odor, and the fact that the scales are yellowish in color and extend along the bottom walls of the cells. Another particularly interesting characteristic is that the scales turn to dust when they are tried to be removed from the cells.

DIAGNOSIS: In the field, diagnosis should be based on the clinical picture. In the laboratory, cultures should be taken to observe the dark red pigment released by the bacterial colonies, or a hanging drop test can be performed. The bacteria's spores do not exhibit Brownian motion.

TREATMENT: There is no specific treatment known, but it is likely that the same recommendations as for treating American Loque will be followed once more is known about this disease.

## 22. BEE DISEASES

## 22.1. BEE GRIT

This is an infectious fungal disease that affects only bee larvae. It is also known as Ascosferosis, Chalky brood, Chalk brood, Sawdust brood, Chalky brood, etc. For some years, it was considered a minor disease, but in the last 25 years, it has become a significant economic problem for beekeeping, as it has become quite common. The disease is caused by the fungus *Ascosphaera apis* (Maassen Claussen).

In 1913, Maassen published the first observations on Chalky brood, naming the causal fungus *Pericystes apis*. Claussen published in 1921 a detailed article on the morphology of the fungus. Spiltoir and Olive reclassified the fungus in 1955, giving it the name *Ascosphaera apis* (Maassen Claussen).

ETIOLOGY: *Ascosphaera apis* (Maassen Claussen) is a fungus of the Ascomycete class, which reproduces heterotally when the hyphae of opposite-sex fungi come into contact, resulting in the formation of spores, which is the means by which the fungus spreads. The hyphae or spores are the growth form of the organism and are white in color, while the spores are dark in color.

There are two varieties that cannot reproduce with each other, the so-called Major variety, whose spores measure 3 to 4 micrometers in diameter, and the Minor variety, whose spores measure 1 to 2 micrometers in diameter. The spores are grouped into "spore balls" that measure 9 to 19 micrometers in diameter, and these balls are enclosed in a cyst that measures between 47 and 140 micrometers. The Minor variety is the most common.

The spores are very resistant and can remain viable in the environment for at least 15 years. In the hive, the fungus develops at temperatures between 20 and 30°C.

EPIDEMIOLOGY: Chalky brood has been reported in all European countries. It is found in New Zealand, Canada, the United States, Australia, South America, and many other countries, but its existence in Nicaragua and Costa Rica has not yet been confirmed.

The disease can affect the larvae of the three bee castes, but it is most common in drone larvae. It is known that Chalky brood also affects the larvae of some wild bee species, such as those in the genus *Megachile*. The disease is most common during rainy periods and in cold weather. The fungus itself does not cause great damage without the help of predisposed factors that allow it to develop, such as humidity, low temperatures, poor ventilation in the hive, and its presence in weak colonies, as well as in colonies that have been treated with antibiotics. Colonies of inbred bees also seem to be more susceptible to the disease.

PREDISPOSING FACTORS favor the development of the fungus's hyphae for several reasons, including:

- 1 Humidity - Provides a suitable environment.
2. Low temperatures. Facilitate the diffusion of oxygen (an aerobic environment) and provide the ideal temperature (20 to 30°C in the brood nest).
3. Poor ventilation - Favors the presence of humidity.
4. Weak colonies. Cannot maintain the brood nest temperature above 30°C.

Antibiotic abuse - Destroys the normal microbial flora of the larval digestive tract.

The honeycomb, especially the older ones, is a potential source of infection, as it is an important reservoir of spores; however, spores can also come from pollen in the flowers where bees have defecated (especially wild bees, which are vectors). Spores can be inadvertently introduced to other colonies by the beekeeper with the frame, honeycomb, or contaminated honey; pillaging also plays a role in the transmission of the disease. The larvae acquire the spores when they consume them with the food, in the presence of predisposed factors.

PATHOGENESIS: Larvae are most susceptible to the disease between days 3 and 4 of age. The spores reach the larval digestive tract with the food, or they adhere to the larvae's skin when present in the brood cells, under the influence of predisposed factors. The fungus's hyphae begin to grow from the spore in the larva's intestine or on its skin. In the intestine, the hyphae penetrate the digestive walls and pass through the larval body, enveloping it completely as if they were roots. The hyphae also envelop the larva from the skin, giving it a mummified appearance. The larva can die in an open cell or in a sealed cell; after death, it dries out and hardens, acquiring the consistency and color of a piece of chalk. The mortality rate of larvae is generally low, but it can sometimes exceed 30%.

SYMPTOMS: When the larvae appear as pieces of chalk (chalk or tiza), they are called "mummified" larvae, and they are observed in both open and sealed cells, as well as on the floor in front of the brood entrances (since the worker bees remove them from the honeycomb). The white color is due to the color of the fungus's hyphae. Sometimes, the larvae appear hardened but with a brown or dark green color, which occurs when the affected larvae are covered by fungi in their reproductive stage. The dark color is due to the color of the spores.

Most of the affected larvae are found on the periphery of the honeycomb, with drone larvae being the most affected. When the infection is severe, shaking the honeycomb can produce a "maraca" sound, as the mummies are not firmly attached to the cells and rattle against the walls.

DIAGNOSIS: Diagnosis can be easily made based on the clinical picture, but it can also be made in the laboratory by taking a wet mount that shows the cysts and spore balls. The fungus can be cultured on Saboureaud agar.

TREATMENT: Few medications have been tested for treating this disease, as it was not considered important. In recent years, the use of Nystatin (Micostatin) and Thibenzole (Thibenzole) at a concentration of 2 g per treatment and per colony has been shown to be effective, with treatments given every 8 to 14 days. The treatments are administered in syrup or as a paste, in a similar way to American Loque. Amphotericin B has shown very good results, but it is unstable and very expensive.

Fumigation of the honeycomb with various products has also been tested. Among the most effective are ethylene oxide, which requires certain infrastructure, which makes it unsuitable for field use; thyme oil at 0.7 percent, formaldehyde at 4 percent, quaternary ammonium, sodium propionate, and even a simple soapy solution.

However, it is likely that measures to prevent or reduce the presence of predisposed factors in the colonies are more important than treating the disease with drugs. These measures include:

- a) Keeping the colonies on stands at least 30 cm from the floor.
2. Keeping the brood entrances open to promote good ventilation.
- b) Not installing apiaries in flood-prone areas or in areas with little wind protection.
- c) Tilting the colonies towards the brood entrances to prevent water from accumulating during rain.
- d) Strengthening or uniting weak colonies with healthy ones.
- e) Protecting the colonies with aluminum foil covers.
- f) Avoiding inbreeding through a good breeding program.
- g) Replacing old honeycomb in the colonies at least twice a year.
- h) Replacing the queen annually.
- i) Avoiding the use of antibiotics.
- j) Burning the mummies found in front of the brood entrances (in a smoker).
- k) Preventing the spread of the disease to healthy colonies by the beekeeper, by not moving frames between colonies, etc.
- l) Taking measures to reduce robbing.

## 22.2. ROCK BROOD

Also known as Stone brood, Aspergillosis, or Rock brood, this is an infectious fungal disease that affects both bee larvae and adult bees. It is caused by the fungus *Aspergillus flavus*, and sometimes by *Aspergillus fumigatus*. It is rare to find, and it is of little economic importance. Under certain conditions, it can cause disease in humans, such as when the fungus is reproducing and the person inhales or ingests the spores. Maassen described the disease for the first time in 1906, after isolating and identifying *Aspergillus flavus* from larvae and adult bees.

**ETIOLOGY.** Several species of the Aspergillus fungus cause it, but primarily Aspergillus flavus, and occasionally Aspergillus fumigatus.

These fungi are commonly found in the soil and the environment; they are also pathogenic to other animals, and in humans, they cause respiratory problems and poisoning. Like Ascosphaera apis, it reproduces heterotally. Its mode of transmission is the spores, which are greenish in color and less than 2 micrometers in diameter. The spores are attached to structures called conidiophores.

EPIDEMIOLOGY.- It has been reported in Europe, North America, Venezuela, and Brazil, but it is suggested that it exists worldwide, given that Aspergillus fungi are commonly found in any country. In Mexico, it was first identified in 1993 in the state of Morelos, and its distribution in the Republic is still unknown.

The disease affects both larvae and adult bees, as well as other insect and mammal species. The fact that the disease is rare and the pathogens are common suggests that for the spores to germinate and cause the disease, there must be a greater dependence on predisposing factors than in the case of Chalk Brood. These factors are the same as those that favor Ascosferosis.

The disease is most common during the rainy season and during the winter, and its transmission is favored by poor management practices, such as robbing, etc. (similar to Chalk Brood).

PATHOLOGY.- Similar to Chalk Brood. The larvae die from poisoning caused by the toxins released by the fungus, as well as from the traumatic damage caused by the mycelium.

CLINICAL PICTURE.- Only a few larvae are affected. The "mummies" have a greenish-gray or greenish-yellow color, especially in the head area. The "mummies" are attached to the bottom of the cells, so the cleaning workers can only remove them by scraping, which they then discard in front of the hive; the remaining debris are covered with propolis. The larvae most affected are those of the Varroa mite, especially those in the honeycomb.

DIAGNOSIS.- At the field level, it is based on the clinical picture. It is worth noting that to differentiate the disease from Chalk Brood, the "mummies" are dark and have the consistency of a stone, and in addition, when the hive is shaken, no sound is produced because the affected larvae are firmly attached to the base of the cells. Laboratory diagnosis is difficult and is based on the identification of conidiophores.

TREATMENT.- The same treatment as for Chalk Brood is recommended. In addition, when hives with the disease are handled, the beekeeper should cover their nose with a handkerchief to avoid inhaling fungal spores. Honey from infected colonies is not safe for human consumption.

## 2.3. VIRAL DISEASES OF THE LARVAE

## 2.3.1. BAGGED LARVAE

This disease is also known as "Bagged Larvae," "Viral Mite Disease," "Moratosis," etc. It is a contagious viral disease that affects bee larvae, caused by Moratoria apis. Although it occurs relatively frequently, it does not significantly affect the beekeeper's economy.

In 1917, White demonstrated that the disease was not bacterial or fungal by successfully re-infecting larvae with fluids from affected larvae that had been previously filtered. In 1949, Steinhaus took the first photographs of the virus using an electron microscope. Bailey in 1964 and 1975 studied the morphology and nature of Moratoria apis, and differentiated it serologically from the viruses causing paralysis in adult bees.

ETIOLOGY.- Moratoria apis is a filterable virus, hexagonal in shape, of the RNA type. It measures 28 to 30 nm. The virus has a preference for certain tissues of the larvae's body, such as the cuticle, muscles, and fat, in which it reproduces. It can be cultivated in both larval tissues and chicken fibroblasts.

EPIDEMIOLOGY.- It is believed to exist worldwide, although many countries have not reported it. In Central America, countries such as El Salvador and Honduras have not reported it.

It mainly affects the larvae of worker bees, and rarely adult bees (this has only been demonstrated at the experimental level). The disease can occur throughout the year, but is most frequent before flowering and during the rainy season, especially in weak colonies or those that have been exposed to some form of stress.

The way in which the larvae become infected has not been fully clarified. Apparently, the virus enters the larvae through one of the mechanisms: with contaminated food, such as royal jelly, pollen, and nectar, as the worker bees store the virus in the pharyngeal and salivary glands, or through contaminated eggs. Some experimental studies suggest that the queen lays infected eggs (as in Salmonellosis in poultry). Poor beekeeping practices, such as robbing, favor the transmission of the disease.

PATHOLOGY.- The larvae are susceptible to infection up to 4 days of age. The virus passes from the digestive tract to the hemolymph, and from there to the tissues where it has a preference, where it multiplies. The death of the larva occurs when the capping of the cell begins, or a few days after being capped. This happens when the process of skin shedding is interrupted, without the old cuticle detaching completely from the larva's body, so it serves as a bag that fills with the ecdysial fluid, which is very rich in viral particles. The cuticle darkens and hardens, particularly in the head area. When the larva dries, it forms a scale that is easily removed.

CLINICAL PICTURE.- Inverted, perforated, and greasy (like in American foulbrood) caps are observed on the affected cells. Inside, the characteristic observation is dead larvae inside a sac; then they take on the appearance of an inverted cone, canoe, or small bucket (this part of the body corresponds to the larva's head, which darkens and hardens and points upwards, giving the appearance of an inverted cone). As the larva dries, it takes on a darker color until it forms a removable crust on the lower walls of the cell. The crusts or scales are free of the virus, so they do not constitute a source of contagion.

DIAGNOSIS.- It can be easily diagnosed in the field, based on the clinical picture, or by removing the larvae from their sac using tweezers. In the laboratory, gel electrophoresis or electron microscopy is required, as the virus cannot be seen in normal microscopes.

TREATMENT.- There is no specific medication to treat it. It has been shown that the growth of the virus is inhibited by administering a syrup saturated with sugar (1 part sugar per 1 part water) to the affected colonies, because sucrose contains high levels of ribonuclease, an enzyme that destroys the viral RNA.

The ideal solution is to replace the queen and destroy the contaminated hives, and it is also recommended to feed the colony.

## 3.1. PARASITIC DISEASES OF ADULT BEES

## 3.1.1. VARROA MITES

Varroa mites, also known as Varroa destructor, are external parasites of honeybees. They are one of the most serious threats to honeybee health worldwide.

The Varroa mite was first identified on honeybees in the Mediterranean region in the late 19th century. However, it did not become a major problem until the mid-20th century, when it spread to other parts of Europe and North America.

In Mexico, Varroa mites were first detected in the 1980s, and have since become a major problem for beekeepers.

Varroa mites are vectors for viruses, and can also directly damage bee larvae and adults. They feed on the hemolymph of adult bees, and can also reproduce in the brood cells.

ETIOLOGY.- Varroa destructor is a species of parasitic mite that belongs to the family Varroidae. It is a Holoopt, meaning it has a complete body with a head, thorax, and abdomen. The mites are typically reddish-brown in color, and range in size from 1.5 to 3 millimeters in length. They have eight legs, and their body is covered in fine hairs. Varroa mites are highly adapted to living on honeybees, and have developed a number of strategies to avoid detection and to exploit their hosts.

EPIDEMIOLOGY.- Varroa mites are found worldwide, and are considered to be a major threat to honeybee health in all regions where honeybees are kept. They are particularly problematic in areas where honeybees are managed intensively, such as in commercial beekeeping operations.

Varroa mites can infect all three castes of honeybees: worker bees, drones, and the queen. However, they are most likely to infect larvae and pupae, as these are more vulnerable to attack. Varroa mites can reproduce in the brood cells, and can also transmit viruses to bee larvae.

The Varroa mite is a major cause of honeybee colony collapse disorder (CCD). CCD is a phenomenon in which honeybee colonies suddenly and inexplicably die. Varroa mites are thought to be one of the major contributing factors to CCD, as they can weaken colonies and make them more susceptible to other diseases and stressors.

CONTROL.- There are a number of ways to control Varroa mites in honeybee colonies. These include:

- Using chemical miticides, such as amitraz, fluvalinate, and coumaphos.
- Using organic miticides, such as thymol and oxalic acid.
- Using physical methods, such as drone brood removal.
- Using integrated pest management (IPM) strategies, which combine multiple control methods.

It is important to use a combination of control methods to effectively manage Varroa mites. Chemical miticides can be effective, but they can also have negative effects on honeybee health. Organic miticides are generally safer for honeybees, but they may not be as effective. Physical methods can be used to reduce the Varroa mite population, but they are not always practical. IPM strategies are the most sustainable way to manage Varroa mites, but they require careful planning and monitoring.

High levels of infestation become more apparent after long periods of keeping bees confined within their hive, which often occurs after periods of rain, wind, frost, and poor flowering. This is because the contact between bees is

reduced, as they spend more time inside the hive.

Varroa mite transmission is favored by poor beekeeping practices, such as using bee pushers and handling swarms. The most common way for the disease to enter a healthy apiary in an area free of the problem is through the migration of swarms or the purchase of sick bees.

Varroa mites are unable to survive without a live host for more than 2 or 3 hours, so honey and equipment are not sources of contamination.

PATHOGENESIS. Young bees (under 6 days old) are infested by the female mite when they come into physical contact with older, parasitized bees. Varroa destructor moves from the thoracic hairs of the infested bee to those of the susceptible bee, where it attaches using its claws. Subsequently, guided by the air currents produced by the bee's breathing movements, it finds the spiracle of a thorax, through which it penetrates.

Once inside the thorax, the female lays eggs (between 5 and 7 eggs), and the eggs hatch and give rise to nymphs in 3 to 6 days, and the nymphs molt and become adults approximately 2 weeks after being laid. The adults mate inside the thorax, and the fertilized females can give rise to the next generation in the same thorax or leave it to infest other bees. The transmitting bee is always older than 14 days. Infestations can be unilateral (parasites in one thorax) or bilateral (in both thoraxes).

Both nymphs and adult mites feed on the bee's hemolymph, which they suck from the walls of the thorax, using their mandibles to perforate them. This causes the keratinization and melanization lesions that are considered pathognomonic (typical) for diagnosis in the laboratory. The insufficient supply of oxygen to the flight muscles, as a result of the thorax being blocked by mites, explains why bees lose their ability to fly. In addition, a general weakening of the host insect is observed as a result of the presence of toxins released by the parasites and the loss of hemolymph.

The lifespan of an infested bee is approximately 30% shorter than that of a healthy bee, as demonstrated by the work of Bailey and, more recently, by Wilson.

CLINICAL SIGNS. The clinical signs of varroatosis are not always present and are generally only evident when infestation levels are very high (more than 50%). Among the clinical manifestations, we have the following: Bees are observed with dislocated wings, flapping them without being able to fly, their abdomen is swollen, dead or dying bees in front of the entrance, and some bees are seen climbing on the leaves of grass or other plants. Other bees have the thorax devoid of hairs, making it appear black and shiny. It is also noticeable that infested bees have lost their instinct to sting. This syndrome appears in bees at low temperatures in the shade, in highly infested colonies that have undergone a prolonged period of confinement. However, it is not exclusive to varroatosis, as it can also be observed in cases of hunger, poisoning from insecticides, or excessive consumption of fermented foods, sudden changes in ambient temperature, or in cases of other diseases such as Nosema, Amebiasis, and paralysis.

DIAGNOSIS. Although the time of year, weather conditions, and the clinical picture (how the disease is observed) can help with the diagnosis, it cannot be definitively established in the field. The help of a laboratory is needed. Therefore, to establish it, a yearly sampling of the apiaries is recommended, taking 3 adult bees from the entrance of each hive, which are placed in a 70% alcohol jar. The owner's data and the hive are written on a piece of paper that is placed in the jar and sent to the laboratory.

TREATMENT. The most likely solution for eliminating mites from the thorax in the long term will be the development of bee lines that are resistant to their attack. Currently, various chemotherapies are used, which have advantages and disadvantages. In some cases, some work better than others, but there is no ideal medication yet. More research is needed in this area. Among the products that have been used in Mexico, we have:

Synthetic or Natural Menthol. Menthol crystals can be provided alone or diluted in ethyl alcohol. When used alone, 30 gr. are placed in a nylon bag with a thin nail, which allows the gradual evaporation of the menthol, while preventing the bees from removing it from the hive. The bag should be replaced each time the product has evaporated, and this procedure should be carried out for a couple of months before the main flowering period. The other method is to dilute 400 gr. of menthol crystals in 1 liter of 70% ethyl alcohol. A cloth or other absorbent material is soaked in approximately 80 cc (ml) of the dilution and is placed on the floor of the hive. The treatment should be repeated 4 times every 2 weeks. The advantages of this product are that it is moderately active (keeping the disease levels below 15%) and is easy to apply. The disadvantages are that it is expensive and causes a lot of stress on the bee colonies, which may abandon the hive.

Formic Acid. The commercial product is Apiplus and has registration from SAGARPA, so its use is subject to the instructions of the laboratory that produces it.

Measures to maintain colonies with high populations contribute to controlling the disease. It is necessary to avoid moving populated colonies and queen bees from affected areas to areas free of the disease, to reduce the spread of varroatosis.

Varroatosis is difficult to eradicate once it has become enzootic, therefore, it is recommended that in areas where the disease is prevalent, a sampling of all apiaries be carried out at least once a year, with sufficient time before flowering, to treat all apiaries that show infestation levels of 35% or more, as there is a positive correlation between low productivity and varroasis levels of 35% or more.

## 3.1.2 VARROASIS

Varroasis, or Varroatosis, is an external and contagious parasitic disease that affects both larvae and adult bees. The disease is caused by the Varroa destructor mite (formerly jacobsoni) and is the most feared disease among beekeepers worldwide.

The Varroa destructor mite was first reported in Apis cerana bees on the island of Java in Asia, where there is a natural balance between the parasite and its host. In the 1960s, the infestation of Apis mellifera bees with Varroa was first reported, causing serious damage.

Its spread to the rest of Asia, Europe, North Africa, and the Middle East occurred rapidly due to the mobilization of populated colonies, beekeeping material, and the natural migration of swarms. Subsequently, it was detected in South America, the United States of America, and Canada, possibly through the introduction of bee queens. In Mexico, it was identified in 1992.

Varroatosis affects all three castes of honey bees and their larvae, with a particular predilection for larvae. Apparently, conditions that favor increased physical contact between bees increase the infestation level in a colony:

In Europe and countries with long winters, Varroasis is severely manifested at the end of the cold season. In tropical countries, infestation levels increase during the rainy season. The economic losses caused by Varroasis vary depending on the duration of the infestation, the way the bees are managed, the measures taken to reduce the number of parasites, the presence of other pathogens, and the region where the colonies are located. Generally, the mortality rate in an infested apiary is progressive and increases year after year if no control measures are taken.

The spread of Varroasis from one colony to another or between apiaries is facilitated by swarms that enter colonies freely, as well as by worker bees that return from the field and get lost. The beekeeper can also spread the parasitosis by exchanging frames between colonies, by introducing unknown swarms into a colony, or by changing queens from an infected nursery.

PATHOGENESIS. Once a colony is infested, the Varroa female begins to reproduce. The Varroa female, which has fed on the hemolymph of the infested bee, penetrates a brood cell (approximately 5 to 6 days old) that is about to be capped (24 to 48 hours before capping), with a preference for the larvae of the drones. Two days after capping (48 hours after entering the cell) and apparently after having sucked hemolymph from the larva, the female begins to lay eggs; the female lays 3 to 7 eggs with an interval of about 30 hours between each egg. The eggs give rise to nymphs in 48 hours, generally the first egg produces a male and the others produce females. The nymphs begin to feed on the hemolymph of the larvae and become adults 3 to 4 days after for males and 5 to 6 days for females, so the entire development period takes 5 to 6 days for males and 7 to 8 days for females. The mating of the mites takes place inside the cell before the bee emerges. The males die from starvation (since their mouthparts have turned into an ejaculatory organ) and the females emerge with the bee when it emerges. The Varroa female has a similar spermatheca to that of bees, where it stores the sperm from the male.

In adult bees, the Varroa female searches for the soft, less keratinized areas to perforate them and suck the hemolymph of its host. These areas include the intersegmental membranes of the first abdominal segments, the joints, the base of the wings, and the areas between the head and thorax and between the last two. Therefore, it is common to find them in these areas. After a time (variable), the female lays eggs in a cell with larvae, thus restarting the cycle. The females live for 2 to 8 months inside the hive, depending on the time of year, living less time when the environmental conditions are favorable for honey and pollen collection.

The damage caused by the mites to the bees is of a physical and toxic-infectious nature. Physical damage is caused by the hemolymph that they suck, and toxic-infectious damage is caused by the wounds they create to feed, which provide entry for toxins and the transmission of pathogens such as American Foulbrood, European Foulbrood, and fungal diseases such as chalkbrood and greybrood in larvae, as well as the latter and paralysis in adult bees. Previously, it was believed that physical damage was the main cause of the weakening and death of the colony, but recent studies indicate that physical damage is not as important as the toxic-infectious damage, as it has been shown that Varroa can carry pathogens for bees. In general, a bee infested lives half the time that a healthy bee, therefore, when the number of infested bees in a colony reaches high levels, the damage caused by the disease is dramatic. It is worth mentioning that for the infestation levels of Varroasis in a bee colony to reach high percentages, several months or years are required from the initial invasion, and that the factors environmental, the management of the "race of bees affected play a very important role in the progression, stabilization or eradication of the parasitosis. The African bees have shown to be more resistant to Varroasis than European bees. It is believed that this resistance is due to the fact that both their development and their average lifespan are shorter than those of European bees, which favors less the life cycle of the mite.

Diagnosis in adult bees (David De Jong test): This technique is very simple and inexpensive. To do this, a container is prepared to "filter" bees – which is made from a plastic bottle, with the bottom cut out and a mesh sieve (with 4 mm squares) placed at the end of the mouth. The bees will remain in the bottle, filtered by the mesh sieve, the liquid will enter the wide-mouthed container, and the mites will be on the white cloth, where they can be easily identified. Number of mites collected x 100 / Number of bees in the sample

Field observations have indicated that it is advisable to keep infestations as close to zero as possible, considering that when percentages exceed 10%, a chemical treatment will be necessary as soon as possible.

It is advisable to randomly select one hive out of every 5 for each apiary to obtain an average.

TREATMENT: The fight against this parasite is complicated by several biological characteristics of the mite, which make it difficult to find an ideal treatment. These characteristics include:

*   a) Parasite of both the brood and adult bees.
*   b) Mites develop resistance to drugs very quickly.
*   c) Their metamorphosis is 2 to 2.5 times shorter than that of bees, so the new generations within the capped cells are much more abundant in mites, and especially protected from the acaricides used in the treatment of the disease.

Approximately 150 remedies have been tested to treat the disease, but none are 100% effective. Many chemical products used have undesirable side effects; some are very toxic, while others are carcinogenic, mutagenic, etc.

Since all the substances used act on the mites that are on the bodies of the adult bees, without affecting those inside the cells, the use of an acaricide that kills the mites on the adult bees, in combination with the mite, is recommended.

Removal of capped brood: Removing the brood can be as harmful as the parasite itself, so it is important to choose the correct technique or method to follow. Within these, the most recommended is the "brood comb" method, which is based on the Varroa's behavior, which prefers cells for reproduction by 90% and only 10% by worker bees. Procedure: To do this, a frame with wax-printed cells is used, or a frame made of wax so that the bees build the comb. During the time when the brood is produced, the frame is placed in the brood chamber for 17 days. After this time, the frame is removed and either the larvae and pupae are removed by uncapping and destroyed by pressure washing, or the combs are melted with the brood.

2.  Use of acaricides. Unfortunately, none of the products tested so far have 100% effectiveness, however, several exceed 95%. It is advisable to alternate the use of acaricides in an endemic area year after year, to prevent the parasite from developing resistance. In recent years, various natural substances have been tested for the control of this parasite; these have the advantage of not contaminating the honey and being safe for people. Unfortunately, their effectiveness varies depending on the dose, method of application, and environmental conditions, so the beekeeper must evaluate the levels of Varroa infestation in their hives to determine the success of their treatment when using these products.

Given the diversity of products used to control Varroasis and the potential risks associated with their use, the Ministry of Agriculture, Livestock, Rural Development, Fisheries and Food has implemented mechanisms for the evaluation and registration of acaricides that guarantee effective control of Varroasis, as well as being safe for bees and beekeepers. Among the most effective and with fewer drawbacks, we have:

*   a) Cimiazole (Novartis): Available at a concentration of 17.5%. The recommended dosage is 2 grams of product per hive, administered in sugar syrup.
*   b) Tao-Fluvalinate (Apistán, Novartis): Available in PVC strips impregnated with the active substance. For the treatment of hives, 2 strips of the product per hive are placed between 38 and 48 of each side of the brood chamber and left for 6 to 8 weeks.
*   c) Flumetrina (Bayvarol, Bayer): Also available in PVC strips. In this case, 4 strips per hive are placed, distributed in the brood chamber, and left for 6 to 8 weeks.
*   d) Formic acid (Api-Plus, Pronabive): This substance is a natural product, therefore it does not contaminate the honey, which makes it the only acaricide authorized for the control of Varroa in organic honey production. The commercial presentation is in 80 ml bags of 65% formic acid, with a device that allows for gradual release and optimal concentration inside the hive. For the treatment of hives, one bag of the product is placed in the brood chamber and left for 4 days; after this time, it is replaced with a new one, and so on, until 16 days (4 bags in total) have passed.

2. Amitraz (Colmesán): For its application, the material is moistened with 0.5 ml of the product (per hive) and placed in a lit smoker. When smoke is released, 5 puffs are applied to the hive. To obtain good results, this process should be repeated 3 times with intervals of 5 to 7 days.

It is very important that the beekeeper is aware of the health risks associated with the use of acaricides, so they should not apply these products before the flowering season or during it, to avoid contaminating the products. It is also advisable to establish a regional treatment calendar, ensuring that they are administered simultaneously in 100% of the hives to prevent re-infestation.

It is likely that over time, new alternatives for chemical control will emerge, either with synthetic molecules or from natural products, so the beekeeper should be properly informed about the incorporation of these into the market to maintain control of Varroasis.

The best control of Varroasis can only be achieved through the participation of all producers, carrying out the following practices in an integrated manner:

*   Measurement of Varroa infestation levels.
*   Periodic replacement of queen bees.
*   Application of biological control.
*   Control of re-infestation sources.
*   Removal or merging of weak hives.
*   Treatment with alternating chemical and biological controls.
*   Diagnosis and control of other bee diseases.

## 3.1.3. OTHER BEE TICKS

Several dozen ticks have been associated with honeybees, but so far, none have been shown to have significant pathogenicity. However, this may be due to the lack of research that has been done on these ticks.

Asian ticks can pose a threat to beekeeping (although they are not currently associated with significant damage, except for Varroa destructor), among these are Tropilaelaps ciareae Delfinado Baker and Euvarroa sirhai Delfinado Baker. Both ticks are confined to the tropical part of Asia.

The so-called external bee ticks, such as Acarapis woodi, include Acarapis externus Morgenthaler and neck tick, called this because it lives in the lower area where the thorax and head of the bee meet. Acarapis dorsalis Morgenthaler, which lives in the mesothorax (middle part of the thorax), and Acarapis vagans Schneider, which is often found at the base of the wings or in the first segment of the abdomen of the bees. It seems that these ticks are widely distributed in the world. They feed on the hemolymph of their host, and their pathogenicity has not yet been fully studied.

## 3.1.4. NOSEMIASIS

Also known as Nosemiasis or the disease of spontaneous disappearance, is a parasitic disease of the bee's digestive tract, caused by the protozoan Nosema apis Zander. The disease is highly contagious, and the damage it can cause can be very serious when the infection level is high.

In 1909, Zander demonstrated that spores were the cause of an enzootic disease of bees, which he called Nosemiasis. In 1952, Katznelson and Jamieson opened a new avenue in the fight against the disease by successfully testing fumagillin.

ETIOLOGY. Nosema apis Zander, is a microscopic parasite of the Phylum Protozoa, of the class Sporazoa and the order Microsporidia, which is characterized by the formation of spores, which are resistant stages.

The spores are oval-shaped, approximately 4 to 6 micrometers long and 2 to 4 micrometers wide. Inside a spore, there is a vegetative form of the parasite, which has 2 nuclei and a filament. The filament is 70 times longer than the spore, and is called polar filament. The spore has a micropyle in one of its poles, which allows the vegetative form to exit through the polar filament. The viability of the spores depends on the conditions to which they are exposed. They can remain viable for many months on dry feces, but lose their viability if exposed to temperatures above 37°C or below 11°C or specific fumigants.

EPIDEMIOLOGY. Nosemiasis is considered the most widespread bee disease in the world, and has been found in all countries where beekeeping is practiced.

This disease is exclusive to the three castes of adult honeybees. The disease is latent throughout the year within the hives, and becomes apparent after periods of confinement of the bees within the hive (rain, cold, etc.). The longer the period of confinement, the more severe the manifestation of Nosemiasis, as the infection levels increase significantly due to the close contact between the bees. This is why the disease is so important in countries with very cold and prolonged winters. Apiaries located in humid, cold, or shady places tend to have higher infection levels than those located in dry and sunny places.

Contaminated combs with bee feces are the most important sources of infection, and the carriers of Nosema apis spores from one season to another. Water from flowers and vegetation contaminated with bee feces does not seem to be a factor in the spread of the disease.

Among the conditions that favor the transmission of Nosema, are the use of contaminated equipment in the hives, robbing, and the acquisition of queens from an infected nursery.

Honey is not a source of contamination because the excretion of waste on the combs; this rarely occurs when the cells of the combs are filled and sealed during the active period.

PATHOGENESIS. When the bees cannot leave their hive for several weeks or months, they are forced to defecate on the combs, contaminating them with spores when they are sick. The combs are cleaned by young worker bees, who acquire the disease. The queen acquires it through royal jelly provided by sick nurse bees; drones become infected when they receive food from worker bees through trophallaxis (mouth-to-mouth).

The life cycle of Nosema apis is approximately 7 days, and its initial and final stages are composed of the spore, which serves for the spread of the disease.

After ingestion, the spores reach the bee's stomach or true stomach, where gastric secretions cause an increase in osmotic pressure inside the spores, which facilitates the opening of the micropyle through which the polar filament exits and attaches to the wall of an epithelial cell. The polar filament is a tube with a light, which injects the vegetative or filamentous form of Nosema apis into the epithelial cell. Inside the cell, the parasite enters the plasmodial stage, at which it feeds and reproduces at the expense of the cell; subsequently, it enters the meront stage, then the sporangiospores stage, and finally the spore stage. The epithelial cell is destroyed, and the spores are released into the lumen of the digestive tract. Some spores released, germinate and infect other adjacent epithelial cells; while others pass into the rectum, where they accumulate to be released with the feces.

If the infection of the epithelial cells is stopped (by improving the time or by treatment), the digestive functions of the bee are inhibited for 26-3 weeks, which leads to a progressive weakening and premature death of the host insect. The parasite also moves from the digestive tract to other organs such as the Malpighi tubules, adipose tissue, thoracic muscles, pharyngeal glands, and ovaries, causing dysfunction in all these organs. Infected nurse bees produce little royal jelly or stop producing it, while the queens lay fewer eggs, and their eggs and larvae are less viable. All these damages cause a reduction in the colony population, low productivity, and, in severe cases, the loss of the colony.

CLINICAL PICTURE. In most cases, the disease does not manifest clinically because it is in a chronic state; however, when some signs appear (which is when the problem is serious), they are similar to those of varroa mites; with the addition that sick queens are replaced by bees.

DIAGNOSIS. Since Nosema can be confused with other diseases, the help of the laboratory is essential to establish the diagnosis. The laboratory should report whether the disease is present and at what level of infection. The level of infection is established according to the number of spores found per bee analyzed. Therefore, the severity of the disease is estimated as follows:

TREATMENT. Many drugs have been tested for the treatment of Nosema, but few have been successful. Fumagillin is undoubtedly the best option, with trisulfates being a second option (although their effectiveness is less than 60% compared to fumagillin). However, these medications affect human health due to their residue in honey, so their use has been prohibited. Treatments also involve measures of cleaning and fumigation of the equipment, so they are expensive, so they are only recommended for colonies when the infection is present.

Levels of infection of 5 million spores per bee (moderate infection) or higher.

- 1 Fumagillin: Is an antibiotic obtained from the fungus Aspergillus fumigatus; it is an imported product that is commercially sold as Fumidil B or Nosema-X. Fumagillin is 100% effective against the vegetative form of Nosema apis, but it does not destroy the parasite's spores, so the infection cannot be completely eliminated, but it can be controlled. It is recommended to administer a syrup of water and sugar containing 25 mg of the active product per liter. 4 liters of syrup should be provided to each colony (100 mg in total).
2. Trisulfates. Combination of antibiotics (sulfates), which can be used if fumagillin is not available. There are several commercial brands, but the most used is ESB-3 from Ciba. 7 g of the commercial product should be administered in a liter of syrup.
- 3 Fumigation of the equipment. Combs from infected colonies can be treated with the gases released by a dilution of acetic acid at 80% (4 parts of glacial acetic acid per 1 of water), the gases of this product destroy the spores of Nosema apis. The procedure consists of cubing the combs and placing a cloth soaked in 150 cc (ml) of the product on the heads of the frames of each body of the hive. After a week, the combs will be free of spores. These fumigations also control varroa mites.

It is important to mention that the use of chemotherapies and the fumigation of the equipment will not have the desired effects if good management practices are not followed.

Among the management practices that favor the control of the disease are the following: changing the queen every year, removing old combs, artificial feeding during periods of scarcity, painting the hives in different colors, placing the apiaries in well-drained sites without excessive shade; uniting weak colonies, etc.

Treatments, fumigations and management measures should be continuous in queen rearing nurseries when the problem is enzootic. And the sampling and diagnosis of all apiaries should be a routine practice as long as the disease exists, in order to be able to make appropriate control decisions.

## 3.1.5. AMOEBASIS

Amebiasis or Amebosis, is a parasitic infection of the Malpighi tubules of adult bees, caused by the protozoan Malpighamoeba mellificae Prell. The disease is contagious, and its severity is still debated; most authors do not consider it important.

Maassen in 1916 in Germany was the first to observe the parasite. In 1928, Prell described and classified the protozoan.

ETIOLOGY. Malpighamoeba mellificae Prell, is a microscopic parasite of the Phylum of Protozoa and the order of Sarcodines, which is characterized by the formation of cysts as a stage of resistance.

The parasites are extracellular and feed by pseudopods, although it seems that they also possess flagella that help them reach the Malpighi tubules. The cysts are round and oval, with a diameter of 5 to 8 micrometers.

The cysts survive for more than 6 months in the feces of bees in the combs, but they are susceptible to common disinfectants.

EPIDEMIOLOGY. The disease is widely distributed in Europe, Oceania, and America.

Amebiasis is almost exclusive to worker bees, as it is very difficult for the queen and drones to become infected. The source of infection and the mechanisms of transmission, as well as the factors that favor the development of the disease, are virtually the same as those of Nosema.

PATHOGENESIS. The life cycle of Malpighamoeba mellificae lasts between 22 and 24 days, and its initial and final stages are composed of its resistance and dissemination form, which is the cyst. Once ingested, the cysts reach the bee's stomach, where gastric juices favor their germination and release of the vegetative form, which occurs at the pylorus, where a lot of solid matter from the food accumulates. This solid matter acts as a "plug", causing the parasites to migrate into the Malpighi tubules, which empty into the pylorus. Once in the Malpighi tubules, the protozoa acquire their amoeboid form, attach to the epithelium, and begin to feed with the help of their pseudopods. The parasites multiply by binary fission, and after 3 to 4 weeks, many epithelial cells of the tubules have been destroyed and released the parasites' cysts. The cysts can infect other cells or pass into the intestine and then into the rectum, where they are excreted with the feces.

So far, only 8 has been proven the presence and damage of Malpighamoeba mellificae exclusively in the Malpighi tubules (organ of excretion that acts as a complement to other diseases such as Nosema), resulting in severe damage. More studies are needed in this regard.

CLINICAL PICTURE. No signs have been described so far.

DIAGNOSIS. It requires the laboratory to establish it clearly. A dissection of the digestive tract of suspected bees allows to see the cysts through the walls of the Malpighi tubules with a microscope at 400X. This is feasible, since the walls of the tubules are inflamed and become transparent.

TREATMENT. There are no chemical products to treat it, but sulfas have some action on the parasite. The use of fumigation with acetic acid as in Nosema has proven to be very effective in decontaminating the combs, but good management as recommended for Nosema is the most advisable.

## 3.1.6. Gregarinosis

This is a contagious parasitic infection of the digestive tract and Malpighian tubules of adult bees, caused by various species of protozoa belonging to the Gregarinidae family. Some authors do not consider them parasites, but rather commensals, but even those who do consider them true parasites, do not believe they are highly pathogenic to bees. However, very little research has been done in this area, so it is difficult to draw any definitive conclusions.

The first observations of Gregarinus in honeybees were made by Morgenthaler in 1926 in Switzerland.

**Etiology:** Several species of the genera *Monoica*, *Apigregarina*, *Acuta*, and *Leidyana* are responsible, with *Leidyana apis* being the most common. Gregarinus are protozoa belonging to the class Sporazoa and the order Macrosporidia, forming spores as a means of resistance and dissemination.

They are the largest protozoa known to be associated with honeybees. The spores are oval and refractile, measuring approximately 85 micrometers in length and 35 micrometers in width. The vegetative form resembles a pear, with the anterior end being narrower than the posterior end, which contains the nucleus, measuring approximately 44 micrometers in length and 16 micrometers in width. The spores die by freezing and are susceptible to common disinfectants.

**Epidemiology:** This disease has been reported in almost all European countries, and in North America, it has been diagnosed in Canada, the United States, the Guianas, and Venezuela. In Mexico, Dr. Franco Meza found parasites that matched the description of Gregarinus in bees from the state of Campeche, but their correct identification could not be established. It is likely that they are quite widespread. The presence of Gregarinus has not been reported in Central America.

Gregarinus are considered occasional parasites of bees, as they also parasitize other insect species, such as cockroaches and moths. These insects may serve as reservoirs and vectors for the disease. The factors that favor the development of the parasitic infection, as well as the mechanisms of transmission, are the same as those for Nosemosis.

**Pathology:** The lifespan of Gregarinus is not precisely known. After ingestion, the spores germinate in the bee's proventriculus in a similar way to Nosema apis, and the vegetative form attaches to the epithelium of the proventriculus and Malpighian tubules, using a structure called an epimeron located on its anterior end. The parasite begins to feed on the cells in its trophont stage, then progresses to the sporangont stage, and finally to the spore stage, which are eliminated with the bee's excretions. Previously, it was believed that the number of spores was limited by the number of parasites ingested, but it is now known that, although to a limited extent, the parasite reproduces in the bee's digestive tract.

The pathogenicity of the parasites is controversial, but it is likely that it is significant at high levels of infection.

**Clinical Signs:** There are no known clinical signs associated with this disease. In 1965, Stejskal reported mortality in bees infected in Venezuela, but their work did not provide conclusive data, so further research is needed.

**Diagnosis:** Can be established using the same technique used for Nosemosis in the laboratory.

**Treatment:** The same as for Nosemosis.

## 3.1.7. Flagellosis

This is a parasitic infection of the intestines of adult bees, caused by various species of flagellated protozoa belonging to the genera *Leptomonas* and *Crithidia*. There is no evidence of damage to honeybees. Lotmar in 1946 was the first to report flagellosis in honeybees, by isolating *Leptomonas apis* from these bees.

**Etiology:** Several species of flagellated protozoa belonging to the genera *Leptomonas* and *Crithidia*, with *Leptomonas apis* and *Crithidia mellificae* being the most common. The parasites have an oval and elongated shape, possess flagella as a means of locomotion, and measure between 5 and 30 micrometers.

**Epidemiology:** Its presence has been confirmed in Europe and Australia. Its presence in North America is unknown, but it is likely to exist.

All three castes of honeybees can be infected, and the mechanisms of contagion and dissemination are unknown.

**Pathology:** Apparently, bees are infected by ingesting the cysts of the flagellated protozoa. Although the time of their life cycle is unknown, it is known that the parasites can be found moving freely in the pyloric region of the bee's digestive tract, between 6 and 12 days of age. The flagellates form rosettes and adhere to the epithelium of the intestine and rectum, on its "costas" (edges).

**Clinical Signs:** None have been reported to date.

**Diagnosis:** A careful dissection of the digestive tract is required to search for the parasites in the pyloric region and to observe the lesions in the intestine (costras). No laboratory in the world performs this diagnosis routinely; it has only been done on a research level.

**Treatment:** None currently exist.

## 3.2 Bacterial Diseases of Adult Bees

## 3.2.1. Septicemia

Septicemia (or Pseudomoniasis) is an infectious disease of adult bees, caused by the bacterium *Pseudomonas apis* Burnside. The severity of the disease has not been well established, although it can kill infected bees quickly under certain conditions.

**Etiology:** *Pseudomonas apis* Burnside, a gram-negative bacterium similar to *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, is widely distributed in the environment.

**Epidemiology:** This disease has only been reported in France, Switzerland, Canada, and the United States, but it is likely to be worldwide.

It exclusively affects adult honeybees. Humidity and excessive handling are factors that favor the presence and development of the disease. The way in which bees become infected is not entirely clear, but it appears that the infection begins in wounds caused by *Acarapis woodi*, in the trachea. The mite may be a vector for the bacteria. The presence of the bacteria has been demonstrated in pollen and in the soil where the sick colonies are located.

**Pathology:** From the trachea, the bacteria enters the hemolymph, where it multiplies, causing "septicemia" and the death of the insect. Young mites that feed on the hemolymph of infected bees can carry the bacteria to healthy bees. Once infected, bees die within 2 days.

**Clinical Signs:** Dying bees are found resting almost motionless in the combs or in the brood chamber, they do not feed and are unable to fly. Dead bees easily fall apart in the hands when lifted (the segments and appendages, such as the head, abdomen, legs, and wings, detach). The color of their hemolymph is yellowish and turbid, and sometimes the affected bees emit a rotten odor.

**Diagnosis:** Can be established based on the clinical signs, but can also be performed in the laboratory.

**Treatment:** Streptomycin has been used with some success, although there have been cases of bacterial resistance to the antibiotic. Another possibility is the use of citric acid in the syrup.

## 3.3 Fungal Diseases of Adult Bees

## 3.3.1. Aspergillosis

This is the same disease as Chalkbrood in larvae. It is not considered a major problem, as the number of adult bees affected is always low. The etiology and epizootiology of this fungal disease in adults are the same as in Chalkbrood.

Regarding the pathology, adult bees ingest the fungal spores with their food, which develops in the presence of favorable factors and from the digestive tract, invading all the organs and structures of the abdomen, causing the death of the host.

When still alive, the affected bees show signs of paralysis and "nervousness." The abdomen appears swollen and dark in color. Dead bees have a hardened abdomen of dark color and do not decompose.

The diagnosis can be made in the field based on the signs, and can also be performed in the laboratory by identifying the conidiophores of the fungi, although it is difficult to carry out.

The treatment is the same as for Chalkbrood, as well as the preventive measures.

## 3.3.2. MELANOSIS

This is an infectious fungal disease that affects the reproductive system of queen bees, causing sterility. It is caused by the fungus *Melanosella apis*.

ETIOLOGY: *Melanosella apis* is a fungus very similar to yeasts, and its taxonomy has not yet been determined. Due to its similarity to yeasts, the name of the disease starts with "H" from "hefe," which in German means yeast.

EPIDEMIOLOGY: The worldwide distribution of this fungus is unknown; however, it has been found in Germany and England.

It affects queen bees of honeybees, although worker bees are often carriers of the fungus; which enters the hive with pollen. It is suggested that the fungus is saprophytic under normal conditions and, like Ascosferosis and Aspergillosis, requires certain predisposing factors to develop; these factors may be the same as those for these diseases.

PATHOGENESIS: It is not known exactly how the queens become infected, but it appears that they ingest the fungus with royal jelly provided by nurse bees. Fyg also suggests that the fungus penetrates through the brood cell: Regardless of the entry point, the fungus lodges in the reproductive organs of the queens, causing a dark coloration (melanosis) and causing sterility, apparently due to a strong inflammation that obstructs the oviducts. The queens stop laying eggs, their reproductive organs atrophy, and are replaced by new queens.

DIAGNOSIS: It is based on the lesions in the reproductive organs (melanosis) of the queens and on the identification of the fungus in the laboratory. There is no clinical picture that can guide the diagnosis.

TREATMENT: There is no known treatment other than replacing the queen, although this is usually done naturally by the bees themselves.

## 3.3.3. OTHER BEES' FUNGI

Some other fungi have been shown to be pathogenic to bees; including those in the genera *Rhizopus*, *Scopulariopsis*, and *Trichoderma*; whose mycelia kill larvae and adult bees when present in hives and under certain conditions.

One of the most commonly found saprophytic fungi in hives is *Bettsia alvei*, also known as the "pollen fungus." *Bettsia alvei* grows in cells where pollen is stored; giving the appearance of cotton or a web on these cells. *Penicillium* fungi are also quite common.

Some of the yeasts that can be found include those in the genera *Saccharomyces* and *Torulopsis*, which sometimes cause pathogens, especially for larvae, although they can also kill adult bees less frequently.

## 3.4. BEES' VIRAL DISEASES AND RICKETSIOSIS

## 3.4.1. PARALYSIS

Paralysis, or Black Bee Syndrome, is an infectious and contagious disease of adult bees, caused by two types of viruses: the acute paralysis virus and the chronic paralysis virus. This disease has some economic importance.

In 1963, Bailøy identified the chronic paralysis virus in electron microscopy; and in 1964, the acute paralysis virus.

ETIOLOGY: Chronic Paralysis: Irregular RNA virus, 65-90 nm in diameter. Can be cultivated in bee nerve cells and chicken embryo cells. Possesses specific antigenic proteins that allow its identification through gel diffusion tests.

Acute Paralysis: Hexagonal virus, RNA type, 28 nm in diameter, very similar to *Morator aetatulas*, so until 1964, it was believed to be the same virus. Also possesses specific proteins that allow its identification with gel diffusion tests.

EPIDEMIOLOGY: This virosis has been diagnosed in Europe, Asia, and the Americas, including Mexico, Australia, New Zealand, China, and Scandinavia.

Its presence is favored during hot periods, especially in colonies with highly inbred queens. The exact natural mode of infection is unknown, but the large number of viral particles found in the pharyngeal and salivary glands of bees suggests that transmission occurs through trophalaxia or through the ingestion of contaminated food.

PATHOGENESIS: Once ingested, the virus passes from the digestive tract to the nervous and adipose tissues, where it multiplies. It is particularly concentrated in the head area. It is possible that the symptoms are triggered by high temperatures inside the hive. Bees die in 3-5 days when infected with the acute paralysis virus, and in 7 days when infected with the chronic paralysis virus.

CLINICAL PICTURE: The same as for Nosemosis and Varroatosis.

DIAGNOSIS: The clinical picture can guide the diagnosis, especially in the presence of "black" bees (without hairs on the thorax), trembling, but a definitive diagnosis cannot be made in the field.

In the laboratory, the disease can be reproduced by inoculating healthy bees with macerates of "sick" bees for a positive diagnosis (see section on Laboratory Techniques).

Another way to identify the virus is through cultures and gel diffusion tests, as well as by observing it under an electron microscope.

TREATMENT: There are no specific antiviral drugs available, but experimental studies have shown that both Oxitetracillin and common sugar can inhibit the development of the virus, so a possible option would be to feed the bees with a syrup containing 300 mg of pure Oxitetracillin salt.

However, the most recommended option is to replace the queen. It is important for beekeepers to consider preventing inbreeding in their queen breeding programs to obtain hybrid bees.

## 3.4.2. VIROSIS

Some viruses associated with chronic paralysis of bees include: the "black cell" virus, the "filamentous" virus, and the "Iridiscent" virus, which are closely associated with *Nosema apis Z*.

The "black cell" virus is particularly important. The walls of the cells acquire a brownish to dark black color. It contains pupae with many viral particles. In the early stages, infected pupae have a pale yellow appearance and a hard, sac-like skin, resembling dead sacciform larvae. These symptoms are more pronounced when many real cells are bred in queen breeding colonies.

The three viruses have been found in Great Britain, North America, and Australia.

The "black cell" disease has been present in Mexico for many years.

Although other viruses that cause significant diseases in honeybees have not yet been found in recurring forms, some viruses have been reported to have some pathogenicity. In 1975, Bailey reported the existence of small 17 nm viruses, which he called "satellite viruses" because they were found around the chronic paralysis virus. In the same year and in the USA, Bailøy reported another virus associated with paralysis, the "Arkansas" virus, a hexagonal RNA virus of 27 nm in diameter, which slowly killed the bees inoculated.

In 1970, Kulincevic and associates noted that this virus shortened the lifespan of bees in a certain part. The "X" virus, also known as the "Iridiscent" virus, which is associated with *Malphagoeba melliferae*, was also found. More recently, Bailey found a 160 nm DNA virus in *Apis corana* bees from India, which he called the "Iridiscent" virus. However, it can also multiply in *Apis mellifera*.

Other viruses are the "nubbed wings" virus. Colonies affected die quickly. It is found in Great Britain.

**3.5. SPREAD OF PARASITIC DISEASES**

The problem of diseases and parasites in beekeeping has taken a drastic turn since humans have made it possible to shorten distances through airplanes and other modes of transportation. Most of these diseases, pests, and parasites were limited to specific areas, but they have now spread throughout the world thanks to people with little regard for ethical considerations, who are only interested in personal profit. The search for bees that produce more honey has led to the import of diseases of all kinds. Bringing germplasm from other parts of the world is not worth it if there is a risk of importing a disease. These diseases generally do not have biological control mechanisms (natural enemies or predators) in their new location, which can cause significant damage to the beekeeping industry. It is time for beekeepers around the world to realize that the best bees are those that exist in their own lands. The necessary improvements can be achieved by using local breeds and subjecting them to a well-planned selection process. Simply avoiding the import of live material from other parts of the world could give us an advantage over other countries in the beekeeping industry.

**3.6. PREVENTATIVE MEASURES FOR DISEASES OF ADULT BEES**

Among the most important, we can mention the following:

- 1. Take all the recommended measures to prevent diseases in the brood.
- 3. Avoid importing queens and biological material as much as possible, only importing from Canada and the United States with a certificate issued by the Animal Health authorities.
- 2. When changing the queen each year, we must ensure that she comes from a certified breeder in SAGARPA, due to her genetic and health quality, where there is control to avoid inbreeding and the presence of diseases (In some countries, queen breeders are obligated to provide a health certificate for each queen to the buyer).
- 4. It is necessary to carry out an annual sampling and diagnosis of adult bees in each apiary. Ideally, this should be done 3 to 4 months before the main flowering period.
- 6. Avoid indiscriminate exchange of honeycombs between hives.
- 5. Do not introduce unknown swarms into the hives.
- 7. Place the hives and apiaries in locations where there is no high humidity, strong winds, or extreme temperatures.

**4. BEESWORM INFESTATIONS**

Beeswarm infestations, also known as wax moths or sawflies, are insects of the Lepidoptera order that cause significant economic losses to beekeepers worldwide each year due to the large number of combs they destroy. In Central America, this problem is particularly serious, as the tropical climate of the region is the most favorable for the development of this pest.

The presence of moths in the hives is due to the negligence of the beekeeper, as they only attack weak or abandoned colonies, or they appear in stored combs that were not fumigated or were not fumigated correctly.

The moths that cause the most damage to the combs are the greater wax moth (Galleria mellonella) and the lesser wax moth (ACHROIA grisella). The female moths lay their eggs on the combs, from which larvae emerge that eat the center of the comb cells, eventually destroying them completely.

The larva leaves tunnels and a white fluff as it progresses. These tunnels are easy to detect when examining the combs. The larvae are white-gray in color. After that, the larva spins a thick, white cocoon in the wood of the frames or boxes, for its transformation into a pupa. The cocoons leave marked impressions on the wood, which also deteriorates the equipment. The adults measure almost one inch, with wings wider than their body.

**TREATMENT.** The best weapon to control this pest is strong colonies, so the beekeeper's management should focus on achieving this. A strong colony will defend itself and destroy the moth.

Stored combs are very exposed to the attack of the moth, especially those used for brood rearing. Empty combs should be stored in boxes inside a shed. Boxes with combs should be stacked in groups of 6 to 12 on a flat surface. The spaces between the boxes should be sealed with "masking tape" and the entire stack should be covered with plastic to prevent air and the escape of product gases. Place 100 grams of paradichlorobenzene on a piece of cardboard or paper on top of the head of the frames of the upper box. If "masking tape" is used, seal it with tape. If plastic is used, no additional box is needed. The treatment should be repeated every 2 weeks to destroy the larvae that have hatched, as paradichlorobenzene does not act on the eggs.

Before placing the frames back in the hives, they should be left to air for two days.

Stored combs can also be treated with cultures of Bacillus thuringiensis, which is commercially available as Certar-M. This bacterium attacks the larvae of all lepidopterans and controls the population to the point that the combs can be left outdoors without significant damage.

Exposing the combs to temperatures of ~7 C. for 5 hours, or -15 C. for 2 hours, eliminates all stages of the larvae. This is very effective in controlling larvae in honey production in frames. In this way, the problem of larvae is completely eliminated without having to expose the combs to any chemicals. The combs can be placed in plastic bags, placed in the refrigerator and then can be removed and stored for a considerable time, as long as the bags are not opened.

**4.2. ANT INFESTATIONS**

Some species of ants can invade hives, but if the colony is strong enough and has access to all corners of the hive, it can repel the ants. The chemicals used to repel them are varied and are distributed by companies that sell agricultural chemicals. It is important to use them sparingly, as they can affect the bees.

Another way to control ant attacks is to place the hives on bases that rest inside containers or containers filled with water.

**4.3. DIPTERA**

**4.3.1. MAVIS**

Mavis are parasitosis caused by flies. Although cases of flies or their larvae parasitizing bees have been reported in many parts of the world, little is known about their life cycle, epizootiology, and control. Among the genera of flies known as parasites of bees in some areas of the region are Wintemia and Melaloncha.

In general, the parasitosis can affect adult bees or larvae.

1. Mavis in adult bees: Some species of flies lay their larvae on the thorax or abdomen of bees, apparently preferring to lay their eggs on weakened or dying bees. The larvae of the flies penetrate the exoskeleton, settling inside the bee's tissues and feeding on them.

2. Mavis in bee larvae: Gravid flies manage to evade the vigilance of the bees and penetrate into the brood nest, where they lay their larvae on the inside of the cells and directly on the bee larvae, which feed on the bee larvae.

**CONTROL** As we do not know much about this problem and its severity, only some management measures can be recommended, such as: Trying to keep hives well populated, closing the entrances to the hive during periods of high incidence of parasitism, keeping the hives in good condition, without many openings, etc.

**4.3.2. BEE LOUSE**

The so-called "bee louse" is actually a wingless dipteran. Braula coeca is not considered a threat to beekeeping, although it can reduce the queen's laying if she carries many individuals.

The bee is attached to the bee's hairs and feeds on the pollen and nectar that it takes from the bee's proboscis. The queen lays her eggs on the cells' caps that contain honey, and the larvae create small tunnels in the wax, spend a period of pupation, and then develop into adults.

Its presence has been confirmed in Mexico, and it is likely also present in other tropical countries.

CONTROL. Only if there are a large number of bees, it is recommended to treat the colony with products such as Fenothiazine or tobacco leaves in a smoker.

## 4.4. REPTILES

Among the reptiles that can be a pest for honeybees are lizards, frogs, and other reptiles. Frogs are the most harmful predators, able to eat more than 300 bees each in 24 hours. To avoid this problem, the hives are raised 3/4 of a meter off the ground. This also helps to improve the situation, as herbs do not obstruct the bees' access to the hive. Another way to control them is to place a wooden fence with chicken wire at least 60 cm high around the apiary, or a piece of mosquito netting along the bottom of the first one, wide enough to prevent the frogs' tongues from reaching in.

There are many other bee predators and pests of lesser importance, except in very localized areas.

## 4.5. AETHINA TUMIDA

The beetle *Aethina tumida* Murray (also known as the Small Bee Beetle) is an invasive pest of honeybees in North America and has caused the loss of thousands of colonies in the United States. It is native to Africa, where it is considered a secondary pest of beekeeping, similar to the wax moths in America.

ETIOLOGY. The adult beetle is small, measuring approximately 5 millimeters in length and 3 mm in width (about one-third the size of a worker bee). It has an oval shape, and its color varies from brown to black, being darker as it matures. Its legs are hard and covered with fine bristles (which make it difficult to collect by hand); at the end of the antennae, it has a characteristic lobe. During the first few days, the beetles are very active, moving quickly inside the hive, between the combs, under the lids, and at the bottom of the walls. Afterwards, they reduce their activity and remain in the less illuminated parts of the hive. On average, the females lay their eggs about a week after mating. The bee beetle eggs are pearly white and measure 1.4 mm in length and 0.26 mm in width, and they are deposited in irregular masses and, generally, in cavities or small holes in any part of the interior of the hive. The incubation period varies from 1 to 6 days. The larvae are pale, have 3 pairs of small legs near the head, unlike the wax moth larvae (which can be confused with them, as they are often found in joint infestations), which have small pairs of legs evenly distributed along the body. The larval development lasts for about 10 to 14 days and can measure from 5 mm to 1.2 cm in length and width. Once developed, the larva moves towards the hive entrance to emerge; it buries itself and continues its pupal stage. The period that the pupa remains on the ground varies and can last between 15 and 60 days (depending on the type of soil), although it feeds on pollen and bee larvae.

The beetle is found in tropical and subtropical Africa. In May 1888, a beekeeper in Santa Lucia, Murray, discovered *Aethina tumida*.

It is now found in Florida and along the coasts of Georgia and South Carolina. When exposed to a type of soil, it does not harm the bees. It has been found in stored equipment.

The spread of the beetle can occur by moving colonies with capped brood or by doubling colonies, both of which can be carriers.

PATHOGENY. In South Africa, *Aethina tumida* is not considered a major problem, and effective control measures are available. In the United States, it has caused the death of thousands of bee colonies. The adult beetles feed on pollen and generally do not cause significant damage. The larvae, which develop rapidly, damage the wax of the combs and destroy the brood. They can also destroy empty combs that are not properly stored.

The damage is usually more severe when the problem is combined with the presence of other pathogens, such as *Vespa*.

In South Africa, the main problems occur when the hives remain in the field for a long time before extraction, as this is when the larvae are most vulnerable.

CLINICAL PICTURE. When the adult beetles first enter a hive, they are difficult to see. However, after a while, they are easily noticeable on any part of the hive or under the floor. In severe infestations, hundreds or even thousands of larvae can be seen crawling on the frames and on the bottom of the hive in search of food. The destruction of combs and the leakage of fermented honey are evident.

DIAGNOSIS. In the field, the diagnosis can be made by identifying the adult beetles or by observing the clinical picture. In these cases, it is advisable to send a sample of the adult beetles or larvae to the laboratory for identification. Because infestations often occur jointly with wax moths ( *Galleria melonella*), and because the larvae of both are very similar at certain stages, there may be confusion in identification. Therefore, it is important to highlight some differences between the two: In terms of behavior, the wax moth larvae spin silky galleries and avoid light, while the bee beetle larvae do not form pupae inside the hive and it is common to see them moving towards the light to leave the colony and continue their life cycle. Because of this, it is possible to see bee beetle larvae even several meters away from the colony, searching for a suitable place to bury themselves, which never happens with wax moth larvae. Regarding their anatomy, the bee beetle larvae have only three pairs of legs near their head, and their maximum length is less than 1.5 cm, while the wax moth larvae have six pairs of legs along their entire body and reach a size much larger than that of the bee beetle. Finally, the cuticle of the bee beetle larvae is very resistant, as they can be pressed with the tip of a finger, unlike the wax moth larvae, which can be easily torn with a slight pressure.

CONTROL. Chemical control is complicated, as there are no products that have been proven to be effective. It is important to consider that control must be carried out in two ways: A) control of the adult beetles in the hives and B) treatment of the floor to destroy the pupae. In the United States, the use of Coumaphos impregnated in plastic strips (CheckMite+ from Bayer) has been authorized in some areas for the control of adult beetles inside the hives. For floor treatment, Permetrina (GardStar) is used, which is emulsified and applied with a low-pressure sprayer around the hive.

The best control is to maintain strong and active colonies, in which the bees themselves keep the number of bee beetle larvae low. Therefore, the management of the apiary will be crucial to prevent damage. To achieve this, beekeepers must pay attention to the following aspects:

- Ensure a clean environment around the hive and inside it.
- Avoid creating empty spaces inside the hive.
- Rotate the combs regularly.
- Do not leave empty equipment in the field, as it can serve as a reservoir for the beetle.
- Although it has been suggested that ants may control the larvae in the soil, the practice indicates that introducing ants is more likely to invade the colony.
- The most vulnerable stage of the *Aethina tumida* beetle is when it enters the hive to pupate, so one of the control measures that could be developed in the future is the development of traps for bee beetle larvae.

For stored equipment, the same measures to prevent wax moth infestation should be used.

## 8. LABORATORY TECHNIQUES FOR DIAGNOSING BEE DISEASES AND PARASITES

## INTRODUCTION

One of the most important aspects of beekeeping is disease control. Beekeepers and those who manage hives need to be able to identify the signs of disease in their bees. This section will provide readers with some of the techniques used to diagnose common bee diseases.

## 6. BEE DISEASES

The accurate diagnosis of any bee disease depends on the sample taken. Table 1 describes some of the most important signs in the diagnosis of bee diseases, under field conditions (see appendix).

First, always look for larvae or pupae with signs that are characteristic of the disease. Do not destroy any larvae or pupae without first examining them carefully. Note the sample, whether it is larvae or pupae, their position in the cell, and their color. Remember that no single sign should be taken as definitive proof of a disease. Laboratory tests for brood diseases and Varroa mites require special sera or an electron microscope; therefore, most laboratories rely on clinical signs for diagnosis.

Often, the remnants of larvae are difficult to find because the combs may be damaged over time. Dead larvae can be easily located using ultraviolet light with a long wavelength (365 nm) or ultraviolet radiation. Exposure to UV light between 3100 and 4000 units stimulates the fluorescence of the larval cuticle. The results of this technique should be interpreted with caution, as both honey and pollen also fluoresce. However, in some cases, accurate diagnosis requires culturing techniques in which the pathogen grows in a artificial medium and is identified through biochemical tests (see appendix, "Cultivation of bee-associated microorganisms").

## 6.2.1 AMERICAN FOOK AND EUROPEAN FOOK

## Hanging Drop Technique

a) Preparing the Smear. Suspicious larvae are mixed with a drop of distilled water on a clean slide until a cloudy film is formed. These remnants can be pre-suspended in water and then smeared on the slide. To fix the smear, heat the slide with the smeared side up over a lamp or flame. Place enough immersion oil on a microscope slide that is twice the width of the slide. Make sure the smear is completely dry before staining.

b) Staining the Smear. Stain the smear with fuchsin-phénix (see appendix) for 5 to 7 seconds. Ensure that enough dye is applied to the slide to stain the entire smear. Be careful not to stain the side of the smear, but the other. Excess dye can be removed with tap water.

c) Preparing the Lamination. Once the slide is wet, place it quickly, smear-side down, on the previously prepared slide with immersion oil. Then, proceed with examining the slide under the microscope.

d) Examining the Lamination. Use the high-power objective of the microscope. The Modified Hanging Drop Technique is particularly useful for differentiating American and European foots. Look for areas where the water has pooled between the oil droplets, focusing the microscope to detect the presence of floating spores. Only the spores of Bacillus larvae, the bacterium that causes American foots, show Brownian motion. This is a very valuable characteristic, as the spores of Bacillus alvei, which causes European foots, are also present, but they are fixed to the slide. In addition, the morphological characteristics of the spores allow for a near-instant diagnosis.

This technique also allows for direct examination to look for Melissococcus pluton, if Bacillus alvei is not present. (See Table II of biochemical tests for differentiating Bacillus spp. and also see figures 24 and 25).

Bacillus larvae. The bacterium that causes American foots, is a thin rod with slightly rounded ends and a tendency to grow in chains. It varies in length from 2.5 to 5 micrometers and has a width of about 0.5 micrometers. The bacterium's spore is oval and approximately twice as long as it is wide, measuring 0.6 by 1.3 micrometers. The spore is stained a dark purple-red color on the outer edges and lighter in the center (Fig. 24).

Melissococcus pluton. This bacterium is the initial cause of European foots. It is observed in pairs or chains and measures 0.5 by 1.0 micrometers (Fig. 25). This bacterium does not form spores.

Bacillus alvei. This is a rod-shaped organism that measures approximately 0.5 to 0.8 micrometers in width and 2.0 to 5.0 micrometers in length. Its spores measure 0.8 by 1.8 to 2.2 micrometers (Fig. 25).

Bacillus laterosporus. Previously, this organism was known as Bacillus orpheus. It can occasionally be found in the dead larvae of European foots. The rods measure 0.5 to 0.8 by 2.0 to 5.0 micrometers, and the spores measure 1.0 to 1.3 by 1.2 to 1.5 micrometers. It is observed as an oval and elongated organism, with rounded ends, very stained on one side and at the ends. The clear part corresponds to the bacterium's spore (Fig. 25).

Achromobacter (Bacterium) eurydice. This is another bacterium that is frequently found in cases of European foots. It does not form spores and measures 0.5 to 1.4 micrometers in length and 0.4 to 0.7 micrometers in width (Fig. 25).

Another procedure for observing the microorganisms associated with American and European foots is to prepare and fix a smear in a similar way to the hanging drop technique and stain it with Gram stain (see appendix). The staining is carried out as follows:

- Cover the smear with crystal violet solution for 1 minute
- Cover the smear with Lugol's solution for 1 minute
- Remove the dye
- Rinse the smear with running water
- Cover the smear with 95% alcohol for about 30 seconds (decolorization)

- Cover the smear with Ziehl-Neelsen stain for about 30 seconds

- Observe under the microscope (100X) with immersion oil
- Rinse with water and dry

Depending on the type of bacterium, it can be observed with a red (gram-negative) or blue (gram-positive) color. Paenibacillus larvae, Melissococcus pluton, and Bacillus alvei are gram-positive, while Bacillus laterosporus and Achromobacter eurydice are gram-negative. Observe the morphology and size of the bacteria and compare them.

A simple test is the "Holts" test, which consists of placing a larva on a tube of 3 to 4 ml of skim milk and 1% dry powder. The tube is shaken (the milk is digested by the enzymes produced by Bacillus larvae) .

## 6.2 BROOD DISEASE

Because these diseases are caused by American and European foots, microscopic examination is important for their differentiation. Dead larvae of brood disease are relatively free of bacteria; however, this fact alone is not enough for diagnosis. The virus that causes the disease is Morator aetatulas, and it cannot be seen even with a high-powered microscope. Virus identification can only be made through serological tests using diffusion tests, which require a very specific and difficult-to-obtain antiserum, so few laboratories in the world perform this procedure. Another possibility for diagnosis is to infect young larvae from a healthy bee colony.

To do this, the suspect larvae must be macerated with an equal amount of water (1 ml per larva macerated). The resulting slurry is filtered and applied to the healthy bee colony, either by spraying the combs or by providing it in a sugar syrup. A similar procedure should be performed with another healthy colony, but instead of a slurry, water should be used as a control.

A positive diagnosis will be indicated by the reproduction of the disease.

## 6.2.4 FUNGAL DISEASE

For any suspected fungal disease, suspend a small sample in water and macerate it. Place a drop of the macerate on a microscope slide and carefully add a coverslip to prevent bubbles. No staining is required; the slide should be examined with the low-power objective (not more than 40X).

This fungus exhibits both a filamentous and a spore-forming stage (see figure 26). The most useful structure for identification is the sporocyst, which measures 47 to 140 micrometers in diameter.

For larvae and adult bees. This is a rare disease. In adults, the main symptom is the appearance of double wings in the bees, which cannot fly or move properly. The disease in young bees is not caused by infection. After death, the abdomen of the affected larva becomes hard and is very difficult to macerate, hence the name "Stone Larva."

A wet mash prepared from the larva, shows fungal hyphae penetrating throughout the insect's body; eventually, the fungi detach from the insect's inner cuticle, forming a false skin (mummy). At this stage, the larva may be covered with a powdery green substance (fungal spores). Generally, the spores are present in large numbers, near the tip of the larva's head.

Because many fungi normally inhabit beehives, it is necessary to correctly identify Aspergillus flavus. Other species of Aspergillus can also cause Stone Larva.

## 6.3. DISEASES OF ADULT BEES

## 6.3.1. PARALYSIS

Caused by a virus. This is a difficult problem, and in severe cases, it can reduce honey production.

Affected bees are observed trembling on the frames, and they are also unable to fly. In severe cases, many bees are observed crawling on the bottom of the hive. These bees are often weak and may die.

Because not all laboratories have the equipment to perform this type of test, diagnosis can be made based on the symptoms and by replicating the disease in healthy bees. Bees can be infected by feeding them or injecting them with an extract made from sick bees. The extract is prepared by macerating the bees in water, at a ratio of one bee per ml of water. The macerate is centrifuged and filtered.

If you choose to feed the bees, 2 ml of the extract should be mixed with an equal volume of sugar syrup and given to 20 bees in a small container. If you choose to inject, use a micropipette and a needle with a diameter of 30. Inject 1 microliter of the extract into each bee, which should have been previously anesthetized with carbon dioxide. The procedure involves inserting the tip of the needle through the membrane separating the two abdominal segments.

After injection, the bees should be kept at 30°C and provided with sugar syrup as needed. The symptoms of paralysis appear around 6 days after injection. It is important to monitor by injecting a batch of healthy bees.

## 6.3.3. NOSEMA

This disease is an intestinal parasitosis of adult bees, caused by the protozoan Nosema apis (Zander). The spores of Nosema apis are oval-shaped and measure 4 to 6 micrometers in length by 2 to 4 micrometers in width. The best time to look for Nosema apis spores is at the beginning of the flowering season, after a prolonged period of confinement (at the end of the rainy season or after the winter).

Method of Cantwell. 25 to 30 bees are taken from each sample and placed on absorbent paper to dry. The abdomens of the bees are then separated using scissors. The bee abdomens are placed in a Petri dish containing 1 ml of water per bee (a total of 25 to 30 ml). These abdomens are macerated with the tip of a clean test tube. Both the test tubes and Petri dishes should be thoroughly cleaned before reuse. Mortars can also be used for maceration.

Preparation of the smear. A small amount of the suspension from the macerated bee abdomens is placed on a microscope slide. A coverslip is then carefully placed over the suspension. The best way to place the coverslip is by touching one end with your finger and placing it at a 45-degree angle to the slide. Then, gently lower the coverslip onto the suspension to avoid trapping air bubbles. The smear is examined under a microscope with at least a 400x magnification (wet mount). The spores are easily distinguished by their bright, refractile appearance.

Counting the spores. If spores are found in the smear, they can be counted using a haemocytometer. Before use, the haemocytometer should be washed. This is done by immersing it in soapy water, rinsing it with running water, and then drying it with a clean cloth.

A small amount of the suspension is taken with a platinum wire or a Pasteur pipette and placed under the coverslip of the haemocytometer until it is filled by capillary action. It is important to fill only the counting chamber of the haemocytometer to ensure the correct volume of fluid is used.

It is also important to ensure that there are no air bubbles under the coverslip. After allowing the spores to settle for 3 minutes, the counting can begin. During this time, the counting area is identified and focused on a 400x magnification (wet mount) or higher. The haemocytometer grid is divided into 16 squares, and each square is bordered by double lines.

All spores enclosed by the double lines are counted, including all spores that touch the double lines on the left and top sides of each square, but not those that touch the double lines on the bottom and right sides. To obtain an accurate average, count spores from 5 squares, the four corners, and the center of the haemocytometer.

If the sample was examined with 1 ml of water per bee, the number of spores per cm³ is equal to the number of spores per bee. The following equation can be used to determine the number of spores per bee:

(Total number of spores counted + 80) x 4.000.000 = Number of spores per bee.

## Precautions to avoid errors:

- 1 Agitate the suspension before taking the sample to ensure an even distribution of the spores.
2. Use a clean haemocytometer for each sample.
3. Flame the wire before using it on each sample.
4. The counting should not be performed if there are air bubbles or if the distribution of the spores in the chamber is uneven.
5. Allow the spores to settle for 3 minutes before starting the count.
6. Perform the count before the sample evaporates from the chamber.

## 6.3.4. Amebiosis

This is a disease caused by the protozoan *Malpighamoeba mellificae*, which only affects adult bees. No obvious symptoms are observed in bees. This disease has also been found in bees infected with the protozoan *Nosema apis*. Both diseases have a very similar course.

The pathogen develops in the Malpighian tubules. Microscopic cysts can also be found in the intestinal contents of infected bees.

## Diagnosis:

- Place the tubules in a drop of water on a microscope slide.
- Carefully remove the Malpighian tubules, which are long, thin projections originating from the junction of the midgut with the posterior intestine.
- Place a coverslip directly over the tubules and carefully apply gentle pressure to create a flat surface for microscopic examination.
- Observe under low power. Amoebic cysts will appear as small, oil-like droplets in the water.

## 6.4. Varroa Mites

Honey bees are affected by both internal and external mites. It is important to note that not all external mites are harmful to bees. *Varroa destructor* is the only internal mite species found in honey bees.

## 6.4.1. Varroatosis

This is a disease caused by the parasite *Varroa destructor*. The female mite enters the bee's body through the first pair of thoracic spiracles; both queens, worker bees, and drones are equally susceptible to varroatosis. The mite enters the bee during the first 5 days of its adult life, after which it can no longer be removed.

After entering, the female mite lays its eggs in the trachea. The mite larvae presumably feed on the bee's hemolymph by penetrating the tracheal wall with their mandibles. This damage causes the affected tissue to undergo melanization, which appears as dark brown spots on the tracheal wall, a characteristic sign of heavy infestation.

One of the signs of this disease is the bee's inability to fly. Bees are observed crawling on the comb. This is believed to be due to the mechanical obstruction caused by the mites in the trachea, which affects gas exchange and prevents sufficient oxygen from reaching the flight muscles. Another sign is the abnormal position of the bee's wings, which appear to be disarticulated.

For diagnosis, a sample of 20 or more bees is required. Each bee is held with the thorax facing upwards on the microscope slide. Using a scalpel or tweezers, the head and first pair of legs are removed, pressing downwards and forwards to expose the mesothorax. Then, the first thoracic segment of chitin is removed with tweezers, as this covers the first pair of thoracic tracheae.

A healthy trachea appears translucent and creamy in color, and contains air. If infested, the trachea will show brown or black spots. The mites can be seen clinging to the trachea using tweezers.

Place the trachea on a slide that has previously been coated with a drop of Canadian balsam. Then, place a coverslip over the preparation and observe under 100x and 400x magnification to confirm the diagnosis.

## 6.4.2 Asian Varroa Mites

One of the differences between *Varroa destructor* and Asian Varroa mites is their size. *Varroa destructor* requires a microscope to be observed, while Asian Varroa mites, *Varroa destructor* and *Tropilaelaps clareae*, can be observed with the naked eye (the size of a pinhead). Another difference is that the Asian Varroa mite can be found in capped brood cells, while *Varroa destructor* is only found in adult bees. The other major difference is that *Varroa destructor* is an internal parasite, whereas Asian Varroa mites are external parasites.

*Varroa destructor*. The mites are found in young drones, especially in the thorax where the wings are attached to the body. The mites can also be found at the junction of the thorax with the head and abdomen. In addition, *Varroa destructor* infests capped brood cells. Bees infested with *Varroa destructor* may emerge with deformed or missing wings.

*Tropilaelaps clareae*. This is the only bee mite that can have another host, field mice. The mite feeds on the hemolymph of live and dead larvae, pupae, and adult bees. This mite appears to be confined to Asia.

Unless special measures are taken, Asian Varroa mites are not detected until they have infested an apiary after 2-3 years. The following methods are used to detect them:
