## I. INTRODUCTION

Rice is one of the oldest crops, and therefore, it has been difficult to precisely determine when humans began cultivating it. However, Chinese literature mentions cultivation dating back 3000 years BC. Rice (Oryza sativa L.) originated in southern India and reached the Americas via China, Mesopotamia, Greece, Egypt, Morocco, and Spain.

In El Salvador, rice is the fourth most important crop in terms of overall production. According to the DGEA (2016), 5,693 manzanas (3,985 hectares) were planted in the 2016/17 agricultural cycle, with a production of 607,579 quintales (30,378 tons), resulting in an average yield of 106.1 qq/ manzana (7.5 tha -1). The departments with the highest rice production in the country are: La Libertad, Chalatenango, Cuscatlán, San Vicente, and Ahuachapán.

This crop is characterized by having the highest net profit per manzana compared to other staple crops, and also offers the highest profitability-cost ratio.

Regarding apparent consumption, in our country, it has tripled in recent years, and this is due to the increase in population and changes in consumption habits, primarily in urban areas, where rice consumption in daily diets is increasing. Rice is consumed by all social classes in El Salvador.

This technical guide contains information on the general cultivation of rice, botanical aspects, edaphoclimatic requirements, agronomic characteristics of varieties, cultivation practices, pests and diseases, and their control, harvesting, and production costs.

## II. BOTANICAL ASPECTS

Rice is an annual grass with round, hollow stems composed of nodes and internodes. The leaves are flat and attached to the stem by sheaths. The inflorescence is a panicle. The size of the plant varies from 0.4 m (dwarf varieties) to 7.0 m (flooded varieties).

## ROOTS

During its development, the rice plant has two types of roots: seminal or dormant, and adventitious or permanent (González et al, 1985).

## STEM

The stem is formed by the alternation of nodes and internodes. The septum is the internal part of the node that separates two adjacent internodes. The mature internode is hollow, finely striated, and has a smooth surface. The color and sheen of the internode depend on the variety; the length of the internode varies, being longer in the upper internodes of the stem. The internodes at the base of the stem are very short and gradually thicken to form a solid section.

## LEAVES

The leaves of rice plants are distributed alternately along the stem. The first leaf that appears at the base of the main stem or the side stems is called a collar. It does not have a lamina and is composed of two auriculate bracts. The edges of the collar secure the young side stems to the main stem. In each node, except for the node of the panicle, a leaf develops. The upper leaf, located below the panicle, is called the flag leaf.

## PANICLE

The flowers of the rice plant are grouped in an inflorescence called a panicle. The panicle is located on the apical node of the stem, called the ciliar node or base of the panicle.

## SEED GERMINATION

Seeds without dormancy can germinate immediately after maturation. Seeds with dormancy are in a natural period of rest, for a period that may be longer or shorter. This dormancy can be artificially broken by descaling or by subjecting them to special treatments to allow germination. Many of the structures of the seed during germination are temporary, such as the coleoptile and the coleorhiza, and their functions are to protect other structures or to establish the seedling while the permanent structures develop (González et al, 1985).

## III. EDAPHOCLIMATIC REQUIREMENTS

## SOIL

The soil in which rice can be grown is as varied as the range of climates to which the crop can be exposed. The texture varies from sandy to clayey, with pH values ranging from 3.0 to 10.0, but its best development is achieved when the acidity is not below 5.0 or above 6.5; with organic matter content ranging from 1 to 50%; and with salt concentrations of 0-1%, allowing for good production in saline soils (Vargas, 1985).

## CLIMATE

Rice is cultivated in a variety of environmental conditions and is a crop particularly suited to humid tropical climates or climates with high temperatures. Temperature affects not only the growth, but also the development of the rice plant. Not all phases of development respond to the same temperature ranges, but each phase is favored by different temperatures. However, varieties have been developed that can adapt to different temperature ranges. The critical temperatures for the rice plant are generally below 20°C and above 30°C, and vary depending on the stage of development. Rice can be cultivated from sea level to 2,500 meters above sea level.

## V. CULTIVATION PRACTICES

## SOIL PREPARATION

Soil preparation is one of the most important practices for rice sowing, as it depends on the establishment of the plants and the subsequent work.

Weeding: This involves manually or mechanically removing weeds 8 to 15 days before sowing, depending on the type and development of the weeds.

Cultivation: This activity loosens the soil to favor seed germination and seedling emergence. It is recommended to perform two passes with the cultivator.

Leveling: Leveling can be done in dry conditions, using a conventional method, or it can be done in flooded conditions using the flooding method. It is recommended to perform one pass with the plow every two or three years to prevent soil compaction.

Furrow: The furrow should have a width of 0.3 m and a depth of 0.10 to 0.15 m to favor seed germination and drainage when direct seeding. When transplanting rice, a furrow width of 0.30 m is recommended.

## PLANTING TIMES

Rainfed rice is sown at the beginning of the rainy season, with a recommended sowing period between the second half of May and the first half of June, under these conditions, the crop depends on rainfall. Sowing under irrigation (dry season) is carried out during the months of December and January.

## Planting Distances and Densities

In rice cultivation, the recommended planting distance for direct seeding is 0.30 m between rows and basins; and when transplanting, a distance of 0.30 m between rows and 0.20 m between plants is recommended. As for planting densities, a recommendation of 97.18 Kgha-1 (150 pounds per basin) of seed is made for direct seeding; for broadcast seeding, the recommended amount of seed is 130 Kgha-1 (200 pounds per basin), and for transplanting, 45.45 Kgha-1 (70 pounds per basin) is recommended for creating a nursery.

## Planting Methods

The method of planting rice can be broadly divided into two main systems: direct seeding and transplanting; in addition, the practice of ratooning is used to obtain a new harvest. The seed can be planted dry, wet, or pre-germinated. Transplanting methods are divided according to the type of nursery: dry and wet beds, and the method of transplanting: manual and mechanical. Mechanical transplanting is used in Japan and other Asian countries, and is currently being introduced in the Americas.

## Direct Seeding

## In Rows

This method uses planting machines that deposit fertilizer under the row for better utilization; in some areas, rows are made with a tractor or wooden plow, and the planting is done manually, depositing the seed in a row at a distance of 0.30 m. Well-prepared soils are required, which allow for the proper and uniform placement of the seed at a depth of 0.02 to 0.05 meters, to achieve uniform germination and emergence.

## Broadcast Seeding

This is one of the most common planting methods, due to its speed and cost-effectiveness. There is manual broadcast seeding, with manual spreader machines, broadcast seeding with a tractor spreader, and aerial seeding; in addition, dry, wet, and pre-germinated seeds can be used, depending on the type of soil preparation. The planting is more irregular, and the germination of the seed is uneven.

## a. Manual Broadcast Seeding

The seed is distributed by hand, in strips or walking in a circle within the plot; this method is used to distribute pre-germinated seed in mud or water; it is a slow method, as the worker plants from 1 to 2 hectares (1.43 to 2.86 basins) per day. When dry seed is planted, it must be incorporated with a tractor using a light harrow; in this case, it is recommended to use a furrow opener while the rice is growing.

## b. Broadcast Seeding with Tractor Spreader

Small centrifugal distribution machines are used, which provide good coverage of the land in strips of 4 to 7 m; the operator requires assistance to provide the seed.

## c. Broadcast Seeding with Tractor

Machines with a hopper with a distributor mechanism, driven by the tractor's power take-off at 540 rpm, are used, which are also used for fertilizing; they distribute the seed in strips, which range from 10 to 15 m in width and a speed of up to 10 Km/hour; the planting density is determined by the output of the spreader, the speed of the tractor, and the width of the strip.

## Transplanting

This is an indirect planting system, in which the plants initially grow in nurseries or basins, to be transplanted to the main field later. The basins must be filled and well leveled. Transplanting is recommended when the seedlings are 15 to 18 days old, and 2 to 3 plants are planted per basin. This system presents differences in the way the nurseries are made, as well as in the methods of carrying out the transplanting.

## a. Types of Nurseries

## a.1. Wet Bed Nurseries

These are made on soil that is filled and well leveled, creating raised beds between 5 and 10 cm high, with channels between them to allow drainage; the width varies between 1 and 1.5 m, with lengths of 20 to 30 m, depending on the area to be transplanted.

## a.2. Dry Bed Nurseries

This type of nursery is carried out in areas with little water for filling, by pulverizing the soil very well and raising the beds to 0.10 m, with channels between them for irrigation.

## a.3. Mechanical Transplant Nurseries

These are made in boxes, in which a thin layer of loose soil is placed, about 3 kg per tray; the soil is fertilized with about 10 grams of a complete fertilizer.

## b. Transplanting

In nurseries with wet or dry beds, the seedlings must be carefully removed, trying to cause the least damage to the foliage and roots; to do this, it is recommended to keep the nursery flooded permanently in wet beds or simply moist in dry beds. To remove the seedlings, a small clump is detached by pulling it by the base near the soil, and then the soil attached to the roots is removed by agitating them in the water.

## c. Transplanting Methods

The methods of carrying out the transplanting are: at random, manual in rows, and mechanical transplanting.

## c.1. At Random

This is the most commonly used method commercially, as it allows for a higher yield of labor; the plants are planted in the mud without following any defined pattern; its main disadvantage is the difficulty in establishing the appropriate population.

## c.2. In Rows

To carry it out, a pita is used, which serves as a guide for the operators in the rows. Another system has been implemented, in which two operators with a pita plant a row with a guide, and another operator plants in the row, allowing them to move the guide without losing time; this helps to reduce labor.

## c.3. Mechanical Transplanting

This system is gaining popularity in Asia, but in the Americas, it has had little success due to a lack of adequate soil preparation and specialized personnel; today, it is known that it is practiced in the Dominican Republic and Colombia.

## Ratoon Cultivation

This system is used to obtain a second and even a third harvest. It consists of manually cutting the stems or using a combine harvester to cut them, and then applying irrigation and fertilization; it is recommended to cut the stems at 0.05 to 0.10 m, as these cuts have yielded the best results. The yield reaches around 80% of the yield obtained in the first harvest, and given the decrease in costs, it is more economical than the unit cost of production (Tascon, 1985).

## Irrigation

Irrigation in rice cultivation involves covering the soil with a layer of water to a depth of 2.5 to 10 cm, which helps to control weed growth without damaging the crop. This is known as flooding. After that, the water is continuously added to replace the water lost through evapotranspiration, deep percolation, and filtration through the edges, which is known as maintaining the flood.

## Irrigation Methods for Rice

Traditionally, two irrigation methods are used in rice cultivation: the flooded or parcel method and the contour or ridge method. The flooded or parcel method consists of square or rectangular sections with the surface level; it is generally used in small areas. In the contour or ridge method, the sections are larger and are traced in strips at the same level; the water applied is retained in each section by edges that surround each strip, built along a level curve (Grillo, 1985).

## Irrigation Methods

The methods of applying and managing water in rice cultivation are very diverse, depending on the infrastructure developed, the type of crop, and the topography of the area. The most common irrigation methods have been divided according to the mode and frequency of application and the duration of water on the soil.

## a - Continuous irrigation with static water level

In this method, water is applied continuously, maintaining a constant water level; this requires good irrigation management and control, and has the potential for optimal yields, when the water level is low (2.5 cm), the plants tend to be smaller, with a higher number of tillers, and have a lower tendency to lodging; when the water level is medium (2.5 to 7.5 cm), the plants produce high yields and with fewer weed problems; when the water level is greater than 10 cm, it is considered high, and there is better weed control, fewer tillers per plant, and greater water loss due to percolation and lateral infiltration; the plants tend to be taller and have a higher tendency to lodging, and yields are lower.

## b - Continuous irrigation with variable water level

Most rice crops in our country fall into this method. The variation in water level is due to uneven leveling, slope within the fields, and stepped irrigation applications, which allow for changes in the water level. When there are no problems with leveling and the irrigation frequency is adequate, without allowing the soil to dry out, the potential yield is high.

## c - Rotational irrigation.

In this method, the frequency of irrigation is wide, allowing the soil to dry out between irrigations; the plants are shorter, and weeds are favored. The amount of water used by the crop is greater, as it allows the soil to dry out to be flooded again, and the yield is affected when there is water stress during the reproductive phase.

## d - Continuous irrigation

This method is widely used in soils that are not leveled. In this method, small ridges are created to distribute the water, following the contour lines. The water is applied to the highest point of the field and carried from one strip to another. This method does not allow for water storage or a constant water level, except in low areas, next to the drainage canals or in areas with minimal slope.

## e - Sprinkler irrigation

This is a supplementary irrigation method for rain-fed crops during critical periods of moisture. It is not a common practice in our country.

## f - Rainwater harvesting

In Latin America, around 70% of rice cultivation depends on rainfall for water supply, and these are called rain-fed or seasonal rice crops. The yield is conditioned by the volume of precipitation, its distribution, and the soil (Tascon, 1985).

## WEED CONTROL

## Mechanical control

This is carried out using tools powered by human, animal, or mechanical force; it involves manually pulling weeds, or using machines and implements.

## Physical control

This is carried out through flooding. Weed control by flooding the soil is carried out by surrounding the area to be treated with dikes or borders, and then applying a water layer with a thickness of at least five centimeters, and keeping it flooded for 21 days.

## Cultural control

This is an integrated management of agricultural practices to create an environment that is unfavorable to weeds, including crop rotation, spacing, density, and planting systems. Crop rotation involves alternating a crop with another that requires different management practices, which may result in less or more shade, requires a drier environment, and the use of other agrochemicals. This is a way to make the conditions unfavorable for weeds and constitutes an excellent control system for difficult species. Spacing and planting density.

## Biological control

This is based on the use of birds, insects, or pathogens to control undesirable plants, without causing damage to the crop.

## VI. INSECTS, PESTS AND THEIR CONTROL

The damage caused by pests to rice plants is one of the factors that affects production. The behavior of insects and mites varies depending on the climatic conditions, the cultivation system, the planting time, the stage of development of the plant, and the variety. Identifying pests, as well as knowing their habits, the damage they cause to the plant, the age of the crop at which they attack, and the time of year in which they appear, allows for efficient management and control, to achieve an increase in crop production. For the purpose of describing them, the insects and mites that attack rice have been classified according to the type of damage and the age of the plants they attack, while the chemical control can be carried out with the insecticides detailed in Annex 1 (Galvis, 1985).

## SOIL PESTS

These pests are present in the early stages of the crop, from planting to the seedling stage, during which they reduce the population of rice plants. They feed on the roots and cut the stem at ground level. The presence of these pests depends on crop rotation, soil preparation system, management of the previous crop's residues, and climatic conditions. The main pests found in the soil are:

The larvae are white or cream-colored and are easily recognized by their small, reddish head.

Damage: The damage caused by the rice weevil is caused by the small larva as soon as it emerges from the egg; it creates a hole in the stem and then pierces the nodes and creates galleries. As a result of this attack, some cases show yellowing of the lower leaves.

This category includes pests that feed directly on the foliage and sap-sucking insects, such as mites.

Noctuidae

The adults are moths with grey color and white and light grey spots on the first pair of wings (Figure 9). The eggs are laid in masses, on the leaf blade or on the ground, and are covered by scales from the female's body.

The larvae, upon completing their development, are green or black depending on the food they consume. On the head, which is black or brown, there is a white-ish inverted Y-shaped marking.

Damage: The small larvae feed on the foliage of the plants, causing scraping. The larger larvae cause the destruction of all the foliage. They generally appear in high populations during dry periods following rainy seasons. The economic threshold fluctuates between 1.03 and 13.84 larvae per square meter.

Control: Recognition of natural control; birds exercise predatory action; beneficial insects from the families Carabidae, Tachinidae and Reduviidae, help by parasitizing or preying. Chemical control is only recommended for intense attacks.

This mite is very small, not visible to the naked eye, as it measures between 100 and 300 micrometers (Figure 13). Females are larger than males. The inactive stage (pupa) has the same appearance as the active larva.

Damage: This mite causes direct damage by feeding on the leaf sheath and indirect damage by injecting toxins into the cells, causing deformities in the grain (Figure 14). The mite, when feeding and sucking sap, produces empty or hollow grains; in addition, it spreads the conidia of the rice sheath rot fungus (Sarocladium oryzae).

Control: It is related to crop management, as it is a opportunistic pest that takes advantage of nutritional and health imbalances in the crop to colonize rice plants. The main difficulty in controlling it is due to its ethological characteristics, as it is located inside the seed casings of the leaves, making it very difficult for chemical and biological products to act through foliar applications, even though they have been effective in laboratory conditions. On the other hand, applications with granulated formulations and other products applied to the soil with chemical products that achieve some control have proven to be economically unsatisfactory. Agronomic practices such as: using certified seeds, appropriate planting methods, and correct applications of herbicides and fertilizers, drastically reduce the damage caused by the mite. The management of this pest is integrated, and the following measures are therefore recommended:

Technical Guide

- Use certified seeds
- Low planting densities (150 lb/mz)
- Avoid practices that cause stress to the crop, such as: compacted soils, late herbicide applications, nutrient imbalance, poor water management.
- Protect biological control agents.
- Do not apply pesticides unnecessarily.
- Remove and incorporate crop residues, as they are hosts for the mite.
- For control, economic thresholds for damage have not been established, therefore, a monitoring program should be established to determine the presence and levels of damage.

Its wild hosts are some plants of the genus Solanum sp. and the weeds Echinochloa colonum, Digitaria sanguinalis and Portulaca oleracea.

## OTHER PESTS

## RODENTS

There are two groups of rodents: (rats and mice) that damage rice crops.

Due to the difficulty in observing them during the day and their ability to hide among the vegetation, the damage caused by rodents is generally considered by farmers as a secondary factor within the group of pests. It has been shown that in some rice-growing areas, the damage caused by rodents is as important or more than that caused by insects or diseases.

Damage of 14% already justifies economically treating for rat and mouse control in rice fields. The damage caused by rats occurs throughout the crop cycle.

Rodent populations multiply rapidly because they find water, food and shelter in rice, which are essential factors for reproduction and development of a population.

Control: In rice, control is only possible by cleaning canals and eliminating crop residues, which destroys potential nesting sites.

Chemical control should be carried out with baits containing a sharp toxic or slow-acting anticoagulant products that do not cause rejection. A good bait is made using a mixture of 90% rice or corn flour, plus 5% corn or soybean oil and 5% sugar.

Once the mixture is prepared, the active ingredient or rodenticide is added, in the recommended proportion, such as warfarin at 5%, zinc phosphate at 2%, or any other rodenticide available on the market.

Rice bran should be placed in dry or within structures such as bamboo pieces, plastic containers, or plastic bags in amounts of 30 to 50 grams, which further facilitates handling for treatment in the field (Valencia, 1985).

## DAMAGING BIRDS.

The estimated damage caused by birds in rice should be made directly on the affected plant and never based on the bird population. Techniques for assessing bird damage in rice are not very advanced, but sampling a square meter at random is suggested to determine the percentage of affected plants and the percentage of grains damaged, knowing the characteristics of the damage caused by the bird and comparing it with a control sample or normal sample. This type of observation is of great importance to avoid confusing it with rodent damage and to avoid unnecessary bird control treatments in rice fields.

Control: Current control methods are based on physical methods, using people (bird watchers) who scare away birds with whistles, noises, and loud sounds such as gunpowder (Galvis, 1985).

## VII. DISEASES AND THEIR CONTROL

Common rice diseases can be grouped according to the organism that causes them, and are divided into two groups: infectious diseases and non-infectious or nutritional disorders.

Infectious diseases are those caused by fungi, bacteria, viruses, and nematodes; and non-infectious or nutritional disorders are those caused by deficiencies and excesses (toxicities) of elements. These diseases can present characteristic symptoms on the leaves, stems, and sheaths, inflorescence, and new growth.

Pyricularia, helmintosporiosis, and leaf scald are the most widely spread diseases in the country; but, like other Latin American countries, Piricularia is the most destructive in this crop (Castaño, 1985).

## DISEASES CAUSED BY FUNGI

## PIRICULARIA, BLAST, BURN, FIRE

Scientific Name:

Pyricularia grisea

This disease is the most complex problem in rice cultivation, and the losses it causes can be total. The fungus can infect rice plants at any stage, and the most serious economic damage occurs during flowering. The fungus can survive as conidia or mycelium in the seed, in plant residues in the soil, and in many grasses. The fungus causes lesions on leaves, node, and panicle, axis of the panicle, peduncle, and grains (Figure 16); the typical leaf lesion has a rhomboid shape with a gray center and sometimes with brown edges, the shape and color of the spots vary depending on the variety and environmental conditions. In the panicle neck, the fungus develops lesions that range from a dark green color at the beginning of the infection to a brown color. Factors that contribute to the development of the disease include:

- a. Periods of high humidity
- b. Susceptibility of varieties
- c. High nitrogen fertilization
- d. High planting densities
- e. Soil type and conditions
- f. Low temperatures, followed by hot days and high relative humidity.
- g. Low light
- h. Gentle winds

Control: The most practical and economical method is the use of resistant varieties, early planting, avoiding high doses of nitrogen fertilizers and high planting densities, and chemical control (see Annex 2).

Figure 16. Piricularia on panicle and leaf.

## HELMINTOSPORIOSIS, MILKY SPOT, BROWN SPOT, CARMELITA, RUST

Scientific Name:

Cochliobolus mayabeanus

This fungal pathogen attacks seedlings, leaves, sheaths, rachis, and grains. Leaf lesions are small, circular, and oval, with a dark brown color; if the lesions are larger, they have a pale, usually gray, center. Most spots have a light yellow halo around the outer edge; grain lesions are dark brown (Figure 17). Nodes and internodes are rarely infected. Factors that contribute to the development of the disease:

- a. Infertile, poorly drained soils
- b. Nitrogen deficiency in the middle stage of growth.
- c. Soils deficient in Silicon, Potassium,

Magnesium, Iron, and Zinc

- d. High doses of phosphorus
- e. High relative humidity

f. Acidic pH

Control: Use of resistant varieties, good soil preparation, balanced fertilization, good water management, and chemical control (see Annex 2) mainly for seed treatment and application of fungicides at the flowering stage.

Figure 17. Helmintosporium on panicle and leaf.

## WHEAT RUST

Scientific Name:

Sarocladium oryzae

The causal fungus has a branched white mycelium; this forms from the slightly thickened mycelium from which the conidia emerge, which are hyaline, cylindrical, unicellular, and produced consecutively; over time, the mycelium turns salmon-colored; if the environmental humidity is high, the fungus develops a white layer composed of mycelium and conidia between the infected sheath and the stem.

The infection occurs on the upper part of the sheath, in the flowering stage. Early symptoms are irregular, pale brown or white spots with a gray center and brown or completely brown margins; the lesions enlarge, often merging and affecting the entire leaf sheath (Figure 18); severe infections can cause partial exsertion of the panicle. Panicles that do not emerge, rot, and show abundant fungal growth inside the leaf sheath; panicles that emerge partially may produce sparsely filled grains, black grains with sterility. This fungus causes hardening of the flag leaf sheath, which, with its narrowness, makes it difficult for the panicle to emerge, as the spikelet stalk is reinforced and twisted inside the leaf sheath. The most important transmission of this pathogen is through the seed. Sarocladium can be found associated with the Steneotarsonemus spinki mite, maximizing the damage by acting together. Factors that contribute to the development of the disease

- a. Use of low-quality seeds
- b. High planting density
- c. High temperature
- d. High relative humidity
- e. Reduced rainfall
- f. High application of nitrogen fertilizer
- g. Imbalance in fertilization
- h. Excessive use of pesticides

## LEAF SCALD

Scientific name: Rhynchosporium oryzae

This fungus primarily affects mature plants after the formation of panicles. It is more severe in dryland fields and under conditions of high relative humidity. This disease causes lesions near the tips of older leaves, sometimes on the leaf margin. The lesions are watery and turn into large, elliptical areas surrounded by dark brown borders and narrow bands, accompanied by a light brown halo (Figure 19). These lesions merge to dry the leaves, stems, and grains. The disease causes sterility; the first symptoms appear between 30-35 days after germination. It is transmitted by seed.

Factors contributing to the development of the disease:

- a. High relative humidity
- b. Cool temperatures at night (20°C)
- c. Continuous heavy rainfall during panicle formation
- d. Acidic soils
- e. Excessive nitrogen fertilization

## RHIZOSPORI

Scientific name:

Sphaerulina oryzina

This disease most commonly affects mature plants and can cause severe damage. It produces brown, short, narrow, linear spots on the leaves (Figure 20). These spots can appear on the sheaths, peduncles, and glumes; the central axis of each spot is parallel to the leaf vein. The spots are narrow, short, and dark brown in resistant varieties; in susceptible varieties, the spots are slightly wider, light brown, with a narrow, light center. Generally, the narrow spots are reddish-brown with less intensity at the edges. The development of the disease is favored by unfavorable edaphic conditions, due to phosphorus and potassium deficiency.

Control: Use of resistant varieties, early-maturing seed varieties, avoid excessive application of potassium fertilizers, and chemical control (see Appendix 2).

Clamidiospores cannot be easily released from the masses of mold due to a sticky material (Figure 21). Factors contributing to the development of the disease:

- a. High relative humidity
- b. Heavy rainfall
- c. Cloudy days
- d. Excessive nitrogen application

## STEM ROT.

Scientific name:

Rhyizoctonia solani

This disease has become a major problem for high-yielding varieties. Symptoms appear until the plant begins to till and elongate internodes. It produces green-gray, elliptical or oval lesions (Figure 22), which can enlarge and merge with others, primarily on the leaf sheaths and occasionally on the leaves. The edges of each lesion and the variation in color of the lesions give a distinct appearance to the infected area. Symptoms are generally different in the flowering and maturation stages. Severe infections cause little grain formation. Factors contributing to the development of the disease:

1. High plant density, due to the microenvironment
2. and to the better adhesion of sclerotia to the surface of the stem, thus favoring the spread of the disease by the mycelium.
3. High application of nitrogen and phosphorus
4. Poor potassium fertilization

Control: Use of resistant varieties, low nitrogen levels, low planting densities, wider spacing in transplanted rice, and control of grassy weeds in the surrounding area.

Cultivation and chemical control. The first application is to elongate and develop the panicle, and the second application is when 80-90% of the panicles have emerged (see Annex 2).

## CONTROL OF BACTERIA THROUGH INTEGRATED PEST MANAGEMENT

It is recommended to take regular samples during different phenological phases of the crop. This will allow for the timely detection of various phytosanitary problems, including those caused by bacteria, and to implement the corresponding control measures.

The recommended integrated management methods, to achieve adequate control of the bacteria that cause losses in rice production, are as follows:

*   Apply appropriate levels of fertilizers, according to the soil analysis.
*   Remove infected seeds (with the bacteria) before planting, placing the seed in a saturated water solution with ammonium sulfate and discarding those that float; the recommended dose is 11 lb (5 kg) of ammonium sulfate per 20 liters of water and then drying.
*   Use certified seed, previously treated with 100 cc of a commercial product containing tiametoxam + fludioxonil + metalxil-M for 1 quintal of seed, to minimize the effects of associated pathogens, such as the fungus *Sarocladium oryzae* and pathogenic bacteria that are transmitted by seed.

Technical Guide

*   Heat treatment of the seed, applying one hour of heat in the sun at 50°C and alternating with 24 hours of rest. Repeat the procedure described three times. This can be achieved by covering the seed with black plastic (separated from the seed) during the hours of maximum solar incidence (one hour per day).

## VIII. HARVEST

The harvest should be carried out as soon as the grain reaches its maturity, for which the best indicator is the grain moisture; but the color of the grain can also be used. The optimal time for harvesting rice is when the panicle reaches its physiological maturity, that is, when 95% of the grains have a straw-colored color and the rest are yellowish, in addition, the grain should have a moisture content of 20 to 27%.

The timing of the harvest is crucial in rice cultivation, as if the grain is not fully mature when harvested, it reduces the yield and the grain is very sticky and broken during threshing, while if it is left to over-ripen, there is a risk that it will disperse during harvesting, which reduces the yield and can result in broken grain during threshing.

In the country, the harvest is carried out manually and mechanically, according to the level of technological development or production system of the different productive areas. After the harvest, it is necessary to remove some of the moisture from the seed, in order to protect it from the attack of fungi and insects during storage. The appropriate moisture content for rice seed is 12%, which inhibits the metabolic processes of the seed and allows it to remain stored for long periods without deteriorating; however, in the tropics, the required moisture for rice is 14% (Tascón, 1985).
