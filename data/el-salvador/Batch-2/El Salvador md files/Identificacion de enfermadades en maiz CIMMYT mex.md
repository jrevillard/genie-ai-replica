## Introduction

This well-known pamphlet, now in its fourth edition, is intended to serve as a quick guide to identifying diseases of maize. Based on previous editions of Carlos De León's work, a pathologist at CIMMYT, this guide has been updated with the contributions of his colleague, Dan Jeffers. Given its target audience of agricultural technicians and maize producers, it was deemed sufficient to use the Latin taxonomic names of the pathogens, although for fungal pathogens, both sexual (teleomorphic) and asexual (anamorphic) names of the fungi are included. Since the third edition, published in 1984, several modifications to the nomenclature have been made. In most cases, the nomenclature of A.J. Ullstrup (1985. Plant Disease 69:658-659) is used to assign common names to the diseases. This document provides a brief description of common maize diseases, their causative agents, and symptoms, as well as the names of new diseases and updated data, based on the scientific advances achieved since the third edition of this guide. Numerous color photographs of diseased plants are also included. At the end, a diagnostic key is provided in the form of an appendix. The text is organized according to the four main causative agents of maize diseases: fungi, bacteria, viruses, and mildews. Fungal diseases are presented in the following order: foliar diseases, stalk rots, ear rots, and stalk blights. Most of these diseases are of economic importance, while others, although not, have the potential to become so.

We would like to thank Mike Listman, a scientific writer at CIMMYT, for his editorial work in English, as well as Wenceslao Almazán for the production and design of this new edition. The Spanish translation was carried out by Ma. Concepción Castro, and the editing was performed by Alma McNab.

## Brown Spot (Pec)

*Physoderma maydis*

This disease typically occurs in areas with abundant rainfall and high temperatures. It attacks the leaves, husks, stems, and, occasionally, the outer bracts.

The first symptoms appear on the leaf blade and consist of small chlorotic spots arranged in alternating bands of healthy and diseased tissue (photo 1). The spots on the central vein are circular and dark brown, while the lesions on the leaf margins are only chlorotic. The nodes and internodes also show brown lesions. In severe infections, the spots merge and cause stalk rot and lodging (photo 2).

## Hairy Mildew (Powdery Mildew)

Several species of the genera *Peronosclerospora*, *Sclerospora*, and *Sclerophthora* cause hairy mildews:

*   Loose smut, yellow rust *Sclerophthora macrospora*
*   Brown rust *Sclerophthora rayssiae* var. *zea* (photo 3)
*   Green ear rot *Sclerospora graminicola*
*   Java mildew of maize *Peronosclerospora maydis* (photo 4)
*   Philippine mildew of maize
*   *Peronosclerospora philippinensis* (photo 5)
*   Sugar cane mildew of maize *Peronosclerospora sacchari* (photo 6)
*   Sorghum mildew of maize *Peronosclerospora sorghi* (photos 7, 8, 9)

5

These diseases pose a serious problem for maize producers in several countries in Asia, Africa, and the Americas. The expression of symptoms depends largely on the age of the plant, the species of the pathogen, and the environment. Generally, chlorotic banding or partial symptoms are observed on the leaves and husks, and stunting. Hairy mildew becomes more apparent when a hairy growth (powdery mildew) appears on or under the surface of the leaves. This condition is the result of the formation of conidia, which generally occurs in the early morning.

The disease is more common in warm and humid regions. Some species that cause hairy mildew also cause spike malformations (panicle), which obstruct pollen production and ear formation. The leaves may be narrow, thick, or abnormally erect.

## Maize Rusts

There are three types of maize rusts: common rust, *Polysora* rust, and tropical rust.

## Common Rust

*Puccinia sorghi*

This disease is widely distributed throughout the world, in subtropical and temperate climates, and in high-altitude areas with high humidity.

Common rust is most noticeable when plants are near flowering. It can be recognized by small, powdery pustules, both on the upper and lower surfaces of the leaves (photo 10). The pustules are light brown in the early stages of infection; later, the epidermis breaks down and the lesions turn black as the plant matures. The alternate host ( *Oxalis* spp.) is frequently infected with pustules of a light orange color (photo 11). This is simply another stage of the same fungus.

9

## Rust caused by *Polysora*

*Puccinia polysora*

Compared to the pustules produced by *P. sorghi*, those of *P. polysora* are smaller, lighter in color (light orange), and more circular (photo 12). Although they are also found on both sides of the leaves, the epidermis remains intact for longer than when dealing with *P. sorghi*. The pustules turn dark brown as the plants approach maturity. The alternate host of this fungus is unknown. Southern rust, also known as this rust, is common in the warm, humid lowlands of the tropics.

## Tropical Rust

*Physopella zeae*

Outbreaks of this disease are sporadic and restricted to tropical regions of the American Continent.

The shape of the pustules varies from circular to oval; they are small and found beneath the epidermis. In the center of the pustule, the lesion is white to light yellow and has a hole (photo 13). The pustule is sometimes surrounded by a black border, but the center remains clear. The alternate host of this fungus is unknown.

## White Edge, Vertical Striped Blotch

*Marasmiellus paspali var. americanus, M. paspali var. paspali, M. paspali sensu lato*

The symptoms on the leaves are very similar to those caused by the banded leaf and spike blight (MBHV; see page 15). This disease has been reported on maize in warm, humid tropical lowlands. However, it does not cause significant economic damage.

Long, concentric lesions of two colors begin to appear on the edges of the leaves (photo 14) when the plants are near flowering, although there are no visible signs of fungal infection in these lesions. Later, small, scelerotic-like fruiting bodies develop. However, a closer inspection reveals that these structures are agaricoid (basidiomata) fruiting bodies of the fungus (photo 15). The high relative humidity and abundant rainfall favor the development of these ephemeral structures.

The three species mentioned have been detected in maize crops in the tropical regions of the American Continent, eastern and southeastern Asia, and in countries in West Africa, respectively.

## Asphalt Spot Complex

*Phyllachora maydis* and *Monographella maydis*

This disease occurs in relatively cool and humid areas of the tropics, similar to those where leaf blight caused by *Turcicum* is common. Initially, bright, slightly raised, black spots (photo 16) appear. In a later stage, necrotic areas develop in the leaf tissue.

In several countries in the American Continent, another pathogen, *Monographella maydis*, and *Phyllachora maydis* form the 'asphalt spot complex'. This complex promotes the development of necrotic tissue around the asphalt spot (photo 17). The necrotic lesions can merge and cause complete defoliation. The lesions caused only by *Monographella maydis* are circular and measure between 5 and 6 cm in diameter (photo 18).

The lesions caused by the two pathogens that cause the complex begin to develop on the lower leaves before flowering, and if the conditions are favorable, the infection continues upwards, affecting even the younger leaves. The ears of affected plants are very light and the kernels are loose and do not compact; many of the kernels at the tip germinate prematurely, while still on the cob (photo 19).

## Leaf blight caused by *Turcicum*

Teleomorph: *Setosphaeria turcica* (syn. *Trichometasphaeria turcica*) (Anamorph: *Exserohilum turcicum*, syn. *Helminthosporium turcicum*)

One of the first symptoms is the appearance of small, slightly oval, watery spots that appear on the leaves and are easily recognizable. These lesions then transform into elongated, wedge-shaped necrotic areas (photo 20), which first appear on the lower leaves and increase in number as the plant develops. Complete defoliation can occur.

The leaf blight caused by *Turcicum* (or northern leaf blight) is distributed worldwide and occurs particularly in areas with high humidity and moderate temperatures during the growing season. When the infection occurs before or during the appearance of the stigmas, and if the conditions are optimal, it can cause significant economic damage.

## Leaf blight caused by *Maydis*

Teleomorph: *Cochliobolus heterostrophus* (Anamorph: *Bipolaris maydis*, syn. *Helminthosporium maydis*)

When they begin to form, the lesions are small and rhomboid. As they mature, they become elongated, but the adjacent veins restrict their growth, and the final shape of the lesion is rectangular, 2 to 3 cm long. The lesions can merge and cause complete defoliation of extensive areas (photo 21).

The symptoms described correspond to the 'O' race of the fungus. In the early 1970s, the 'T' race caused severe damage to maize varieties in the United States that had been bred with the Texas sterility source. The lesions caused by the 'T' race (photo 22) are oval and larger than those caused by the 'O' race. A key difference between the two is that the 'T' race affects the bracts and the leaf sheaths, while the 'O' race typically does not.

Corn leaf blight (caused by *Exserohilum turcicum*) is widespread in warm, humid corn-growing areas. To cause infection, the fungus requires slightly higher temperatures than *E. turcicum*; however, both species are often found on the same plant.

## Leaf spot caused by *Carbonum*

Teleomorph: *Cochliobolus carbonum* (Anamorph: *Bipolaris zeicola*, syn. *Helminthosporium carbonum*)

This disease is more common in humid, moderately warm areas.

The five known races of the fungus produce different symptoms on the leaves. For example, race 1 produces oval, zoned, and brownish lesions throughout the plant, including the ears, which rot and turn black. Lesions of race 2 are elongated, thin, and brown, mainly on the lower leaves; it can also cause ear rot (photo 23). Race 3 produces narrow, grayish lesions with chlorotic margins. Lesions of race 4 are similar to those produced by race 2, but often show concentric structures. Race 5 produces only small necrotic spots on immature leaves.

The symptoms of ear rot caused by races 1 and 2 are very similar.

## Leaf anthracnose

Anamorph: *Colletotrichum graminicola* (Teleomorph: *Glomerella graminicola*)

This disease is distributed worldwide in warm, humid climates. It manifests in two phases: first, it infects the leaves, and then it rots the stem.

There is no report of leaf infection causing significant economic damage in corn. The most severe damage is caused by stem rot. Leaf infection can occur at different stages of plant development. In the seedling stage, lesions on the leaves are irregular, oval to elliptical, with yellow to reddish-brown margins (photo 24). In later stages, similar lesions can be observed on the upper leaves of infected plants, mainly those showing symptoms of stem rot.

## Yellow leaf blight

Anamorph: *Phyllosticta maydis* (Teleomorph: *Mycosphaerella zeae-maydis*)

In 1970, this disease was associated with the presence of Texas sterility in susceptible materials, and several scientists linked it to yield losses and increased lodging. Humidity and heat favor its development.

Symptoms on young, diseased plants are similar to those of nitrogen deficiency. In mature plants, the lesions are narrow, necrotic, and parallel to the veins, although they do not restrict growth (photos 25 and 26). Later, the lesions merge and produce a characteristic necrosis near the leaf tip.

## Banded leaf and husk spot

Anamorph: *Rhizoctonia solani* f. sp. *sasakii* (Teleomorph: *Corticium sasakii*, syn. *Thanatephorus cucumeris*)

As the name suggests, this disease develops on the leaves and husks. Symptoms consist of characteristic concentric spots that cover large areas of the leaves and infected husks (photos 27 and 28).

In humid tropical areas, the main damage consists of a brownish rot of the ears, which presents a cottony mold of light brown color with small, round, black sclerotia.

## Leaf Spot caused by Leptosphaeria

*Leptosphaeria michotii*

This disease has been detected in the high, humid areas of the Himalayas. Other species of *Leptosphaeria* are known in other parts of the world, which cause different symptoms on corn leaves.

The symptoms consist of small lesions that, as they increase in size, become concentric and cover large portions of the leaves (photo 29). It is most noticeable on the lower leaves during flowering.

## Leaf Spot caused by Phaeosphaeria

*Phaeosphaeria maydis*

This disease is present in Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, northern India, eastern and southern Africa, North America, and Mexico, where *Exserohilum turcicum* is also widespread. Conditions of high humidity and relatively low nighttime temperatures favor its development. The lesions appear as small areas of light green color, which later turn chlorotic and, finally, necrotic, and are surrounded by dark brown margins (photo 30). The spots on the leaves are circular or slightly elongated.

## Leaf Spot caused by Hyalothyridium

Anamorph: *Hyalothyridium maydis* (Teleomorph: *Leptosphaerulina* sp.)

This leaf disease has been detected in Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, and Mexico. It can cause significant losses in commercial crops in warm, humid climates with cool nights. The lesions begin on the lower leaves, in the form of round spots, when the plants reach approximately one meter in height. Three weeks after flowering, the lesions are round, dark, and necrotic; they can measure between 3 and 4 cm in diameter and have concentric circles surrounded by chlorotic tissue (photos 31 and 32).

In Colombia, the teleomorphic phase of *Leptosphaerulina* sp. has been associated with leaf spot in the later stages of infection.

## Leaf Spot caused by Curvularia

*Curvularia lunata*, *C. pallescens*, and *C. maculans*

These fungi produce small, necrotic or chlorotic spots with a halo of light color (photo 33). The diameter of the lesions, when fully developed, is approximately 0.5 cm. The disease is widespread in warm, humid corn areas, where it can cause significant damage to crops.

## Leaf Spot caused by Cercospora

*Cercospora zeae-maydis*, *C. sorghi* var. *maydis*

This disease, also known as gray leaf spot, can occur in temperate or subtropical, humid climates. The lesions begin as small, regular, elongated, grayish-brown necrotic spots, which grow parallel to the veins (photo 34). They can sometimes reach 3.0 x 0.3 cm in size. It is believed that there is a relationship between minimal tillage practices and an increase in its incidence. This disease is a problem in South America and in eastern and southern Africa. Prolonged periods of leaf humidity and cloud cover favor its development. It can cause severe leaf senescence after flowering, or poor grain filling.

## Zonal Leaf Spot

*Gloeocercospora sorghi*

Zonal leaf spot is more frequently found on sorghum plants than on corn plants.

This disease is characterized by small necrotic lesions that then spread and produce large, concentric, necrotic rings (photo 35). The lesions can reach a diameter of 5 to 6 cm and are mainly found on older leaves.

## Leaf Spot caused by Septoria

*Septoria maydis*

This disease primarily affects corn in cool, humid climates.

The initial symptoms appear as small, light green or yellow spots on the leaves (photos 36 and 37). These lesions merge and cause intense staining, as well as necrosis of the affected areas, where dark structures that produce spores called pycnidia develop.

## Leaf Spot caused by Kabatiella

Kabatiella zeae (syn. Aureobasidium zeae)

Leaf spot caused by Kabatiella can affect commercial corn crops in countries with cool, humid climates.

This disease is characterized by small (1 to 4 mm), circular, and translucent lesions that develop on the plants. The characteristic "eye" forms when light-colored centers develop surrounded by rings that range from black to purple, with a yellow halo (photos 38 and 39).

These symptoms are easily mistaken for genetic or physiological spots, which are very common on corn leaves. The symptoms are also similar to those of powdery mildew, which are observed in the early stages of infection by Curvularia.

## Foliar Streak caused by Macrospora

Stenocarpella macrospora, syn. Diplodia macrospora

There are no reports of this disease causing economic losses, although it does affect commercial corn crops in warm, humid areas. Stenocarpella macrospora is primarily a stalk rot agent, but under suitable climatic conditions, it can also cause damage to the leaves.

The symptoms consist of necrotic lesions along the veins. The lesions are similar to those caused by bacteria or by Exserohilum turcicum (turcicum leaf blight). However, if the damaged leaves are held against the light, a distinct yellow border can be seen around the lesions of S. macrospora, which do not have the lesions caused by other pathogens (photo 40). In humid climates, the black pycnidia that produce spores in the necrotic lesions emit fine, dark threads, and the lesion breaks (photo 41).

## Stalk Rot caused by Pythium

Pythium aphanidermatum, Pythium spp.

Species of Pythium cause stalk and seed rot, as well as damping-off in seedlings. This disease occurs in some subtropical or tropical warm and humid areas, and in temperate regions.

Generally, the lower nodes become soft and discolored, taking on an aqueous appearance and causing the plants to wilt. The damaged nodes twist before the plants wilt. Diseased plants may remain alive until the vascular tissue is destroyed (photo 42).

It is necessary to perform isolations on culture media to distinguish between Pythium stalk rot and Erwinia rot.

This disease can affect plants before flowering.

## Stalk Rot caused by Gibberella and Fusarium

Fusarium moniliforme, syn. Fusarium verticillioides (Teleomorph: Gibberella fujikuroi)

Gibberella zeae (Anamorph: Fusarium graminearum)

Two species of Fusarium cause stalk rot in corn:

Fusarium moniliforme is more common in dry, warm climates. It is particularly damaging if it begins before flowering (photo 43).

Gibberella zeae is more common in cold regions. It is one of the most damaging stalk rot agents (photo 44).

The symptoms caused by these pathogens are similar to those caused by Stenocarpella or Cephalosporium, and they cannot be distinguished until the structures that produce spores are visible. Wilted plants remain upright when dried, and small, dark brown lesions can be seen on the lower nodes. When cut vertically, the phloem of the infected stalks is dark brown, and there is a conspicuous darkening of the tissues.

In the final stages of the infection, the pith is destroyed, and the adjacent tissues lose their color.

## Stem Rot Caused by *Rhizoctonia*

*Rhizoctonia solani*

*Rhizoctonia* stem rot can cause significant economic losses in maize crops in both warm, dry, and intermediate-altitude areas, as well as in temperate climates.

The infection is systemic, meaning the fungus penetrates seedlings and develops within the plants without causing visible symptoms until flowering and stigma development.

The most noticeable symptoms are: a) abnormal stalk development (sheathing), where the stalks become deformed and excessively swollen (photo 45); b) the formation of dark masses of spores in some male flowers; and c) the development of dark spore masses instead of ears, exposing the torn vascular bundles (photo 46).

## False Stem Rot

*Ustilago virens*

False stem rot is very rare in humid, warm, and dry conditions, and generally affects rice plants more than maize plants.

The symptoms differ from those caused by other maize pathogens. False stem rot does not cause stalk deformation or infect the ear, as true *Rhizoctonia* does; instead, only a few male flowers on the stalk show dark green spore masses (sori; photo 47). It also differs from common *Ustilago* in that it does not produce husks or the characteristic deformities of this pathogen.

## Vascular Necrosis and Late Blight

*Acremonium strictum* (syn. *Cephalosporium acremonium*) and *C. maydis*

Vascular necrosis is caused by *Cephalosporium acremonium* and is widely distributed throughout the world. Late blight, caused by *C. maydis*, has only been detected in Egypt and India. Both diseases cause premature plant death as they approach flowering (photo 48); they are more common in heavy, wet soils in warm regions. The infection can originate from the soil or from infected seeds.

Infected plants do not show symptoms until they reach flowering and begin to wilt; the first leaves to wilt are the upper ones. Plants produce small ears with underdeveloped and shriveled grains. When cut vertically, the vascular bundles of the affected stems show a brownish discoloration that starts from the roots (photo 49). Similar symptoms are observed in plants infected with *Fusarium moniliforme*.

## Anthracnose Stem Canker

Anamorph: *Colletotrichum graminicola* (Teleomorph: *Glomerella graminicola*)

The fungus *Colletotrichum graminicola* causes stem canker and leaf blight. Stem canker has been reported mainly in warm, humid areas around the world. The symptoms of the infection are clearly visible as dark, elongated, and narrow lesions (initially brown and then black) along the surface of the stem; they appear as plants approach flowering (photo 50). In infected plants, premature wilting (caused by the complete destruction of the vascular tissue) and tearing of the vascular bundles are observed, which turn a dark brown color (photo 51). Since this and other fungi survive the winter in the infected tissues of maize, agricultural practices that involve the use of organic mulches have been shown to increase the incidence of the disease.

(photo courtesy of Dr. R. Carvalho)

## Charcoal Rot of the Stem

*Macrophomina phaseolina*

Charcoal rot of the stem is more common in warm, dry climates. The incidence increases rapidly when conditions are dry and temperatures are high before flowering.

The pathogen invades the root hairs of the seedling. After flowering, one of the first symptoms is the abnormal drying of the upper leaf tissue. When plants approach maturity, the internal parts of the stems show a dark brown discoloration and tearing of the vascular bundles (photo 52), mainly in the lower internodes of the stem. A careful examination of the bark and vascular bundles reveals the presence of small, black sclerotia (photo 53), which can survive the winter for several months and infect the next crop. The fungus can also infect the grains, giving them a dark color.

Many crops can serve as hosts for this pathogen.

## Botryodiplodia Stem Rot

*Botryodiplodia theobromae*

This disease was first reported in India, but has also been found in several other countries in Asia, Africa, and the Americas. It develops in warm, humid climates.

The plants become infected prematurely. When a vertical cut is made into the stems, the vascular tissue can be seen to be torn and discolored, ranging from gray to black. In the rotted areas, abundant gray-black mycelium is observed, mainly in the lower internodes of the plant (photo 54).

Unlike charcoal rot, *Botryodiplodia* does not produce black, spike-like sclerotia in the affected areas, but rather abundant, cottony, gray-black mycelium in the cavities that form in the vascular tissue of the infected internodes.

## Stem Rot caused by Stenocarpella

*Stenocarpella maydis, syn. Diplodia maydis S. macrospora, syn. D. macrospora*

The species *S. maydis* causes stem rot in cool, humid areas, while *S. macrospora* causes it in warm, humid areas. In susceptible corn varieties, a brownish discoloration is observed in the pith of the lower internodes (photos 55, 56). The stems become weak and easily break when there are strong winds or heavy rain. Subsequently, the most conspicuous symptom is the abundant formation of spore structures known as perithecia on the surface of the damaged internodes.

## Ear Rot caused by Penicillium

*Penicillium spp.*

The most common damage is caused by *Penicillium oxalicum*, although other species may also be involved. The infection is often associated with damage caused by insects on the ear.

A very conspicuous bluish-green powder grows between the kernels and on the surface of the cob (rachis) (photos 57, 58). Kernels damaged by the fungus develop a yellowish color and visible streaks in the pericarp.

**63**

## Ear Rot caused by Aspergillus

*Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus spp.*

This disease can be a serious problem when infected ears are stored with high humidity. Several species of *Aspergillus* are capable of infecting corn plants in the field. The most common is *A. niger*, which produces masses of black spores that cover the kernels and the cob (rachis), unlike *A. glaucus*, *A. flavus* (photo 59) and *A. ochraceus*, whose masses are typically yellowish-green; and *A. parasiticus*, the least common species, whose mass is green.

*Aspergillus flavus* and *A. parasiticus* produce mycotoxins, known as aflatoxins, which are toxic to mammals and birds.

## Ear Rot caused by Gibberella and Fusarium

*Fusarium graminearum (syn. F. roseum) (Teleomorph: Gibberella zeae)*

*Fusarium moniliforme, syn. F. verticillioides (Teleomorph: Gibberella fujikuroi)*

In corn, these two fungal species cause ear rot and stalk rot, as well as seedling blight. *Gibberella zeae*, the sexual state of the pathogen, is more common in cool, humid areas. The first signs of infection are the formation of white mycelia, which descend from the tip of the ear and give the infected kernels a reddish-pink color (photo 60). The fungus produces mycotoxins (known as deoxinivalenol, zearalenone and zearalenol) which are toxic to several animal species.

*Fusarium moniliforme* rot is likely the most common ear rot pathogen worldwide. Unlike *G. zeae*, the damage caused by *F. moniliforme* is mainly observed in individual kernels or in certain areas of the ear (photos 61, 62). Infected kernels develop a cottony mold or white streaks in the pericarp and germinate while still on the cob. Generally, ears infected by stalk borers are infected by *F. moniliforme*. The fungus produces mycotoxins known as fumonisins, which are toxic to some animal species.

## Corn Smut, Horse Blister

*Claviceps gigantea*

This disease (anamorph *Sphacelia sp.*) is endemic in certain high, cool, and humid areas of the central plateau of Mexico. Infected kernels turn into extensive fungal structures known as sclerotia, which mix with healthy kernels (photo 63). In the early stages of infection, the sclerotia are light-colored, soft, and sticky, and they become harder as the harvest approaches. They do not produce the characteristic black powder of common smut, but when they fall to the ground, they germinate and develop many head-like structures (stromata), which will release new spores during the release of stigmas in the next crop cycle (photo 64).

This pathogen is closely related to that of barley smut and also produces toxic alkaloids.

## Black rot of corn

*Macrophomina phaseolina*

Similar to stalk rot (see page 57), this disease develops in hot, humid regions with dry periods, primarily during flowering. At harvest, the ears are shrunken and the kernels are loose, light yellow in color, and have dark streaks beneath the pericarp. The kernels are easily removed from the cob and show superficial, small, round, black sclerotia, the size of a pin (photos 65-67).

Plants affected by stalk rot do not always develop black rot of the ear caused by the same pathogen.

## Grey rot of corn

*Physalospora zeae* (syn. *Botryosphaeria zeae*) (Anamorph: *Macrophoma zeae*)

If, after flowering, a period of several weeks with high heat or humidity occurs, this will favor the development of grey rot. The initial symptoms are very similar to those of *Stenocarpella*: a white-gray mold appears growing between the kernels and the husks, which then turn brown and clump together. In later stages of infection, the two fungi can be easily identified.

- (a) Grey rot of corn. The ear has a distinct black color; the mold is also dark and produces small, scattered black sclerotia on the ear (photo 70 courtesy of Dr. A.J. Ullstrup).
- (b) *Stenocarpella* rot of corn (see page 83). The ear is gray-beige in color, and the mold is white with black perithecia covering the cob and kernels.

## Common bunt

*Ustilago maydis*

Common bunt occurs in all corn-producing regions, but can be more severe in humid, temperate climates than in tropical lowlands with hot, humid climates.

The fungus attacks the ears, stalks, leaves, and tassels (photos 71-73). Prominent, closed, white galls replace the individual kernels. Over time, the galls break open and release masses of black spores that will infect the corn plants in the next crop cycle. The disease causes more severe damage to young plants in active growth and can stunt or kill them.

Common bunt is easily distinguished from bunt of the tassel by the absence of vascular tissue, which appears as fibers in the ears infected by this pathogen.

## *Botryodiplodia* or black grain rot

*Botryodiplodia theobromae*

This disease has been reported in India, Nigeria, Pakistan, and Thailand, and to a lesser extent, in the Americas. The same fungus can cause stalk rot with a distinctive dark coloration, in humid and warm climates (see page 58).

## Stalk rot caused by *Stenocarpella*

*Stenocarpella maydis*, syn. *Diploidia maydis*, *S. macrospora*, syn. *D. macrospora*

*Stenocarpella maydis* is most commonly found in warm, humid corn-growing areas.

The ears exhibit characteristic irregular necrotic areas on the husks; these areas enlarge and completely dry out the husks, even when the plant is still green. If the husks are removed, the ears are discolored and whitish, with cottony white mold between the kernels (photo 78). After that, a large number of small black pycnidia form between the kernels and the surface of the stalk (photo 79). The pycnidia serve as a source of inoculum for the next crop cycle. Only through microscopic examination of the spores can the specific pathogen be identified.

Ears with severe infections are very light. The infection most often occurs through the stalk and spreads from the stalk to the kernels. Injuries caused by stalk borers can increase the incidence of the disease. *Stenocarpella maydis* produces the mycotoxin known as diplodiatoxin, and *S. macrospora* produces diplodiol. Both substances are toxic to birds.

## Stewart's Wilt

*Erwinia stewartii, sin. Pantoea stewartii*

It is known that this pathogen is transmitted by beetles (Chaetocnema pulicaria) of maize, and occasionally also by seed. When the infection begins, the lesions are elongated and watery, and they turn a light yellow color, with irregular margins along the leaves (photo 82). The infection can reach the stem and cause stunting, wilting, and death of the plant. Severely infected plants that produce seed produce small ears with little grain. An infection at the end of the growing season can cause severe leaf necrosis, but not wilting (photo 83).

The wounds caused by insect vectors when feeding serve as entry points for the pathogen, which is carried from one growing season to the next by beetles.

## Bacterial Leaf Spot

*Pseudomonas rubrilineans, syn. P. avenae, Acidovorax avenae subsp. avenae*

While there have been no reports of severe damage caused by this disease, it can be considered a problem in certain warm and humid areas where susceptible germplasm is used.

Bacterial spotting affects maize plants from their early stages of development (seedlings) to after flowering. The leaves develop several small, light green spots. Under optimal climatic conditions, the spots spread along the veins and produce a conspicuous spotting, mainly on the younger leaves. Later, the spots dry out and darken (photo 84); often, the infected leaf tissue shows tearing. The severe damage to the upper leaves causes ear rot, when the dead leaves encircle the ear (photo 85).

## Maize Chlorotic Dwarf

(Maize chlorotic dwarf virus, MCDV)

Infected plants initially show small chlorotic spots that later develop into a general chlorosis in the collar leaves (photo 86). The plants do not develop fully due to shortened internodes, and the leaves may turn reddish after flowering, similar to the symptoms of maize stunting and reddening. The vectors of this disease are the grain weevils Graminella nigrifrons and G. sonora, which, when feeding on an infected plant, spread the virus for long periods. Johnson grass serves as an alternate host for the virus and the vector when maize is not present. To date, this disease has only been found in the continental part of North America, but it is likely to have a wider distribution.

## Maize Chlorotic Mottle

(Maize chlorotic mottle virus, MCMV)

In the early stages of infection, the younger leaves show small chlorotic spots that merge and form wide chlorotic bands along the veins. The chlorotic bands contrast with the normal dark green tissue when viewed against the light (photos 87, 88). The leaves with chlorosis eventually die. Infected plants exhibit stunting due to shortened internodes and produce fewer, smaller ears. In most cases, the ear is deformed.

The virus is mainly transmitted by several species of leafhoppers, such as Chaetocnema pulicaria and Diabrótica spp., during a short period. The available data on the virus indicates that transmission through seed occurs very rarely.

When the virus appears together with maize yellow dwarf virus (MDMV) or wheat streak mosaic virus (WSMV), it causes a severe reaction known as lethal maize necrosis (MLN) (see page 96).

(Maize dwarf mosaic virus, MDMV)

## Sugarcane Mosaic

(Sugarcane mosaic virus, SCMV)

These viruses are transmitted by various genera and species of aphids (e.g., Rhopalosiphum maidis (Fitch)) and rarely by seed (photo 89). When feeding on an infected plant, aphids acquire the virus and immediately begin to spread it. This type of pathogen can affect other hosts, including grasses and cereals such as sorghum, Johnson grass, and sugarcane, but does not attack broadleaf species. Infected plants develop a characteristic mosaic (irregularities in the normal green color distribution) at the base of the younger leaves (photo 90). Sometimes, the appearance of the mosaic is intensified by narrow chlorotic streaks that form along the veins. Later, the younger leaves show general chlorosis and the streaks are larger and more abundant (photo 91). As the plants approach maturity, the foliage turns purplish or reddish. Depending on the stage of development in which the infection occurs, a high degree of stunting may be observed. Plants infected in the early stages produce very small ears or are completely sterile.

It has been reported that in China, SCMV has caused significant damage to maize production.

## Lethal Necrosis of Maize

(Maize lethal necrosis, MLN)

This disease is caused by the combination of two viruses, which can be the maize chlorotic mosaic virus (MCMV) and the maize dwarf mosaic virus (MDMV) or the wheat streak mosaic virus (WSMV). If only the last two are combined, lethal necrosis will not develop. Infected plants do not grow normally, become chlorotic, and die during the flowering period (photo 92). Infected plants do not produce ears during the early stages of development.

## Maize Mosaic Virus I

(Maize mosaic virus I, MMV)

This disease has been detected in many countries around the world. The vector insect is the maize weevil *Peregrinus maidis*, which acquires the virus by feeding on an infected plant and then spreading the infection until it dies. This same vector also transmits the maize streak virus. Hosts of MMV include maize, sorghum, and some other species of grasses. Plants are more susceptible when infected 4 to 6 weeks after emergence. The most noticeable symptoms are stunting and streaking along the veins (photos 93, 94).

The degree of stunting depends on the age of the plant at the time of infection. Due to the shortening of internodes, the leaves appear crowded and erect. Very fine, continuous streaks begin to form at the base of the leaves along the veins. Subsequently, the symptoms include shorter leaves than normal, with a rough and fleshy appearance. The streaks can be a very intense yellow color, which eventually develops into necrotic tissue. Before total tissue necrosis, the foliage takes on a reddish or dark purple color.

## Maize Stripe Virus

(Maize stripe virus, M StV)

This disease has been detected in the tropical regions of Africa, Asia, and the Americas, including Hawaii, India, and Australia.

In the leaves, the initial symptoms consist of small, chlorotic spots that later develop into narrow, chlorotic streaks in younger leaves. The width of the chlorotic streaks can vary and extend from the base to the tip of the leaves (photos 95, 96). Infected plants generally show stunting and the ear bends downwards. The normal development of ears and yield is reduced.

The virus is transmitted by weevils of the genus *Peregrinus maidis*, which acquire the virus by feeding on an infected plant and then spreading the infection until it dies. The vector can also transmit the maize mosaic virus I.

## Maize Streak Virus

(Maize streak virus, MSV)

This disease, which was first reported in eastern Africa, has spread to many other countries in that continent. The virus is transmitted by weevils of the genus *Cicadulina spp.*, with *C. mbila (Naudé)* being the most important species, as it acquires the virus by feeding on an infected plant and then spreading the infection until it dies. The initial symptoms of the disease, consisting of very small, round, and isolated spots on younger leaves, appear one week after inoculation. The number of spots increases as the plant grows. Although the spots develop along the veins, they are soon more abundant at the bases of the leaves and particularly conspicuous in younger leaves. Fully developed leaves show chlorosis with discontinuous yellow streaks along the veins, contrasting with the normal dark green color of the foliage (photos 97, 98). When the infection is severe, there is curling and the plants either die prematurely or do not develop properly. Many cereal and wild grasses serve as hosts for the virus and the vectors.

## Maize Rough Dwarf Virus

(Maize rough dwarf virus, MRDV)

This virus has been known for several years in European and Asian countries, and its variant 'Mal de Río Cuarto' in Argentina and Uruguay. Infected plants show symptoms of stunting. The secondary veins become chlorotic and thickened. The leaves become hardened; younger leaves curl upwards and show excessive growth (enations) on the veins of the underside of the leaf (photo 99). Symptoms can be detected in seedlings approximately one month old. In later stages, infected plants take on a reddish color, do not produce ears, or if they do, the ears are very small and often have a bent tip. The ear and upper leaves do not develop properly (photo 100).

The virus is transmitted by several species of leafhoppers, including *Laodelphax striatellus* (which transmits MRDV) and *Delphacodes kuscheli* (which transmits MRCV). The vector acquires the virus by feeding on an infected plant and then spreading the infection until it dies; the females transmit the virus to the next generation through their eggs.

It has been reported that the *Mal de Río Cuarto* virus has caused significant damage to maize production in the central region of Argentina, and MRDV in the north of China.

## Corn Streak Mosaic Virus

(Corn Streak Mosaic Virus, CSMV)

"Streak mosaic" refers to a pattern of light and dark green mottling on the leaves, caused by a virus transmitted by the weevil *Dalbulus maidis*. The weevil acquires the virus by feeding on infected plants and then spreads the infection to healthy plants. *Dalbulus maidis* is also a vector for the maize stunt Spiroplasma and the maize bushy stunt fitoplasma. This virus is found from the southern United States to South America, including the Caribbean, and has been observed to reduce yields by up to 43% in several Central American countries.

Weevils can carry more than one of these pathogens at the same time, which is why mixed infections are common. Symptoms appear about two weeks after the plants have been infected. Small, isolated chlorotic spots can be easily seen by placing the leaves against the light (photo 101). Later, the spots become more numerous and merge, forming streaks of 5 to 10 cm along the veins (photo 102). If the infection occurs during flowering, the plants may not show symptoms, but if it occurs during the seedling stage, the kernels will not develop properly and the filling will be poor.

## Maize Bushy Stunt

(Maize Bushy Stunt, MBS) Maize bushy stunt fitoplasma, *Spiroplasma maidis*

This disease has been detected in several countries, from the southern United States to Argentina. The pathogen is transmitted by the weevil *Dalbulus maidis*, *D. elimatus* and other *Dalbulus* species. The weevil acquires the virus by feeding on an infected plant and then spreads the infection to healthy plants. The same weevils can transmit both the CSMV and the maize stunt Spiroplasma, which is why mixed infections are common.

The pathogen is a non-helical mollicute called fitoplasma. Maize bushy stunt is more common in relatively cool climates, while warm and humid climates favor maize stunt.

Infected plants show a variety of symptoms, depending on the maize genotype. The most common symptoms are chlorosis on the margins of young leaves, and the tips gradually turn a reddish-purple as they mature.

A conspicuous symptom is excessive stem elongation, which also turns reddish-purple and chlorotic (photo 103) and is more common in highland germplasm. The symptoms on the leaves are most noticeable as flowering approaches. The axillary buds develop into sterile ears. When the plant is infected at the beginning of its development, ears develop on many of the nodes, but their diameter and kernel size are reduced, which greatly reduces the yield (photo 104).

At a glance, it is not possible to distinguish between the symptoms caused by maize bushy stunt fitoplasma and those caused by the maize stunt Spiroplasma in the field.

## Maize Stunt

(Corn stunt, CS) *Spiroplasma kunkeli*

This disease is known in warm, humid lowlands in several countries in Central and South America, the Caribbean, the southeastern United States, and Mexico, although it is also found at elevations of over 2,000 meters. The disease is transmitted by weevils of the *Dalbulus* genus, including *D. maidis*, *D. elimatus* and other less important species. The weevils acquire the virus by feeding on an infected plant and then spread the infection to healthy plants. The same weevils can transmit both the CSMV and the maize stunt Spiroplasma, which is why mixed infections are common. The pathogen is the helical mollicute *Spiroplasma kunkelii*.

Infected plants show a variety of symptoms, depending on the maize genotype. The most common symptoms appear on the leaves, which turn reddish or purplish, and yellow (photo 105), and show chlorotic streaks at the base of young leaves, whose tips may turn reddish-purple (photo 106). The foliar symptoms usually appear as flowering approaches. The stunt or shortening of the plants is due to shortened internodes. The axillary buds develop into sterile ears, and there is excessive branching of the roots. In severe cases, the plants may not produce ears, or if they do, their diameter is significantly reduced, or kernel formation is poor. The plants die prematurely.
