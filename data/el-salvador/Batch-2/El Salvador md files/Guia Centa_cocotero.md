## BOTANICAL AND MORPHOLOGICAL ASPECTS

Coconut palms belong to the family Arecaceae, and are plants that can grow to heights of over 30 meters. They have a smooth, light gray trunk that originates from a prominent base and ends in a green crown of long, pinnate leaves with numerous clusters of nuts emerging from the leaf axils. According to taxonomic studies, *Cocos nucifera* is monotypic.

## Trunk:

The trunk has a single stem, developing from a terminal bud at the center of the upper part; during the first few years, very short internodes develop, from which many adventitious roots sprout; once the trunk has reached its full thickness, its elongation increases, and it emerges from the ground. The base of the trunk widens, depending on the variety; most dwarf varieties do not have a swollen trunk base. Changes in the trunk diameter (spiling) are due to the availability of water. The height varies depending on the different varieties, the age of the plant, and the ecological conditions in which it grows.

## Roots:

As a monocot, the coconut palm does not have a main root. From the initial stage of germination, adventitious roots develop, originating from the base of the trunk. In a mature plant, the number depends on the variety, the growing conditions, the age, and the health of the palm (it can have up to 5,000). The roots emerge from all sides of the trunk, growing mainly in the first layer of soil, horizontally; they reach a considerable length, with the main ones having a diameter of up to 1 centimeter, and produce a constant supply of secondary roots that branch abundantly. Most of the root mass is found in the first meter of soil; in sandy soils, they can reach up to 6 meters in length.

## Leaves:

A mature palm leaf can reach a length of 6 to 10 meters, with a weight of 10 to 20 kilograms, and a leaf surface area of 7 to 8 square meters. Dwarf palm leaves reach 3 to 4 meters, while young leaves can grow by 2 centimeters per day; in healthy, mature palms, they grow by 5 to 6 centimeters per day. The length of the petiole is one-fourth of the total length of the leaf. The number of leaflets on a tall palm leaf varies from 200 to 250, with the first ones being short at the base, the following ones gradually increasing in length, reaching up to 130 centimeters, one-third of the midrib, and becoming smaller towards the apex, where they reach up to 25 centimeters. The leaflets have rigid central veins, with veins on both sides. The number of leaves on a tall palm varies from 30 to 40, depending on the coconut variety and the conditions in which it grows. Each year, 14 to 16 new leaves are formed, with a lifespan of 4 years, from their formation to abscission.

## CULTIVATION OF COCONUT

## Flowers:

The inflorescence of the coconut palm is a spathe (also known as an areola) that develops in the axil of each leaf. It is a modified axillary bud, so the number of leaves produced per year determines the number of inflorescences. When the inflorescence grows, the spathe opens at the apex, revealing the inflorescence and spreading.

The spadix is a main axis with 20 to 60 spikelets that produce female and male flowers. The female flowers are usually located at the base of the spikelets, while the rest is covered with male flowers. Each spikelet can have 15 to 30 female flowers and 200 to 300 male flowers, depending on the variety; in some cases, hermaphrodite flowers are also found.

The male flowers open after the opening of the spadix, and are located on the sides of the female flowers; two days after releasing their pollen, they wither and fall. In tall coconut palms, the shedding of pollen from an inflorescence takes 18 to 22 days.

The female flower is larger than the male flower and its shape resembles that of a small fruit; the ovary increases in size after a few weeks. When the female flower is receptive, it opens at the apex. This phase lasts 3 days in tall palms and 2 days in dwarf palms.

In favorable conditions, dwarf palms begin to flower in the third year; the male flowers open and release their pollen at the same time as the female flowers are receptive, so dwarf coconut palms are self-pollinating. In tall coconut palms, flowering begins in the fifth to seventh year; generally, the opening of the male flowers does not coincide with the receptivity of the female flowers, so these palms are cross-pollinating (require pollen from other inflorescences of the same plant or from other plants). Hybrid palms begin to flower in the fourth year. The flowering of coconut palms can be delayed by several years due to unfavorable conditions, such as water stress and nutrient deficiencies, or excessive shade.

Pollination occurs through wind and some insects such as bees, wasps, ants, and some flies.

## Fruit:

It is a drupe. To reach full maturity, it requires between 10 and 12 months, depending on the variety. Initially, the husk and shell grow in size, not in thickness, and the embryonic cavity is filled with liquid. After 4 months, the husk and shell/endocarp acquire more thickness. This growth lasts for 2 months. After 6 months of fruiting, the solid endosperm forms inside the cavity. This first layer is thin and gelatinous. When the fruits are ripe, they tend to detach, although in some varieties, they remain on the plant for several more months. The wall

The final weight and volume of the fruit vary between varieties, and its size depends on abundant rainfall and the growing conditions during formation. In nature, coconut trees are found in yellow, gold, green, and brown colors. The shape of the fruit varies from round to oval (fig. 2).

The epicarp (outer surface) has a thickness of 0.10 millimeters, the fibrous husk (mesocarp) can vary in thickness from 1 to more than 5 centimeters, reaching up to 10 centimeters at the base of the nut.

## Seed:

It consists of the endocarp, endosperm, embryo, and water; just like the fruit, it varies in shape (elongated, oval, or almost round).

Dwarf coconut trees usually have a round seed, the size of the seed does not always correspond to the size of the fruit. How often do large coconuts have small seeds, while small coconuts can have large seeds and a relatively thin husk. The husk/endocarp is very hard and varies in thickness from 3 to 6 millimeters. At the base of the nut, the embryo is attached to the endosperm.

The endosperm has variable hardness and thickness; in dwarf coconuts, it can reach 1 centimeter, and in tall ones, 1.5. The dehydrated endosperm (copra) of tall varieties weighs between 200 and 250 grams.

## DEVELOPMENT STAGES OF THE COCONUT TREE

Germination: when the fruit is ripe, the development of the embryo and germination begins. The germination phase until the emergence of the shoot lasts from 60 to 120 days and depends on the variety and the humidity of the fruit.

Juvenile: this period starts from the emergence of the plant until the emergence of the first inflorescence. In this stage, tall plants produce 10 to 11 leaves in the first year; dwarf plants produce 13 to 14, and hybrid dwarf plants produce 12 leaves.

Productive phase: this period starts from the beginning of flowering until the cessation of flowering. Tall coconut trees, on average, reach this phase at 70 years; in the Pacific, coconut trees have been found in production for more than 75 years (Isla El Espíritu Santo, Usulután; and Caluco, Sonsonate). Dwarf coconut trees reach the productive phase at 50 years; some hybrids reach this age in 50 years.

## CLIMATIC AND EDAPHIC REQUIREMENTS

In El Salvador, it can be cultivated in the coastal zone with annual rainfall of 1500 to 2000 mm. Excessive rainfall affects pollination and fertilization. The optimal average annual temperature varies from 22° to 30°C.

The relative humidity of the air determines the water absorption by the palm tree. The coconut reduces the opening of its stomata when it is below 60%, even when the soil moisture is at field capacity, humidity influences the development of diseases.

The coconut adapts to altitudes of 0 to 450 msnm; it requires a minimum of 2000 hours of sunlight per year.

It adapts to a variety of soils in the tropical zone, with sandy and alluvial soils up to moderately clayey soils. They must have good internal and external drainage; with a pH of 5.0 to 7.5.

## VARIETIES

Coconut trees are divided into three groups: tall, dwarf, and intermediate; each group contains a series of varieties belonging to two different types, Niu Kafa and Niu Vai, which are intermediate types.

Niu Kafa is characterized by having scarce, large, round fruits, slow germination, with thick husks, fusiform nuts with thick husks, and thick endosperm with a high oil content.

Niu Vai produces more fruits than the previous one; with round shape, thin husks and husks that germinate earlier than Niu Kafa. The selection of these palms is due to their ability to resist storm damage, as they have a wide base and a larger trunk circumference.

In El Salvador, tall coconut trees are referred to as "Alto del Pacífico." This type of palm is common along the Pacific coast of Mexico and Central America. Approximately 90% of commercial crops are derived from this germplasm. There are also dwarf varieties known as "Enano Malasino," which are distinguished by the color of their fruit: Malasino Yellow, Golden, and Red, as well as Green and Filipino Dwarf.

Hybrids: These are the result of cross-breeding between different varieties (tall with tall, tall with dwarf, and dwarf with dwarf). ## Recommendations for the nursery.

When the seedlings are approximately 30 cm tall, select the most robust ones, free of pests, to establish the nursery in the soil or in bags.

## Nursery in the soil:

When preparing the soil, it should be well-drained, free of stones and clods. Add organic matter or straw (at 50%) to promote internal drainage, good root development, and easy plant extraction.

When transplanting directly into the field, they should be 50 cm or more (3 to 4 months after germination). If there is no irrigation available, it is recommended to do this at the beginning of the rainy season.

## CONTAINER CULTIVATION

## Nursery setup:

- Use black, UV-resistant polyethylene bags, 20/100 mm thick and 40 x 40 cm (16x16 inches) in size.
- Punch 5 mm diameter holes in the lower half of the bag to facilitate drainage, with 50 holes in a row, leaving the lowest one 5 cm from the bottom.
- Mix black soil (75%) with sawdust (25%) to fill the bags; deposit the mixture to a level that leaves space for the plant.
- Once you have enough bags prepared for transplanting, remove the selected seedlings; these should be carefully removed, avoiding damage to the roots. This can be done with a trowel or spade.
- Place the seedlings in the bags, ensuring the roots are in a good position without being crushed, and then fill the remaining space with the mixture.
- Create plant blocks, spaced 40 cm apart when they are dwarf, and 60 cm apart when they are tall.

Transplanting into containers means a higher cost for the producer, but this will be offset by the advantages of using this technique:

- It prevents the seedlings from being in contact, reducing the risk of etiolation (when seedlings turn yellow and elongated due to lack of sunlight).
- It reduces the risk of leaf diseases.
- It makes it easier to move the plants.

## Nursery management

The areas where the nursery and the field will be established must be protected from animal intrusion; they should be free of weeds, both between the bags and within them. Weeds between the bags can be controlled chemically with herbicides such as glyphosate and diuron, and those within the bags, manually.

If the conditions are dry or during a prolonged summer, regular watering is required. In this case, a sprinkler system is recommended, as the distribution of the droplets is better and provides a fine mist that reaches the plants evenly.

Monitor regularly to detect and combat pests; a preventive measure is to maintain a 20-meter strip free of weeds around the perimeter.

An indicator that the plants are ready and suitable for transplanting is that their middle and most recent leaves are pinnate (palm-shaped) (Figure 12). ## CULTURAL PRACTICES

## Soil preparation

The soil where the planting will be established must have good drainage, and be free of weeds, trees or shrubs, to facilitate tillage, plowing and planting.

If leguminous cover crops are used to control weeds, they should be established 6 months before planting. Some of the most commonly used for this purpose are: Pueraria phaseoloides "Kudzu" and a mixture of Kudzu with Calopogonium caeruleum and Centrosema pubescens, which can give the best results for more than a year.

## Planting spacing

The recommended planting spacing for the three-row system is:

- Monoculture: 8 x 8 meters (180 plants per hectare or 126 per plot)
- Intercropping: 10 x 10 meters (115 per hectare or 80 per plot)
- In association: 9 x 9 meters (143 per hectare or 100 per plot)

For all the indicated spacings, plant in square or triangular patterns (three-row or "foot of the rooster"), to make the best use of the available land.

## Planting Holes

Planting holes should be 50 cm deep and 40 cm in diameter; it is desirable to carry out this practice one month before planting.

## Planting

Once the holes and plants are prepared, 30 g of phosphorus should be added to the bottom of each hole. Whether planting with root ball or seedling in a bag, the holes should be filled with the best soil to about one-quarter of their capacity. Ideally, the plant should be planted with the top of the nut level with the ground or slightly covered with a thin layer of soil; otherwise, seedlings that are heavily covered may suffer from neck rot due to excessive moisture.

In dry conditions, coconut husks can be buried with the concave side up to help retain moisture and improve nutrition.

The seedling should be carefully placed in its bag in the previously mentioned depth. The bag, which has been carefully torn at the bottom, should be removed, ensuring that the soil does not spill out. The hole should then be filled with the remaining soil. The plant should be aligned with the other plants or stakes, and the soil around it should be firmly pressed down with the foot to prevent air pockets that could become waterlogged and damage the roots.

## Replanting

As with all perennial crops, some seedlings will be lost at the beginning due to weak development, poor drainage, mechanical damage, insect attacks, rodents, diseases, and therefore, to maintain a uniform population and ensure higher yields, it is recommended to replant the areas that are affected. To this end, the farmer should reserve an average of 5% of the originally planted number of plants.

## C O C O T E R O C U L T I V A T I O N

## Weeds

Weeds compete with the crop for nutrients, water, and light, and this competition is particularly intense in sandy soils and with low rainfall; therefore, before planting, it is necessary to control them.

Maintain a 1.5 m radius around the trunk of the plant free of weeds, carrying out cleanings every two months; in the case of plantations without cleanings, they should be carried out at the beginning of the rainy season. As the plant grows, the weed-free area should be gradually expanded. After cutting, the weeds should be left under the palm tree's canopy, so that as they decompose, they add organic matter to the soil. Weed control is carried out mechanically or chemically; in the areas between the rows and palm trees, it is carried out with a hoe or cultivator. This practice is recommended in the first two years of planting. The machinery is used when the weeds reach 50 cm:

The damage caused by weeds is greatest in the first three years of planting. To avoid this situation, it is recommended to plant companion crops or annual or biennial crops, as the management of these benefits the coconut trees by controlling soil pests and by providing fertilization that is utilized by the coconut tree.

Weeds between rows and in the rows can be controlled with systemic herbicides such as Glyphosate or 2, 4-D, at a rate of 2 liters per hectare, or Halosulfuron at 140 g/ha, dissolved in 250 to 300 liters of water, when the plant stem is more than two meters high. For application, clean water and nozzles such as TJ-8002, TJ 9003, TJ 11002 or their equivalents should be used. With two annual applications of herbicides, combined with three passes with machinery, good weed control can be achieved.

Weeds can be controlled using cover crops such as forage millet (Arachis pintoi), sword bean (Cannavalia ensiformes), tropical kudzu (Pueraria phaseoloides), dolichos (Dolichus lablab), which, in addition to suppressing weeds, fix nitrogen in the soil, retain moisture, and add organic matter to the soil.

## Coconut Weevil and Scale Insects

## 1 Coconut Weevil (Rhynchophorus palmarum)

This pest causes economic damage in areas where coconut palms are grown. When no control measures are taken, the losses are severe, and in certain areas of the country, more than 50% of the plants have been lost after 10 years of establishment.

The damage is caused by a larva that creates tunnels or galleries within the plant. In addition to the direct damage it causes, the adult is a vector for the nematode Bursaphelenchus cocophilus, the causal agent of "Red Ring Disease," which is responsible for the death of the plants.

## PESTS

## Distribution

R. palmarum is an American species found throughout California, Brazil, and the Caribbean, from Trinidad to Saint Vincent.

Because it is a polyphagous insect, it can survive under adverse conditions, and it has few natural enemies that can significantly reduce its populations.

## Host Plants

Coconut palm (Cocos nucifera L.); African palm (Elaeis guineensis Jacq.); papaya (Carica papaya); pineapple (Ananas comosus); sugarcane (Saccharum officinarum).

## Life Cycle

The weevil's life cycle is 120 to 170 days, encompassing the egg, larva, pupa, and adult stages. The egg to pupa stages occur within the plant. Adults are 3 to 5 cm long, black in color, with an elongated head (beak-like), covered in dark brown scales on the male and smooth on the female; they have nocturnal flight habits, flying in the morning (until 10 a.m.) and in the afternoon (from 4 to 7 p.m.). Females can lay up to 900 eggs in wounds on the trunk and crown. They can lay up to 63 eggs per day on different plants; the egg stage lasts for 4 to 6 days. The larvae remain within the trunk for 60 days. The pupae are found in a cocoon or puparium formed from coconut fibers; after 28 days, the adult emerges, which lives for about 60 days, completing the life cycle of this insect. Adults exhibit sexual dimorphism; the male has scales on the rostrum (beak), resembling a fine brush, while the female's rostrum is smooth (Photo 13).

## Damage

The damage caused by this insect begins when the female lays eggs; the larvae, upon hatching, bore into the trunk, creating tunnels upwards and downwards. The larvae can feed within the trunk for up to 60 days. A single larva can consume up to 500 grams of trunk tissue. If the perforations occur at the growth point (crown), the tree dies. When the attack is at the base, the overall appearance is diseased, but the plant can continue to live for years, with a decrease in yield. The external symptoms of damage depend on the part of the plant that is affected; the bud or terminal shoot initially turns yellow, then bends and falls. If the bud is cut, a complete rotting is observed, with a large number of larvae in active or pupal stages. Another symptom is observed in the leaves, which become curled, twisted, and brown; when the larval population is abundant.

The Coconut Weevil is also a vector for a nematode that causes the disease known as "Red Ring Disease," which can kill the plant within 6 months to 1 year.

## Control

## Natural Enemies

Among the natural enemies of the weevil are the beetle Xanthopygus sp, which feeds on eggs and small larvae. This predator is active in the larval and adult stages, with a life cycle of one month. Another beetle associated with the weevil is Leionata sp. This is found in decaying tissue. During the rainy season, when there is high humidity, some fungi and bacteria attack the weevil pupae.

## Scales

The following scales have been found in the cultivation: Aspidiotus (Aspidiotus destructor), Florida red scale (Chrisomphalus aonidium L), and Oriental scale (Aonidiela orientalis Newstead)

## Biology and Habits

Armored scales are adapted to disperse to different host plants, remaining attached to them, sucking sap, throughout their life cycle. They are dispersed when infested coconut or other palm host plants are transported to new locations.

## Distribution

Found in plantations from near sea level to intermediate valleys (around 460 msnm).

## Damage

Damages plants in the nursery and in established plantations. It manifests as yellowing and subsequent necrosis of the leaf tips, with a burnt appearance, due to the loss of sap and the blockage of stomata; consequently, the plant's vitality decreases. In severe attacks, premature fruit fall.

## Control

Scales appear in plantations in a localized manner, so it is necessary to perform a sampling before applying control measures. When the first infested plants are found, the damaged leaves should be pruned and burned. If the damage is widespread, the application of agricultural oils and the release of coccinellid beetles (predators of scales) is recommended.

## Pale Scale (Myndus crudus Van Duzee)

## Biology and Habits

This insect lives in grasslands, in a nymph stage, feeding on the phloem of numerous palm trees, including coconut palms. When feeding on a diseased plant and a healthy one, it transmits the disease.

The palm weevil exhibits sexual dimorphism, with females being larger than males. Females measure 5 mm in length, while males measure 4 mm. The head and thorax are grayish, sometimes brownish; the abdomen is greenish, with transparent forewings and brownish veins.

## Cultural Control

Removing and burning diseased palm trees, manually or mechanically (using chainsaws), reduces the likelihood that the vector will acquire the pathogen from diseased plants. Using cover crops to suppress grasses reduces the insect population.

Removing weeds to reduce the vector population. This can be done manually, mechanically, or by spraying with herbicides.

## Chemical Control

Applying Diazinon at a concentration of 1 liter/100 liters of water, or Endosulfan at 2.8 liters per hectare, or Bifentrin at 0.6 liters per hectare.

## Genetic Control

Control by planting tolerant cultivars, such as: Dwarf Malasino: Yellow, Golden, and Green, with 90%, 80%, and 60% tolerance to the disease, respectively; and Panama Tall, with 50%. The cross between Dwarf Malasino and Panama Tall has produced a hybrid known as Maypan, with good agronomic characteristics and tolerance to the disease.

## Cultural Control

- Maintain the crop free of host plants for the vector. This can be done manually, mechanically, or by spraying with herbicides, such as 2-4-D or Glyphosate, at a dose of 2 liters per hectare.
- Remove and burn affected plants in areas of advance, to reduce the likelihood that the vector will acquire the pathogen.

## Control

Remove infected material by cutting off the diseased parts and applying Bordeaux paste or copper-based paste to the cracks; it can also be controlled using Benomil or Mancozeb, at a dose of 10 grams per liter of water.

## Collar Rot (Phytophthora palmivora)

It appears in the seedling, but is more common in plants over two years old. It is a fungus that generally attacks the meristematic area. It requires high relative humidity and abundant rainfall for its development. Early symptoms are difficult to detect, as they only present with wilting and drooping of new leaves (1 and 2), which are hidden by the others. In advanced stages, the central leaf turns brown and bends at its base. Preventative chemical control is achieved with Fosetil-Al and Metalaxil + Mancozeb, which have given good results.

## Red Ring Nematode (Bursaphelenchus cocophilus)

This is an obligate endoparasite of palm trees, with its entire life cycle, from egg to adult, within the plant's tissue. It is transmitted between coconut palms by *R. palmarum*. Occasionally, it can be found in the soil around the roots of infected plants.

## Host Plants

Coconut palm (Cocos nucifera), Oil palm (Elaeis Guineensis Jacq.) and other palm species.

## Coconut Production

When the coconut is intended for copra production, in high-yielding varieties, the harvest should be carried out between the 11th and 12th month, after the opening of the inflorescence. In the Maypán hybrid, the harvest will be done in the tenth month.

## For Seed Production

In this case, a preliminary selection of the seed plants, the harvest will be carried out when the epidermis is light brown. When sampling the endocarp (shell), the color should be black. It is important to avoid damaging the seeds. Immediately after harvesting, they should be moved to the areas designated for nurseries.

## Harvesting Methods

- Cutting the bunches and lowering them with a rope to avoid damaging them. This type of harvest is carried out when the product is intended for fresh consumption.
- Using towers. In this case, the towers are mounted on a cart pulled by a tractor, and a pole is used to reach the bunches. The operator cuts them and collects them in a cart for transport.
- Using a bamboo pole whose height reaches the bunches. At the tip of the thinnest part, a crescent-shaped blade is attached to cut the ripe bunches and the old leaves.

## Domestic Market

The Salvadoran market presents an unmet demand, leading to the import of fresh and dried coconut. The origin of this product is mainly imported from Guatemala, and to a lesser extent from Costa Rica.
