## INTRODUCTION

In El Salvador, the genus *Annona* includes several species, which are highly sought after for their delicious pulp and are consumed as fruit. Among these, the pink and white anonas (*Annona diversifolia*) are particularly valuable commercially, as they are preferred in the local market, generating good income for producers.

The information presented in this guide is the result of research, experience, and dedication by the technical team of the Fruit Program at CENTA, as well as the practices of producers. This document serves as a didactic tool for technicians, students, producers, and the general public, to provide information on the recommended agronomic techniques for cultivating this crop.

The guide covers general information about the crop, botanical aspects, climatic and soil requirements. It describes the morphological characteristics of the different *Annona* species. It details the propagation stages, from the seedling stage, through nurseries, and the type of grafting used. The establishment of the crop includes soil preparation, planting, staking, planting system, spacing, pruning, and soil conservation practices. Agronomic management includes pruning, fertilization, and pest control. It also provides general information on harvesting, post-harvest handling, and marketing.

## IMPORTANCE

*Annona diversifolia* is native to Central America and Mexico. It is considered one of the best tropical fruits and the best *Annona* species for warm climates.

The genus *Annona* includes around 120 species in warm, tropical climates, primarily in the tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas. Of these, only 10 species have significant agronomic importance. This species is found in Mexico, Guatemala, El Salvador, Costa Rica, Miami, Florida (USA), Bolivia, Ecuador, and Peru (IBGRI, 1986, Ponce, 1978, Popenoe, 1920, and Vidal, 1993).

*Annona* is a small tree that produces the pink and white anona fruits. It is considered the species that produces the finest and most delicious fruit, with a unique flavor. It is also the most commonly cultivated species in the country, with a high commercial value due to its preference in the local market, generating good income for producers. It is traditionally grown in open spaces or home gardens.

## Fruit

## ANONA

The leaves are simple and alternate, elliptical to oblanceolate, 8 to 16 cm long, with a rounded apex. At the base of young branches or floral peduncles, there are circular, leaf-like bracts, which are characteristic of this species. The plant is relatively small and delicate, with branching from the base, and an upright growth habit with a dominant central stem. It can reach a height of 5 to 10 meters.

## Flowers

The flowers are hermaphroditic, with a tripartite perianth; androecium with 6 to more stamens; apocarpous gynoecium with superior ovaries. They appear during new growth, solitary or in groups of two to three external petals, which are dark red to brown, 2.5 to 3 cm long.

In the ethnobotanical study of the anona, carried out by Chávez Primitivo et al (1995), two flowering periods (early and late) were identified. The early flowering occurs between March and April, while the late flowering occurs at the end of May to early June.

## Seeds

The seeds are oblong-ovoid, with a glossy brown color. They have a period of dormancy, and often do not germinate easily.

## Phenology

The anona plant changes its leaves between March and April. The inflorescence begins in March, with July, August, and September being the months of peak harvest.

## CLIMATIC AND EDAPHIC REQUIREMENTS

The cultivation of the anona (pink or white) is considered a rustic fruit, with the ability to tolerate adverse conditions, typical of soils with a sandy, loamy, or clay texture, well-drained, slightly acidic (pH 5.5-6.5), with a rainfall of 1800 to 2200 mm. The plant requires a warm climate, with temperatures between 20 and 25°C, with a dry and humid season. It adapts well to altitudes of 100 to 800 meters above sea level, in sandy-loamy or clay soils, which allow for good vegetative and productive growth of the crop.

## PROPAGATION

There are two ways to propagate Anona: by seed (sexual) and vegetatively (asexual).

Sexual propagation (by seed)

This is the traditional and most commonly used method by producers. It is recommended to collect seeds during the harvest period and plant them the following year, as seeds sown immediately after harvest do not germinate due to the dormant period.

The seeds should be selected from trees with excellent production, good phytosanitary conditions, and fruits of good size and flavor.

Research conducted in Mexico on pregermination treatments of Anona seeds report that the highest germination rate was 20.5% with scarification, adding gibberellic acid at 500 mg per 24 hours. Similarly, Anona diversifolia seeds stored for a period of two years lose their germinative capacity (Marroquín and Hernández, 1995). Campbell and Popenoe 1967, state that the seeds are inactive for several months after the fruit ripens.

## Advantages

- Greater genetic diversity.
- Ease of obtaining clones. These can be used as clones: A. muricata, A. purpúrea; A. squamosa and A. glabra.
- Unevenness in production and fruit quality.

## Seed Handling

After separating the seeds from the pulp, they are washed and dried in the shade for two to three days on paper or any other type of material that can absorb moisture. They are stored in bags at room temperature for a period of 7 to 12 months. This is the time required to achieve up to 90% germination.

## Seedling

## 1. Substrate and disinfection

It is recommended to use a porous substrate with good drainage to prevent the development and proliferation of pathogens and to allow good aeration of the seeds, which benefits the growth of the seedling. Use 50% black soil, 30% sand, and 20% decomposed matter. The disinfection of the substrate should be carried out with 100 cc of Carbendazin plus 150 cc of propamocarb per 100 liters of organic water.

## 2. Planting bed

The length will depend on the number of plants required, and the width should be 1.0 m to facilitate the management of the seedling. It should be clean of weeds and well-drained. The disinfection of the substrate should be carried out with Carbofuran, 10 to 12

## 3. Planting the seedling

The seeds should be placed horizontally, with a spacing of 2.0 cm between them, at a depth of 2.0 cm and 5.0 cm between rows. Germination begins after 20 to 25 days. Seedlings grow best in the shade, so it is necessary to regulate the amount of light, using a shade cloth or screen.

## Nursery

In the nursery, 9 x 12" bags (300 - 350 mesh) are used. Transplanting the seedlings to the bags is carried out when the seedlings are between 10 and 12 cm tall, selecting those with good development.

The main agricultural activities that should be carried out for the good management and development of the plants in the nursery are:

- Removal of suckers (to favor the growth of the stem)
- Watering should be done three times a week, in the morning and afternoon.
- Fertilization in the bag: place 4.0 g of ammonium sulfate or 2.0 g of urea per plant and supplement with foliar applications to maintain the plants in continuous and vigorous growth.
- Weeds should be removed manually to prevent them from affecting the growth of the plants.
- Remove sick plants to prevent the spread of pests.

## Grafting

It is carried out through side grafting, which is the most recommended method. The Anona species used as rootstock can be: A. muricata (guanaba), A. reticulata (anona colorada), A. (anona de manglar), A. purpúrea (sincuya) and the same A. diversifolia. (Anona). glabra

## Vegetative propagation

Grafting is carried out when the stem of the plants reaches a diameter of 1 cm. This occurs between 6 and 8 months. After grafting, three months should pass before transplanting to the final location.

## Advantages of vegetative propagation

- Guaranteed plants with the same genetic identity.
- Early production.
- Better production and quality of the fruit.

## Disadvantages of asexual propagation

- Incompatibility between the rootstock and the scion variety.
- When grafting fails, it takes more time to attempt grafting again.
- Lack of scion wood.
- Requires skilled personnel.

## Selection of rootstock or scion.

The plants used as rootstock or scions should be healthy, well-developed, and vigorous, with easy bark stripping.

## ESTABLISHING THE CULTIVATION

When establishing an Anona plantation, factors such as climate, topography, wind, soil, and water should be considered.

## Soil preparation

Proper soil preparation is essential for successful cultivation: If the soil contains weeds, they need to be controlled manually, mechanically, or chemically. If using chemicals, use glyphosate at a rate of 1.5-2.1 I/ha. A clean soil facilitates agricultural work for establishing the plantation. If there are crop residues from previous harvests, the soil preparation should be done in advance, incorporating them to facilitate their decomposition. This cultural practice also helps control soil pests.

## Trenching and planting.

Trenching and planting aim to create a better arrangement of plants in the field. The planting system, the slope of the land, the position of the plantation relative to the sun (to maximize light exposure), and the direction of the wind (to avoid damage to grafts, flowering, fruits, and branches) should be considered.

## Planting systems.

The planting system refers to the distribution of plants in the field. It should consider the growth habit of the species or variety, the topography, soil texture, and climate. Planting systems can be:

- In rows.

To make the most of the land during the first years of Anona establishment, it can be associated with other fruit species such as passion fruit, papaya, and some vegetables.

## Spacing

The soil conditions and the cultivar to be used determine the spacing between plants. Other factors, such as soil fertility, climate, and water availability, should also be considered. Modern horticulture aims to obtain plants of smaller size and vigor, with acceptable production, while increasing the number of plants per unit area and facilitating different cultivation practices. Spacing can range from 4 x 4 m to 5 x 5 m for grafted plants, and from 6 x 6 m to 7 x 7 m for non-grafted plants.

## Planting.

Once the planting distances have been defined, the planting process should be carried out during the last week of April or the first week of May.

The depth of the planting hole should be determined according to the type of soil. In soils with a franco-sandy texture, it is recommended to dig holes of 0.30 x 0.30 x 0.30 m, and in soils with a higher clay content, it is necessary to dig holes of 0.40 x 0.40 x 0.40 m. The hole should be filled with a mixture of soil and well-decomposed organic matter (approximately 4 kg per plant).

## Planting

Planting should be carried out at the beginning of the rainy season or at any time of the year; if plants are available, they should be planted in the center of each hole, removing the plastic bag. Compact the soil around the plant to prevent air pockets that could damage it. Water the plant.

## Pruning

Pruning is carried out to form vigorous, well-shaped and healthy trees, to obtain high-quality fruit, to facilitate harvesting, fertilization and pest control.

Recommendations for pruning:

- Disinfect tools with chlorine or formaldehyde.
- Make cuts without leaving stubs that protrude above the level of the stem.
- Protect the cut part of the tree with a copper-based product.

## Fertilization

To obtain good yields and fruit quality, fertilization is recommended. Before planting, soil samples should be taken and sent to a laboratory for analysis, in order to obtain a fertilization plan for the crop. It is advisable to carry out three fertilizations: at the beginning of the rainy season, two months after the first fertilization, and at the end of the rainy season. Reinforce during the flowering and fruiting period with 2 or 3 foliar applications.

It is ideal to have a fertilization plan; if one is not available, the second annex presents a program that will serve as a guide to provide the necessary nutrients to the crop.

When fertilizing, it is important to consider the age of the plant and the type of soil.

## Irrigation

This ensures that plants maintain a constant flow of water and nutrients from the soil to the leaves, resulting in more vigorous, productive trees with larger fruits. It has been observed that providing water during the flowering stage leads to increased fruit production.

## Selection of irrigation method.

The choice of method depends on the availability of water, soil type, topography, climate, availability of labor, and energy.

## The most commonly used systems are:

## Micro-sprinklers

This system operates at low pressure (1 to 3 atmospheres) and uses micro-sprinklers that discharge 20-175 liters per hour.

## Drip irrigation

This system also operates at low pressure (0.3 to 1 atmosphere). The discharge rate can be 2 to 10 liters per hour. It allows for efficient watering and greater versatility and adaptability in the field.

## Irrigation frequency.

The frequency of irrigation depends on the type of soil; the water requirement and the consumption rate. In sandy soils, frequent irrigations with a low flow rate are preferred, to keep the root zone moist and to ensure that fertilizers are better dissolved.

A practical way to calculate the amount of water required per tree per day is through the following formula:

Gallons (plant/day) = 0.623 x Root zone area x number of plants x Eto / Efficiency of the drip irrigation system.

## Where:

- Eto = Potential evapotranspiration per day in inches per day; the maximum evapotranspiration of the area should be considered.
- 0.623 = Conversion factor that relates the area in square feet to gallons of water.

## Pests

Pest control should be preventative and ongoing throughout the crop cycle, with particular attention paid to the beginning of flowering and fruit development.

## Weeds

To achieve better fertilizer utilization, increased yields, and reduce the impact of other pests, it is important to control weeds, as they serve as hosts and obstruct access to the plantation during agricultural operations. Weed control is carried out manually (pruning), mechanically, or by applying chemical products, such as glyphosate (which controls grasses and broadleaf weeds), at a dose of 1.5 to 2.1 liters/hectare. Green cover crops, such as legumes, also help to keep the plantation clean and promote nitrogen fixation. However, this practice is not carried out in anona cultivation.

## Insects

Fruit borer (Bephratelloide sp)

This insect causes damage to the fruit, causing perforations from the skin, reaching the seed, where it feeds and completes its life cycle.

Control: This is carried out using perforated plastic bags to protect the fruit. The bag acts as a barrier and prevents egg laying and the entry of small larvae. This bag must meet certain requirements to be effective: the plastic must have a special thickness, with perforations on the side walls, ensuring adequate ventilation and preventing moisture. Other control measures include collecting or cutting damaged fruits and burying them.

## Pyrallid

The adult lays its eggs on the fruit, where it completes its life cycle. The damage it causes to the fruit is a black secretion, causing rot inside.

Control: Remove fruits with perforations, collect and bury the damaged ones to prevent the insects from completing their life cycle. When the fruits are small, Metamidophos can be applied at a dose of 1.0 to 1.4 liters/hectare to prevent larval penetration.

## Crown rot (Fusarium sp)

This disease affects seedlings. It causes rot in the stem, causing the plants to wilt and die.

Anthracnose (Colletotrichum gloeosporioides, Penzig).

It primarily attacks young tissues from leaves, branches, flowers, and fruits. On leaves, it causes black spots at the tips and deformations at the edges. On stems, it causes cracks in the bark and black lesions. On flowers, it causes spots on the carpels, causing brown or black lesions. Fruits can be damaged from very early stages of development, with black spots that cover them quickly and cause them to fall. This reduces production and the quality of the fruits.

Control: Carry out pruning, sanitary pruning, weed control, and good fertilization. You can also apply chlorotalonil at a dose of 11 to 1.7 kg/hectare, Mancob 200 at a dose of 1.4 to 2.0 kg/hectare, or oxichloride of copper plus propineb at a dose of 0.8 to 1.5 kg per hectare. These applications can be made every 7 to 10 days.

## POST-HARVEST

The selection is based on: sanitation (without pest damage), cleanliness (without soil or vegetable residues), size, color of the pulp, and maturity. The harvested fruits must be handled carefully. They should be wrapped in newspaper or dry vegetable leaves, placed in plastic bags or baskets, taking care not to damage them during transport, as they are very delicate fruits. Anona is suitable for consumption 2 to 3 days after harvest.

## COMMERCIALIZATION

The fruit has good acceptance and a good price, due to its excellent taste, pulp color, and size. It is sold directly from the production site or taken to the local market. When harvesting begins, the price ranges from 5 colones ($0.57) to 10 colones ($1.14) per unit, depending on the quality and size of the fruit and location.

## GLOSSARY

Alternation: A term used to indicate that the yield of a fruit tree varies from year to year.

Flow: The volume of water per unit of time.

Carpels: Round, smooth protrusions that characterize the skin of the anona fruit.

Cultivation: The set of genotypes cultivated, which is distinguished by morphological, physiological, cytological, biochemical, and other characteristics, belonging to the same group, and which, when multiplied sexually or asexually, maintains its distinctive characteristics.

Sprout: A vigorous shoot that originates from the trunk or main branch of a fruit tree.

Clone: An individual or group of individuals that have been obtained or formed through asexual reproduction.

Disease: An abnormal physiological activity caused by the presence of pathogenic organisms.

Scab: A treatment for seeds with physical dormancy. It consists of slightly damaging the outer layer to allow water penetration for the embryo's imbibition.

Evapotranspiration (ET). The rate of water loss due to the transpiration of vegetation, as well as evaporation from the soil and the moist surface of vegetation (mm/day).

Phenology: The study of the appearance of periodic phenomena in the natural life cycle of organisms.

Infiltration trenches: A physical soil and water conservation structure. Constructed in a trapezoidal shape, used to conserve soil and filter water, with dimensions of 0.3 m depth, 1.5 m to 2.0 m width, and 0.60 m length.

Frequency of days it takes for the irrigation layer to be consumed: irrigation

Germination: The growth of the embryo that emerges from the seed and develops into a seedling.

Grafting: The act of transferring a portion of a stem that carries one or more buds to another plant, aligning the cambium layers so that the graft and rootstock can unite.

Incompatibility: An abnormal behavior that occurs in plants propagated asexually, for example, the failure of the graft to adhere to the rootstock.

Dormancy: A physical and physiological state in which a seed that is predisposed to germination does not germinate, even under favorable environmental conditions.

Irrigation layer: The amount of water expressed as a height, that has been consumed by the crop and that needs to be replenished in the next irrigation.

Weed: A plant that grows where it is not desired, often the wild relative of a cultivated plant.

Micro-sprinkler: The application of water to the soil or plant in a fine, atomized form and in small quantities.

Orthotropic: Vertical growth of a branch, a tendency to grow vertically.

Rootstock or graft: The plant on which a graft is established.

Pruning: The removal of branches from trees or plants to promote more vigorous fruiting.

Pulp: The fleshy part of the fruit that surrounds the seed.

Seedling: A young plant obtained from seed.

Strictly associated with the group of insects and mites: Pests; disease.

Stubble: Crop residues used as soil cover, with the aim of improving soil texture, fertility, and weed control through drip irrigation: This is a system that delivers filtered water and, sometimes, fertilizer directly to the soil, either on or within it. Irrigation

Seed: The reproductive part of a plant.

Taxonomy: The discipline of classification, especially of organisms.

Individual terrace: A physical soil and water conservation structure, constructed in a circular shape with a diameter of 1.0 to 1.5 m, depending on the size of the plant, with a cut at the top and a fill at the bottom; with a slope of 1.0% inwards and with a drainage channel.

Stump: The remaining part of a tree, which often sprouts. After pruning.

Consumptive use: The amount of water expressed as a layer (cm or mm) that a crop consumes over a given period (days, weeks, months, or cycle).

Whip: The portion of a plant consisting of one or more buds, prepared for grafting.

Variety: Refers to the set of cultivated individuals, which are distinguished by some characteristic (morphological, physiological, chemical, or other).

Bud: A rudimentary bud, shoot, or flower that often forms in the axil of a leaf.
