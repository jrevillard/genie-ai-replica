The Gambia National NCD Strategy 2022-2027 — Key Protocols and Health System Context

THE GAMBIA NCD BURDEN AND HEALTH SYSTEM

1. NCD BURDEN IN THE GAMBIA
- NCDs account for an estimated 34% of all deaths in The Gambia
- Leading NCD causes of death: cardiovascular diseases (13%), cancers (5%), chronic respiratory diseases (3%), diabetes (2%)
- 79% of rural NCD patients are estimated to be UNDIAGNOSED
- Hypertension prevalence: estimated 30-40% in adults over 25
- Diabetes prevalence: estimated 3-5% (rising rapidly with urbanization)
- Tobacco use: approximately 15% of adult males, 1% of females
- Physical inactivity increasing with urbanization
- Diet transitioning from traditional (protective) to processed (harmful)
- Obesity rising, especially in urban women

2. THE GAMBIAN HEALTH SYSTEM STRUCTURE

TIER 1 — Community Level:
- Community Health Workers (CHWs/VHWs): village-based, trained for basic health education, screening, referral
- Village Health Services: basic first aid, health education, growth monitoring
- Community midwives: maternal and newborn care
- Coverage: approximately 1 CHW per 500-1000 population
- CHW capabilities for NCDs: BP measurement, blood glucose (glucometer), health education, medication adherence support, referral
- Training: MRC Fajara and regional health directorates provide NCD training

TIER 2 — Health Posts and Minor Health Centres:
- Approximately 80+ health posts across the country
- Staffed by Community Health Nurses (CHN)
- Services: outpatient care, basic diagnostics, essential medicines
- NCD services: BP screening, glucose testing, essential NCD medicines (Metformin, Amlodipine, HCTZ), health education
- Limitations: no lab services, limited drug supply, one nurse may cover multiple posts

TIER 3 — Health Centres and District Hospitals:
- Major health centres: Brikama, Farafenni, Bansang, Basse
- Services: outpatient and inpatient, laboratory, X-ray, minor surgery
- NCD services: diagnosis, treatment initiation, HbA1c testing, cervical cancer screening (VIA), referral
- Staffed by Clinical Officers, nurses, midwives, lab technicians

TIER 4 — Regional and Teaching Hospitals:
- Edward Francis Small Teaching Hospital (EFSTH) — Banjul: national referral hospital
- Services: specialist care, surgery, ICU (limited), dialysis, chemotherapy
- NCD services: cardiology, endocrinology, oncology, nephrology, ophthalmology
- Also: Sheikh Zayed Regional Eye Centre (diabetic retinopathy screening)
- Emergency services: 199 ambulance line

3. ESSENTIAL MEDICINES FOR NCDs IN THE GAMBIA

Diabetes:
- Metformin 500mg tablets (first-line, widely available)
- Gliclazide 80mg tablets (second-line, available at health centres)
- Insulin (soluble and NPH): available at some health centres and hospitals, requires cold chain
- Glucose testing strips and glucometers

Hypertension:
- Amlodipine 5mg and 10mg (CCB — first-line for Gambian/African populations)
- Hydrochlorothiazide 25mg (thiazide diuretic)
- Enalapril 5mg and 10mg (ACE inhibitor)
- Losartan 50mg (ARB — available at some facilities)
- Methyldopa 250mg (for hypertension in pregnancy)

Cardiovascular:
- Aspirin 75mg and 100mg (prevention and treatment)
- Simvastatin 20mg (cholesterol — available at hospitals)
- Atorvastatin 10mg (some facilities)

Respiratory:
- Salbutamol inhaler 100mcg (widely available)
- Beclometasone inhaler 100mcg (available at health centres/hospitals)
- Theophylline 200mg tablets
- Prednisolone 5mg tablets (for exacerbations)
- Amoxicillin 500mg (for respiratory infections)

Pain:
- Paracetamol 500mg
- Ibuprofen 400mg
- Codeine 30mg (limited availability)
- Morphine oral solution (very limited, hospital-level)

4. NATIONAL HEALTH INSURANCE SCHEME (NHIS)
- Gambia is developing a national health insurance scheme
- Aims to provide universal health coverage for essential health services
- NCD medicines included in essential medicines list — should be available free or subsidized
- Dialysis costs: approximately $10,000 per patient per year — prevention is cost-effective
- Current out-of-pocket spending is a major barrier to NCD care
- Transport costs: rural patients may spend 200-500 dalasi just to reach a health facility
- AMINA (this AI) aims to be the "5-cent digital triage" that prevents unnecessary travel

5. KEY HEALTH FACILITIES AND EMERGENCY CONTACTS

Emergency:
- Ambulance: 199 (national)
- Fire: 118
- Police: 117

Major facilities by region:
- Greater Banjul Area: EFSTH, MRC at Fajara, Serrekunda General Hospital
- West Coast Region: Brikama District Hospital, Gunjur Health Centre
- North Bank Region: Farafenni District Hospital, Kerewan Health Centre
- Lower River Region: Soma Health Centre, Mansakonko Health Centre
- Central River Region: Bansang Hospital, Janjanbureh Health Centre
- Upper River Region: Basse District Hospital

Specialized services:
- EFSTH: only hospital with dialysis, basic chemotherapy, specialist cardiology
- Sheikh Zayed: eye care and diabetic retinopathy screening
- MRC Fajara: clinical research, some specialized care
- SOS Children's Village Clinic: pediatric services

6. COMMUNITY HEALTH STRUCTURES

Village Development Committees (VDC):
- Community governance structure
- Can support health initiatives, organize screening days
- Alkalo (village head) chairs the VDC

Traditional Birth Attendants (TBAs):
- Community trust, especially in rural areas
- Can be trained in NCD risk factor screening during antenatal visits
- Bridge between traditional and modern health care

Women's Groups (Kafos):
- Strong social networks for health education
- Can organize group health activities (walking groups, cooking classes)
- Influential in changing household diet practices

Youth Health Scouts:
- Young community members (12-24) trained in basic health monitoring
- Can check BP on elders, remind medication, report emergencies
- Bridge between healthcare system and hard-to-reach populations

7. CULTURAL CONSIDERATIONS FOR NCD MANAGEMENT

Traditional Medicine:
- Approximately 80% of Gambians use traditional medicine alongside modern medicine
- Common traditional remedies: kinkeliba tea (anti-malarial/general health), bitter leaf, neem, moringa
- Marabout (traditional healer) consultations common, especially before/instead of hospital
- WHO PEN approach: do not reject traditional medicine, integrate and bridge
- Key message: "traditional and modern medicine can work together — tell your doctor what herbs you use"

Religious Context:
- Approximately 95% Muslim, with Christian and traditional belief minorities
- Ramadan fasting: major consideration for diabetes and medication management
- Prayer times: can be used to align medication schedules
- Imam support: can legitimize health-seeking behavior in conservative communities
- Friday (Jummah): community gathering opportunity for health education
- Religious leaders are trusted — involve in health messaging

Gender Dynamics:
- Women often need husband's or elder's permission for health facility visits
- Alkalo (village head) can grant permission and legitimize health-seeking
- CHW home visits provide privacy for women
- Women's kafos provide safe space for health discussions
- Male engagement: important for changing household diet and supporting women's health

Food Culture:
- Communal eating from shared bowl (calabash): portion control is challenging
- Benachin (jollof rice): national dish, eaten almost daily — reducing portion is key intervention
- Domoda (groundnut stew): high calorie but nutritious — reduce groundnut paste, add vegetables
- Supakanja (okra stew): healthy — encourage more frequently
- Chere (millet porridge): healthier than white rice — encourage as breakfast and alternative
- Attaya (green tea ceremony): social ritual, 3 rounds — reduce sugar, don't add condensed milk
- Maggi cubes: ubiquitous seasoning — salt reduction is the single most impactful dietary change

8. SEASONAL HEALTH CONSIDERATIONS

Dry Season (November - May):
- Harmattan (December-February): cold nights, dust — worsens asthma/COPD
- Fresh fruit abundant: oranges, grapefruit, mango (late season)
- Market garden vegetables available
- Easier transport (roads not flooded)

Rainy Season (June - October):
- Malaria peak: co-morbidity with NCDs complicates management
- Roads flooded: access to health facilities difficult
- Fresh fruit peak: mango, watermelon, papaya
- Farming work: high physical activity (counts as exercise)
- Hunger season (July-August): food insecurity in some areas — medication on empty stomach problematic

Ramadan (moves through calendar):
- Fasting diabetics: high risk of hypoglycemia, especially with sulfonylureas
- Fasting hypertensives: medication timing adjustment needed
- Dehydration risk in hot-season Ramadan
- IDF-DAR (Diabetes and Ramadan) guidelines: risk-stratify patients BEFORE Ramadan
- Post-Ramadan period: often weight gain due to feast foods — monitor

Lumo (Market Days):
- Monday, Wednesday, Saturday: weekly markets in different towns
- Opportunity to buy fresh vegetables and fruits
- Opportunity for health education at busy gathering point
- Health workers can set up screening stations at lumo

9. MONITORING AND EVALUATION INDICATORS
- Proportion of adults screened for hypertension and diabetes
- Proportion of diagnosed patients on treatment
- Proportion of treated patients achieving target BP/glucose
- Emergency department visits for NCD complications
- NCD-related mortality rate
- Essential medicine availability at health facilities
- Patient satisfaction with NCD services
- CHW NCD training coverage
