## Origin

The Celia rice variety, line 2310, was introduced to El Salvador's Rice Improvement Program at CENTA in 1991, originating from the International Tropical Rice Institute (IRRI) as part of the Latin America and the Caribbean International Rice Observation Nursery (VIOAL), with pedigree CT 9159-13-2-4-3-MS (assigned line number 2310), derived from the cross P3059F4-11M-2P-2P-1-B / P3084F4-56-2-2 //P 5746-18, with origin number CIAT 900510. It was evaluated in a field trial of uniform rice lines in 1992 (Castaneda and others, 1992) in the municipalities of Sa Andrés and Santa Cruz Porrillo, yielding the results presented in Table 1.

## Social

Rice is of great importance for food security; it accounts for 5.26% of the population's dietary structure nationally, with consumption in rural areas reaching 5.31% compared to 4.72% in urban areas. Despite this, per capita consumption (9.0 kg) is still relatively low (50%) compared to the recommended 18.0 kg per capita by the Central American and Pan-American Institute of Nutrition (INCAP).

In the context of food security, it is essential to ensure sufficient caloric intake to meet the nutritional needs of the growing population. This poses a continuous challenge to achieving this goal.

## Economic

Rice production in El Salvador during the 96/2002 period did not meet domestic consumption demand, primarily due to a reduction in planted area, caused by adverse climatic phenomena such as El Niño, which led to delayed rainfall, forcing a shift in production to other crops. Furthermore, the prospect of low international prices reduced the profitability of rice cultivation (MAG, 1999), resulting in a production and consumption gap of 45,352 tons (DGEA). This deficit is being compensated for by imports to cover internal demand, with a value of $8.25 million, which has a negative impact on employment and income for agricultural workers.

## Environmental

The development of high-yielding technologies with resistance to the main pests and diseases will result in the use of a smaller area of land to meet the production deficit, and also in a reduced use of pesticides, contributing to the preservation of the environment.

## Crop Management

## Planting Time

Rain-fed rice is sown at the beginning of the rainy season (mid-May and early June).

When sowing under irrigation, it is recommended to do so in December and January.

## Sowing Methods

Direct sowing involves depositing dry seeds in rows spaced 0.20 to 0.30 meters apart. Well-prepared soil is required to allow for the uniform and proper placement of the seed at a depth of 0.02 meters, with a slight covering to ensure uniform and rapid emergence of the seedling. A recommended rate of 98 kg per hectare is used in this system.

Broadcast sowing involves evenly spreading dry seeds manually or mechanically over the prepared soil, which is then covered with branches or light rakes. For this method, a seed rate of 130 kg per hectare can be used.

Transplanting involves sowing on prepared, muddy, and level soil. This is an indirect sowing method, where plants initially grow in seedbeds or nurseries before being transplanted to the main field. The average transplanting time is 25 days, and 45 kilograms of dry seed per hectare are required.

The spacing at the time of transplanting is 0.25 meters in the row.

## Fertilization

In modern agriculture, proper fertilizer management increases crop productivity. In rice soils, the main limitation is nitrogen deficiency. At the farm level, deficiencies of phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium may occur, and in some areas, zinc. For rain-fed rice, nitrogen management is particularly important, taking into account the variety, efficient weed control, and the impact of diseases.

It is undeniable that to provide the rice plant with all the essential elements in the necessary amounts, it is necessary to apply fertilizer. However, in general, the following fertilization plan can be applied to rice crops:

First fertilization: 286 kilograms per hectare of a complete fertilizer (15-15-15, preferably), at the time of sowing.

Second fertilization: 30 days after sowing, 286 kilograms per hectare of ammonium sulfate.

Third fertilization: 30 days after the second fertilization (60 days after sowing), 200 kilograms of urea, plus 68 kilograms of 0-0-60 per hectare.

## Diseases

Rice blast. Disease caused by (Pyricularia grisea), which causes the most severe damage to rice crops, both at the leaf and panicle level. Control requires an integrated approach. It starts with the use of varieties with resistance or tolerance, such as CENTA A-7, proper fertilizer management, especially nitrogen, is of vital importance, as high levels of this element condition the crop to severe attacks, which, combined with high relative humidity, dew, and high densities of weeds, provide ideal conditions for the appearance and/or development of the disease. For its control, both preventive and curative, conventional products such as Benlate and Cycosin, Sil 30EC, Amistar, Carbendazim, etc. are used.

Brown spot, Coffee spot. The pathogen causing it is (Cochliobolus miyabeanus), and its control also starts with the use of resistant varieties. In addition, proper fertilization, crop rotation (when feasible), leveling of plots, and good water management, are cultural practices that will improve the health of rice fields. Chemical control can be achieved using agrochemicals such as Dithane, Manzate, Cycosin.

Leaf scald. Its causal agent (Rhynchosporium oryzae) is favored by high relative humidity, cool temperatures, and prolonged, but not heavy, rainfall during the peak flowering period, acidic soils, and excessive nitrogen fertilization. One of the recommended cultural practices to reduce the incidence of this disease is to avoid excessive applications of nitrogen. The use of resistant and tolerant varieties is always a good alternative.

Sheath blight. Its causal agent (Rhizoctonia solani) requires appropriate fertilization, avoiding high planting densities and maintaining good weed control. Chemical control can be effective.

False hooch. Caused by (Ustilago virens), it is a disease that can be epidemic. It is favored by prolonged drizzle, periods of high relative humidity, followed by hot days during the flowering period, poor panicle development, and the presence of sucking insects.

## Weeds

Rice crops should be kept free of weeds during the first 45 days of the cycle. Research has shown that weeds can reduce rice yield by 50% or more at this stage. Given that manual control is quite difficult, it is recommended to use chemical products. Some herbicides that can be applied in mixtures and at different times, depending on the type of weed, its stage of development, and the rice crop, are recommended.

Depending on the application time, herbicides can be pre-emergence and post-emergence.

Pre-emergence herbicides are generally systemic and are applied to weeds that are actively growing, preferably when they reach a maximum size of 20-30 cm. These are mainly used for this purpose, with glyphosate (Round-up, Ranger, etc.) being a broad-spectrum chemical.

As for pre-emergence applications, Pendimethalin (2.50 - 3.50 l/ha) is frequently used. For this type of application, it is necessary that the soil is moist.

For post-emergence applications, products such as propanil (5.5 - 10 l/ha), bentazon + MCPA (1.5 - 2.0 l/ha), Nominec 4 0 SC (145cc/ha), and fenoxaprop-ethyl (1.4 - 1.7 l/ha) are regularly used, the latter being specific for difficult-to-control grasses such as Ixophorus, Cynodon, Digitaria, and Echinochloa, among others.

It should be considered that for the selection of the herbicide, its mixtures, dosages, and application times, a good field diagnosis should be carried out, which allows us to verify the condition of the soil, the type and development of weeds, as well as the rice crop.

## Harvesting

The optimal time for harvesting rice is when the panicle reaches its physiological maturity, that is, when 95% of the grains in the head have turned straw-colored, and the rest are yellowish; and the moisture content is 20 to 27%. The time of harvest is crucial, as it is when the grain has not fully matured, the yield is reduced, and the threshing results in a lot of moist and broken grain; while if it is harvested too early, the risk of grain loss during harvesting is increased, reducing the yield and the threshing may result in a lot of broken grain.

In the country, harvesting is carried out manually or mechanically, depending on the technological development and the production system of the different productive areas.
