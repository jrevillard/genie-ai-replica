## INTRODUCTION

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a crop that provides more than half of the daily food for a third of the world's population. It is the only major cereal crop primarily used for human consumption. Its nutritional benefits are numerous: it is rich in vitamins and essential minerals, covering a significant portion of human nutritional needs. It has low fat content (1%), is cholesterol-free, and has a very low sodium content. Due to these characteristics, rice is an important resource for rural populations and food security in many low-income countries. It is grown in over 100 countries, with a cultivated area of 158 million hectares. For the 2015/2016 period, the world rice production was estimated at 471.09 million tons, with a 1.3% increase in rice consumption as food.

Despite the fact that Latin America contributes 3.9% of the world's production and 5.5% of the cultivated area, rice plays a strategic role in the region, both economically and socially. Therefore, any biotic or abiotic factor that affects the traditional yield of the crop is a potential threat to food security in the region.

In recent decades, rice production has increased as a result of improved cultivation practices and the introduction of new varieties. However, these advances have not been able to prevent pest damage to yields. In addition to this, there are environmental costs, public health concerns, and the loss of competitiveness associated with the overuse of agrochemicals.

Rice is infested by various pests, but in the last 15 years, mites have become a major problem due to the appearance of Steneotarsonemus spinki (Smiley) (Acari: Tarsonemidae) in Cuba and other countries in the region.

There is a significant body of knowledge in the region regarding the management of rice mites. Therefore, it is necessary to make this information available to producers, as a starting point for developing specific integrated pest management strategies for each country. This will undoubtedly be a significant contribution to solving this problem.

Given the importance of this pest for the food security of the Central American population, the International Regional Committee for Agricultural Sanity (CIRSA) – the governing body of the International Regional Organization for Agricultural Sanity (OIRSA) – included the development of a Manual on Integrated Pest Management for this pest in its Biennial Work Program (2014-2015). This manual will be a valuable tool for technicians in extension services, phytosanitary surveillance, and producers in member countries.

## BACKGROUND

The original description of the rice mite, S. spinki, was provided by Robert L. Smiley (1967) based on samples collected in 1960 by Dr. Will Spink, for whom the species was named. Smiley's article (1967) states that S. spinki was collected on Togasodes orizicolus (Muir) (Hemiptera: Delphacidae) (formerly Sogata orizicola Muir) in Baton Rouge, Louisiana, United States. However, a review of Dr. Spink's notes revealed that the S. spinki specimens were actually collected on the eggs of the leafhopper, not on adults.

This mite was first reported in the 1960s in China, south of the Yangtze River, where it caused losses of up to 30% in rice cultivation, and in more severe cases, reduced yields by 70-90% in the second harvest.

By 1993, S. spinki was considered a serious pest of rice in China, Taiwan, and the Philippines. In 1985, it was considered a pest throughout tropical Asia. In this region, it was also reported in Thailand and Korea, as well as in Sri Lanka. Prior to this, in 1976, an epidemic of sterility in rice caused extensive damage in the Tainán district (Taiwan). Although the cause was unknown, many mites were found in the rice panicles, and at that time, Steneotarsonemus madecasus Gutiérrez was identified. However, a subsequent evaluation corrected the classification of S. madecasus to S. spinki, which was identified as the cause of losses between 20 and 60%.

In India, in 1977, a new rice pest was reported, affecting both the glumes and the stigmas, as well as the ovaries of the flower. In 1984, the mite was reported in the rice panicles of plants grown in greenhouses in Japan. In the Tainán region, south of Taiwan, losses of 60% were reported. Researchers concluded that the Tainán #5 variety was particularly susceptible to damage by S. spinki, and in response, the cultivated area of this variety was reduced from 400,000 hectares in 1975 to 80,000 in 1981. Another report on the 1976 outbreak in southern Taiwan reported that approximately 20-60% of the harvested grains were empty, resulting in a loss equivalent to 20,000 tons. The damage was particularly severe in the second rice harvest.

In the province of Guangdong, in southern China, S. spinki was attributed to a reduction in yields of 5-20%. In India, it was determined that Japanese rice varieties were more susceptible to S. spinki than Indian varieties, with a yield loss of 20%. A detailed study in India revealed that S. spinki represented 80% of the total mite population in rice cultivation, and that sterility can be correlated with the size of the S. spinki population present in the crop. In Vietnam, a 15% loss of grains due to the presence of S. spinki was reported.

The first report of this pest in the Americas occurred in the Caribbean, when S. spinki was confirmed in Cuba in 1997, with a reduction in yields of 15-20%. In the following years, the pest spread rapidly throughout the Caribbean. In 1999, it was confirmed in the Dominican Republic, with estimated yield reductions of over 40%. In the same year, it was reported in Haiti, resulting in a 60% loss of yields.

In Central America, S. spinki was first reported in Panama in 2003. The reduction in rice production was approximately 40%. Experimental studies determined that losses in irrigated areas fluctuated between 17 and 23.5%, while in rain-fed areas, they ranged from 34.7 to 74.2%. In the first year of S. spinki infestation in Costa Rica, losses of up to 45% and 100% were recorded in some areas. When the recommended phytosanitary measures were adopted, only 3 t/ha of rice were harvested due to the high infestation.

By 2005, it was reported in Colombia, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Venezuela, the latter country with no significant yield losses. In North America, the pest was reported in 2006 in Palizada, Campeche, Mexico. In the United States, it appeared in 2007, where S. spinki was identified on plants in greenhouses and in research plots in Texas.

This species has been considered the most important and destructive pest of rice cultivation worldwide. This mite also carries the spores of the fungus Sarocladium oryzae (Sawada) W. Gams and D. Hawks, which causes the disease known as 'Rice panicle sterility syndrome' in Asia.

## 1. ECONOMIC DAMAGE

In general, S. spinki causes damage by feeding on the panicles, which become discolored or necrotic, resulting in the grains not being fully filled, and the panicles remaining erect. The mite can be found in the pistil and stigmas of the rice flower, and can only cause damage to the plant when it is in high numbers.

Studies have shown that the deterioration of grain quality, poor filling, and grain staining were caused by the infestation of S. spinki, and that the presence of the tarsonemid increased the percentage of damaged grains.

The damage and effects associated with S. spinki infestations in rice crops in Panama in 2003, when the pest appeared, resulted in a decrease in production. The plants showed normal development during the vegetative period, but when they reached the flowering stage, the visual symptoms appeared, consisting of empty and blackened spikelets, 'pea-shaped' grains, sterile grains, and panicle abortion (Fig. 1).

(C) Erect panicle and discolored grains. (D and E) Erect panicles with 'pea-shaped' grains (Nicaragua). Photos: Evelyn Quirós, Héctor Rodríguez.

The damage and effects associated with S. spinki infestations include sterility, partial sterility of the panicle, and deformation of the rice grain. It is difficult to accurately characterize and isolate the damage caused by S. spinki, as it commonly occurs alongside other pathogens of the crop.

From samples of grains and spikelets severely affected by the presence of the mite-fungus complex in various rice-growing areas of Panama, the bacterium Burkholderia glumae Kurita and Tabei was isolated, and this bacterium was considered to be part of the complex. In 2007, from samples with damage attributed to S. spinki, the bacteria B. glumae and Burkholderia gladioli Severini were isolated, concluding that these bacteria are associated with the rice panicle blight disease. It has been mentioned that insects that suck and scrape, and S. spinki, help to spread the bacteria and increase the incidence when the presence of both is observed in the complex.

The damage associated with S. spinki in rice plants can be described as 'Sterile grain syndrome', as described by Chen et al. (1979), which stated that the density of mites and the percentage of empty grains are strongly correlated with yield reduction.

## 3. MORPHOLOGICAL DESCRIPTION

The female of S. spinki exhibits an elongated, ornamented idiosoma, and a tegument finely punctate, with a length of 222-350 μm and a width of 84-109 μm. Sub-quadrangular gnathosoma, with the dorsal gnathosomale setae longer than the ventral ones. Short, strongly curved cheliceral setae. Anteriorly truncated prodorsal shield. Robust, slightly pilose v1 seta (16-20 μm); long and filiform sc2 seta (48-58 μm). Base of the sc2 seta posterior to the sc1 seta. Ovoid, slightly pilose, sc1 seta (18-21 μm) (pseudo-stigmatic organ), with a rounded base. C1 seta (9-12 μm) shorter than the c2 seta (17-24 μm), filiform. The bases of the

c1 and c2 setae are aligned transversely. D seta (9-10 μm) setiform. E seta (8-10 μm) as long as the f seta (8-10 μm). Short I apodeme, attached to the II apodeme; conspicuous and curved (ap2) II apodeme, attached to the II apodeme. 1a seta (15-17 μm) twice as long as the 2a seta (7-9 μm), 3a seta (39-44 μm) long and filiform, six times as long as the 3b seta (6-8 μm). I leg (47-74 μm) and II leg (44-77 μm) of equal length; III leg (99-116 μm) longer than I, II and IV legs (50-63 μm). The I and II legs are of similar length, while the III leg is longer than the remaining legs. The IV leg is short and cylindrical, with a terminal seta (tg´´) three times as long as the subterminal seta (v´) (Fig. 2A).

The male has a smooth idiosoma, with a tegument finely punctate, with a length of 217-244 μm and a width of 109-122 μm. Sub-quadrangular gnathosoma, with the dorsal gnathosomale setae pilose at the base and the ventral ones smooth. Moderately short cheliceral seta. Dorsal shield without ornamentation. Anteriorly triangular, sclerotized prodorsal shield. Robust, slightly pilose, vertical v1 seta (24-35 μm), larger than the v2 seta (17-31 μm). Robust, slightly pilose, scapular seta, sc1 (33-49 μm) approximately the same length as the Sc2 (30-45 μm). The bases of the sc2 setae are longitudinally aligned with the sc1 seta. The I and II setae are of similar length, while the III leg is longer than the remaining legs. The IV leg has a long, filiform seta on the trochanter, of the same length as the setae on the femur (vG´) and the tibia (vTi´´). These setae are robust, knife-shaped, and slightly pilose (Fig. 2B).

In several publications, this mite is referred to as: rice mite, rice planthopper, rice weevil, and rice grain mite. These are crystalline mites, not visible to the naked eye; their observation in the field is only possible with a magnifying glass of 10x magnification. The eggs and larvae are even smaller and of a similar color to the adults. They live and develop inside the leaves.

As a summary of the distinctive taxonomic characteristics, which are easier to observe, it can be said that the females of S. spinki have a body that is longer than it is wide, with the first pair of apodemes in the shape of a "Y", and the second pair are longer and stronger, and they have pseudo-stigmatic organs that are ovoid with small projections on the surface (Fig. 3 A, B, E). The males have a body that is wider in the hysterosoma region, they have a pair of knife-shaped or sword-shaped setae on the femur and the gena of the fourth leg, and a solenidium on the tibia of the third leg (Fig. 3 C, D, F).

## 4. FIELD IDENTIFICATION

Since the rice mite is such a small organism, it is only possible to recognize it in the field using a magnifying glass. This should be a 10x magnification or higher, preferably with a built-in light. It is necessary to cut or tear off a stem, and then cut the sheaths at the base, from the lower to the upper leaves, using a knife or scissors (Fig. 3). Subsequently, each sheath is separated from the stem, opened in two parts, and observed on the inside of the same (Fig. 4). It is necessary to count the number of adults present in three points of the sheath (base, center, apex). To achieve adequate visibility, it is necessary to position yourself correctly to ensure that the sunlight falls on the surface being observed. It is important to know that you are observing and adapt to the dimensions of the organism you are going to find.

## 5. BIOLOGY AND ECOLOGY

Biology. In its development, S. spinki goes through the stages of egg, larva (active and inactive), and adult. The eggs are white and translucent, oval in shape, and can be found isolated, in small groups, or forming large compact masses of eggs. From them emerges the larva, of a similar color and elongated body, with only three pairs of legs (Fig. 5). The larval stage goes through an active period, during which the larva feeds, and another inactive or quiescent period. They are oval-shaped with sharp ends, white and translucent, and larger than the active larva. The inactive larva, which gives rise to a female, can be carried by the males, as is common in the behavior of other Tarsonemidae species.

The females are elongated, and their pairs of anterior and posterior legs are widely separated. The males are smaller, with a different body outline. The males walk quickly and are very active, searching for an inactive larva, which they lift with the last pair of legs, fix it with the genital male papilla at the posterior end of their body, and carry it as they walk, until the emergence of the adult female (Fig. 5). Copulation takes place, generally, almost immediately after the adult female emerges. Once copulated, they separate. Both sexes are depressed dorsally, a configuration that is very favorable for their activity in the confined spaces between the sheaths and stems of the plants.

Studies of the development cycle have shown that temperature is one of the factors that determines its duration. Table 1 shows the duration of the cycle at different temperatures, according to several authors. As can be seen, the duration of the S. spinki cycle decreases as the temperature increases, in the range of 20 to 34 °C.

The embryonic period is the most variable, which suggests that it is the stage that is most sensitive to changes in environmental conditions, and its duration is affected by temperature changes. This stage had a maximum mortality of 40% at 15 °C. An increase in mortality was observed from 16.7 to 26.7% in the range of 20 to 34 °C, which coincides with what was stated by various authors, who found an increase in mortality in the range of 25 to 32 °C as the temperature increased. The remaining stages showed a higher stability, with mortality rates below 10%.

The minimum development threshold was 16.10 °C for the embryonic period, 15.91 °C for the larval, and 16.06 °C for the complete cycle, which did not differ from what was obtained experimentally, since at 15 °C the eggs had a high mortality and the larvae did not survive. The Sum of Effective Temperatures (STE) calculated from 16 °C as the minimum development threshold for the Cuban conditions, was 62.03 ± 10.03 degrees/day, for a number of annual generations of 48 to 55.

In another study of biology developed with this species in Colombia, a development duration of 3.5 days for the Perla de Cuba variety at 27 °C was obtained. These parameters show a net reproduction rate (Ro) of 52.72, a natural growth rate (rm) of 1.36%, a finite multiplication rate (λ) of 3.88, a generation time (T) of 4.59 days, and a doubling time (Td) of 0.51 days.

The biological and behavioral parameters described are consistent with the characteristics of invasive exotic species, and they support the explosive manifestation of the damage associated with the detection of rice mites in Cuba and other countries in the region.

The females of S. spinki can produce 50 to 70 eggs. They can also reproduce parthenogenetically, producing only males from virgin female eggs. They are capable of producing 48-55 generations per year, due to ideal climatic conditions.

However, it was shown that S. spinki prefers O. sativa and O. latifolia (Desv.), according to its survival and development rates in each of them.

Population dynamics. *Steneotarsonemus spinki* can be dispersed by wind, water, insects, plants, and contaminated agricultural implements. The rice weevil presents an aggregated distribution and does not show preferences for particular areas of the field. It is most commonly found on the grains of the 1st to 4th leaves, and is more abundant at the base and center of the grains than in other areas. Adults represent 30% of the total population, with a similar proportion of eggs and immature stages.

In an analysis based on the proportion of *S. spinki* per grain, compared to the total population, it was determined that the weevil generally prefers the 2nd and 3rd grains, with the highest population levels occurring after flowering. While the predatory mites showed a similar behavior, albeit with less regularity.

The population fluctuation of the rice weevil shows a clear relationship with abiotic factors and the crop phenology. In relation to rice plant growth, weevil populations appear in the "tillering" phase and reach a peak population in the beginning of grain formation, before declining during maturity. This preference for the beginning of grain formation is due to the high accumulation of nutrients in the grain, which are then transferred to the grain, causing the weevils to move.

Crop phenology has a significant influence on the pest population. Within the phenological phases, the period of grain formation and harvest is where the highest populations are found. In the phases of primordium and early growth, the population levels are significantly lower. The maximum populations are generally found after 90 days of crop growth.

Studies on population dynamics in Cuba indicated that the highest populations of this weevil are reached during the flowering and grain formation phase. This was confirmed through the results obtained with the linear model applied to measure the influence of climatic factors (average temperature and relative humidity and accumulated rainfall), predatory mites, and crop phenology on the average population of *S. spinki*, where only a highly significant influence of crop phenology was found.

As is known, climate determines the timing of rice planting, and consequently, the timing of each phenological phase. According to studies conducted with the "Perla de Cuba" variety, both the crop phenology, temperature, and the amount of predators are factors that determine 42.8% of the fluctuations in the population density of *S. spinki*. Without forgetting that rainfall also influences, albeit in a secondary way, the population variations. The warm and humid climate favored the increase of the pest in Cuba.

In the climatic conditions of Cuba, populations are generally not observed until 35 days after the germination of the crop. The lowest densities were found between December and May.

During these months, minor damage occurred, while from August onwards, the levels of infestation and damage were higher.

In Cuba, the highest populations were observed in the spring-summer plantings, where the first populations of the weevil were detected 45-60 days after the germination of the crop. Coincidentally, the greatest yield losses occurred in the plantings carried out in the months of April, May, and June, while the lowest populations were recorded in the plantings carried out between the months of December and March, a period with temperatures below 24 °C.

In Panama, in a study conducted with the cultivars of the Institute of Agricultural Research of Panama (IDIAP) 38 and IDIAP L7 in 32 separate plantings, where the effect of climatic factors on the fluctuation of the *S. spinki* population was analyzed, the relationship between the *S. spinki* population and relative humidity and -to a lesser extent - temperature was evident. In the characterization of the climate in Panama, it is important to note that the average temperature of the country is around 29 °C, with little variation between the rainy and dry seasons. These conditions favor the reduction of the development cycle of *S. spinki*, with a high multiplication rate of the population throughout the year, resulting in more generations per year, which is not the case in other places or countries with lower temperatures. The marked influence of relative humidity on the *S. spinki* populations is based on the fact that this factor oscillated between 45% and 72% during the dry season, values that are considered detrimental to these weevils.

On the other hand, the relative humidity values during the rainy season oscillated between 65 and 90%, which are considered favorable for the development of *S. spinki*.

To determine the influence of crop phenology on the population fluctuation of *S. spinki* in each cultivar, the average populations recorded in three phenological stages of the cultivars were considered. In the IDIAP L7 cultivar, the populations increased in each stage towards the maturation of the plant, with statistically significant differences. While in the IDIAP 38 cultivar, no differences were observed between the phenological phases.

When comparing the *S. spinki* populations between the two cultivars, in the vegetative growth stage, the populations of the weevil were similar. For the reproductive and maturation stages, significant differences were observed between the cultivars. The tendency of *S. spinki* to reach the highest population in the IDIAP L7 cultivar, and for this population to increase towards the reproductive and maturation stages, was consistent in all plantings. While the population of the weevil in the Idiap 38 cultivar did not show an increase in population towards these two stages.

The growth of *S. spinki* populations was consistently related to the phenological stages of the crop. This implies that when the maximum populations are reached during the reproductive phase (grain formation), significant reductions in yields can occur. This reduction in yields is related to the damage caused by the weevil to the rice grains and, consequently, to the formation and filling of the grains.

## 6. METHODOLOGY FOR EVALUATING POPULATIONS

Monitoring populations is a vital activity for deciding the appropriate time to apply a treatment and for evaluating its effectiveness. It is also important to establish the frequency of sampling and the number of samples to be evaluated. The monitoring methodology for *S. spinki* used in Cuba is summarized in the Manual by Almaguel et al. (2005). The following elements are included:

Samples should be taken weekly or every two weeks from germination until the dough stage, a total of 30 stems, one per plant, approximately 10 stems per irrigation terrace.

In phase A. From germination and before mass flowering. Observe the two first leaves. These have small, open grains and low population levels.

In phase B. From mass flowering (panicles) until the beginning of grain formation. Observe the younger leaves, from top to bottom, on the plant.

Field observations should be carried out with a magnifying glass with a magnification of 10 or more, and the number of adults present at the base, center, and apex of the leaf grain should be counted. Observation under a stereoscopic microscope is more effective. To determine the infestation levels in the fields, in Cuba, the following scale was used:

## 7. SYMPTOMS AND DAMAGE

The rice mite, *S. spinki*, is a very small arthropod that is not visible to the naked eye. It is typically found on the inner side of the leaf sheaths, which makes diagnosis and control difficult. The most noticeable symptoms caused by *S. spinki* occur during or after the emergence of the panicle, such as vane damage and grain spotting. The severity of vane damage varies depending on the intensity of the infestation.

The damage caused by rice mite infestation can be direct, due to the feeding of a large number of individuals, and indirect, due to the injection of toxins during feeding and the spread of plant pathogens, especially fungi and bacteria.

The stylets of *S. spinki* penetrate the epidermis of rice plant cells, and this feeding mechanism causes necrotic lesions on the surface of the sheath and the grain. Rice plants affected by rice mites generally become stunted and exhibit problems with their development: deformed panicles and inflorescences, necrotic and dehydrated tissues, brown spots on the grain surface, sterility, and reduced grain quality and panicle number.

The main problem for the farmer is that when the symptoms on the panicles are visible, most of the damage has already been done, and it is difficult to reverse the process. Direct damage caused by mite feeding on the leaf sheaths includes loss of moisture in the tissues and tissue damage (empty cells without chlorophyll).

Indirectly, the introduction of toxins (toxic substances) has been demonstrated, which stimulate tissue deformation, especially in the grain, inducing a symptom called "oriole spot." It has also been shown that it can carry spores of the fungus *S. oryzae* on its body, and as a result of its feeding, it causes the rupture of plant tissues, which allows the entry of other opportunistic pathogens.

Field and laboratory observations show that rice plants affected by *S. spinki* have vanes that are damaged, partially filled, and spotted. Mites were also observed feeding on the grains. Damaged panicles showed abnormal curvature of the peduncle, and those in the ripening stage remained upright. Inside the sheaths, small or necrotic areas with high populations of mites were observed in all stages: eggs, larvae, and adults.

In the affected plants, brown necrotic spots were observed, mainly at the top of the leaf sheath enclosing the panicle. This lesion can extend and cover the entire sheath. Panicles from necrotic sheaths had discolored, wrinkled grains with brown-red spots up to complete necrosis, ranging from dirty brown to dark brown. However, the symptoms are not always visible in the early stages of plant development, which makes early detection difficult.

*Steneotarsonemus spinki* lives and develops inside the leaf sheaths of rice. Specifically, at the base, on the spikelets before and during flowering, and inside the floral organs. It feeds and causes necrosis, ovary atrophy, and flower abortion. It interrupts the nutrient circulation for grain formation until the milky stage, and it spreads *S. oryzae* conidia.

In a study of the histological damage caused by *S. spinki* feeding on the leaf sheaths of the *Perla de Cuba* variety, it was possible to observe disordered epidermal tissue and hypertrophied cells, which can be related to the observed damage to yields. In the *Morelos A-92* variety in Mexico, it was demonstrated that plants with high mite populations showed significant histological damage, characterized by the destruction of epidermal cells on the adaxial surface, disordered growth, color change, and hypertrophy of mesophyll cells, as well as color change in the abaxial epidermis.

Due to the protected position that this species occupies inside the leaf sheath of rice cultivation, it is very important to know the symptoms it produces, in order to guide surveys for detection in countries that are free of this invasive exotic species, or for systematic monitoring based on signaling and forecasting.

In a study conducted in Vietnam, where rice plants were inoculated with increasing densities of *S. spinki* (1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 16, and 32 mites/stem) 30 and 45 days after germination, it was observed that the mite significantly reduced plant height, panicle weight, and curvature, and increased the percentage of sterile inflorescences. These effects increased with the increase in mite density per stem, leading to a reduction in yields of up to 89%.

Several authors have reported that high mite densities are associated with high planting densities and high relative humidities. Cultural practices contribute to delaying the presence of the mite in the crop and reduce yield losses and production costs.

## 8. DISEASES TRANSMITTED BY THE MITE

The American Society of Plant Pathology (APS) reports 43 diseases affecting rice, distributed as follows: 21 caused by fungi, 12 by viruses, six by bacteria, and four by nematodes. Of these, the diseases with the greatest economic impact are those caused by the following fungi: *Pyricularia oryzae* Sacc., *Cochliobolus miyabeanus* Ito and Kobayashi, *Rhizoctonia solani* Kühn, and *S. oryzae*. The following diseases, in one way or another, have been reported in association with *S. spinki* (Table 5).

Several authors have reported the co-occurrence of *S. spinki* and diseases of rice crops, and it is evident that, in many cases, *S. spinki* was the dominant problem. It is believed that mite feeding on the sheath and grains increases the incidence of sheath blight and grain blight, by facilitating the entry of the pathogen into the plant.

One of the most important problems that affects the identification of *S. spinki* symptoms is its association with the fungus *S. oryzae* and the bacterium *B. glumae*. The association of the mite with the fungus exacerbates sheath blight, and these are reported as being responsible for the high sterility of panicles, which causes significant yield losses.

In Panama, in 2007, the association of the bacteria *B. glumae* and *B. gladioli* (Severini) was described, which causes the symptoms of panicle blight ('panicle blight') and subsequently, in Louisiana, United States, bacterial panicle blight ('bacterial panicle blight') was found, a damage that includes grain discoloration and upright panicles in rice plants affected by the mite.

Since diseases can cause severe damage to rice crops, it is important for the farmer to be able to identify and carry out frequent monitoring in their crops to detect the early symptoms of the presence of diseases, in order to take control or prevention measures.

The damage caused by the fungus to the affected plants includes reducing the size of the flag leaf sheath, preventing panicle emergence, and reducing grain weight. The disease causes rotting of the flag leaf sheath, an affliction that either prevents or restricts panicle emergence.

The panicles that do emerge have curved flowers, ranging in color from reddish-brown to dark brown. Sterility and grain shriveling are also symptoms caused by the presence of this fungus. The grains of infected plants contain 2.2 µg of helvolic acid and 1.75 µg of cerulenine per gram of infected seed, which induces chlorosis and reduces the viability of the seeds and the health of the seedlings.

The fungus enters the plant through stomata or through wounds, and sometimes through the sheath tissue. Secondary infections have also been observed due to inoculum transported by wind. The fungus has a white mycelium and vertical conidiophores with one or two branches. Conidia are formed at the terminal end of the conidiophores, and they are cylindrical and, in some cases, curved, hyaline, and unicellular.

Over time, the mycelium turns salmon-colored. If the relative humidity is high, the fungus develops a white layer composed of mycelium or conidia between the infected sheath and the stem. The S. oryzae mycelium is capable of surviving in crop residues and in seed. This pathogen has some weeds and bamboo as alternative hosts. S. oryzae causes a reduction in rice germination between 11 and 32%, and delays this stage of the crop by 7 to 11 days. When the severity of the disease is high, the discoloration of the panicles can reach 78 to 100%.

Once the fungus has entered the plant, it grows intracellularly. Damage caused by mites, stalk borers, and cutworms favors the development of the disease. This disease is also favored by high levels of nitrogen, high planting densities, relative humidity above 90%, and temperatures between 20 and 25 °C.

The harmful effects that S. oryzae has on rice plants include reducing the translocation of nutrients from the leaves to the grain and blocking panicle emergence. This directly affects grain formation. The components of yield that are affected are: a reduction in the weight of the grains of the affected panicles, which can range from 53 to 79%, the affected panicles can account for 18 to 33% of the total harvest, and estimated yield losses of 9 to 25%.

It is known that S. oryzae is capable of surviving in crop residues for 2 to 6 months, depending on environmental conditions. Therefore, the straw plays an important role as a source of inoculum for seedlings, and therefore, is a reservoir of the pathogen in the fields for transmission and epidemiology of the disease. An association has been found between S. oryzae and the presence of the Leptocorisa acuta weevil, and the tarsonemid mites S. spinki and S. madecassus. In this sense, in Cuba, a marked relationship has been observed between this disease and the S. spinki weevil.

Field recognition. The symptoms begin as an oblong lesion, with irregular brown margins, with a pale-colored center forming a patch, mainly at the top of the sheath enclosing the panicle. Many necrotic spots can also be observed at the top of the sheath. In cases where the intensity of the attack is very high, the necrosis of the top of the sheath extends and leads to a complete and total necrosis of the sheath, which is red-violet (Fig. 6).

The phenology of the crop plays an important role in the manifestation of symptoms caused by S. oryzae. This pathogen is transmitted through infected seeds, which constitutes the first mode of infection in the fields. During the active flowering stage, it remains systemic in the plant, so its appearance is only observable in the field when the rice is in the panicle stage. The increase is greater as it approaches harvest. Plants are more susceptible from tillering to the milky stage of the grain. An infection during this stage of development causes a significant reduction in yield.

The intensity of the disease is strongly associated with entomological factors, to the point that this relationship has become a strategy for searching for rice varieties that are genetically resistant to this disease. Among the species reported are: the tarsonemid mites S. spinki and S. madecassus, the weevil Brevennia rehi (Lindinger) and Leptocorisa acuta (Thunb), and the stalk borer. Certain cultivation conditions, mainly fertilization, directly influence the incidence and severity of the disease.

Increases in nitrogen doses increase the incidence of the disease, both on the foliage and on the ground, while increases in potassium doses up to 200 kg, and foliar applications of calcium sulfate, potassium chloride, or zinc sulfate, reduced the incidence of the disease.

The interaction between S. oryzae and some rice pathogens has also been investigated, and it has been shown that O. oryzae restricts the growth of Sclerotium oryzae Catt. and Gaeumannomyces graminis var. Gramini (Sacc.) Arx & D.L. Olivier, while no effects have been observed on other pathogens such as Rhizoctonia solani (Kühn) and Pyricularia grisea (Sacc.).

Disease management. Rice varieties show different degrees of susceptibility to S. oryzae. In a study carried out in Cuba, attack intensities of 70 to 89% were recorded in the sheaths, with panicle discoloration ranging from 75 to 88%. The variety 2084 contributed the most to the discoloration. IACuba 23, IACuba 19, IACuba 22 and IACuba 15 were classified as intermediates. Finally, Perla de Cuba, J 104, IACuba 24, IACuba 17, IACuba 16 and Reforma were the least affected. The varieties with the highest transmission of S. oryzae -through seed- were Perla de Cuba, J 104, 2084 and Reforma.

Seed disinfection contributes significantly to yield indicators with a weight of more than 1000 grains, a decrease in the percentage of shriveling, and therefore, a loss of weight due to this concept. The most effective chemical treatments in Cuba for controlling seed fungi are: benomyl (150 bp/100 kg of seeds), carbendazim (150 bp/100 kg of seeds), azoxystrobin (25 and 50 g bp/100 kg of seeds), triadimenol (50 and 75 g bp/100 kg of seed), the mixture of triadimenol + tebuconazole (Silvacur Combi EC 30) (11+34 g bp/100 kg of seed) and fludioxonil (100 g bp/100 kg of seeds).

Biological control is a promising strategy in the management of the disease. It has been shown that protecting rice seeds with Trichoderma harzianum Riley (strain A34) and Bacillus subtilis (Ehrenberg) Cohn (strain A38) reduced infections by the pathogen, with increases in germination and seedling vigor as a more option within the control measures. The strains of B. subtilis 1L and 1R managed around 60% of the infection by this fungus in artificially inoculated rice seeds.

Treating seeds with Trichoderma antagonists provides good control of diseases in the early stages of plant development, so a proper covering of seeds with biocontrol agents of this genus can guarantee satisfactory control of pathogens present both in the seeds and in the soil.

The strain A34 of T. harzianum -the standard strain for production that is produced in the Centers for Reproduction of Entomophagous and Entomopathogenic (CREE) in Cuba- for applications in substrates in nurseries and for seeds that are sown by companies and organic farms, showed good effectiveness against S. oryzae.

Furthermore, studies have been carried out in Cuba that demonstrate that the isolation of T. harzianum A34 is recommended to combat fungal pathogens in vegetables. This is because it is capable of proliferating in the soil from treated seeds and colonizing the substrate before the root system of numerous plants, such as peppers and tomatoes. Therefore, after 18 days of germination, the root growth reaches between 1.5-3.5 cm on average, but the depth recorded in the soil by T. harzianum A34 is superior, ensuring adequate protection of the rhizosphere.

This indicates that preventive treatments in seeds can guarantee the proliferation of Trichoderma in the soil. In this sense, it has been pointed out that after protecting seeds with Trichoderma, the population of this fungus in the rhizosphere of the plants increases. This, with proper protection against the presence of pathogens in the root zone.

It is recommended to use chemical control only when necessary. When evaluating the action of 19 fungicides in vitro at 50, 100 and 200 ppm of active ingredient against S. oryzae, it was found that among the most effective in controlling the growth of this fungus were Benomil PH 50. In a study carried out in Colombia, the fungicides Octave PH 50, Derosal 50 SC, Moncut SC 20 were tested, with the first, Octave PH 50 (procloraz) being the most effective in inhibiting the growth of the fungus. The trials carried out at the microparcel level showed the protective (90%) and curative effect of Octave, which was corroborated with the applications carried out at 75 days after germination, when the incidence of the disease was reduced by 92%.

In Cuba, Amistar SC 25 EC (azoxystrobin) at 0.48 L/ha, Silvacur Combi CE 30 (triadimenol + tebuconazol) at 0.5 L/ha, and Anvil SC 25 EC (hexaconazol) at 0.5 L/ha, were the most effective in controlling S. oryzae in the field, and are generally effective where azoxystrobin and triazoles are included. Mainly at the time of panicle emergence.

To manage and prevent an attack of sheath blight, the following recommendations are considered: 1. Plant tolerant varieties in areas with high S. oryzae infection.
2. Use high-quality seeds that are free of pathogens.
3. Avoid planting at a very high density and with an imbalance of nutrients in the soil.
4. Do not use herbicides that can cause physical damage to the plants, as the fungus can penetrate through the wounds.
5. Avoid stressing the crop.
6. Properly dispose of crop residues that have been heavily infected by the fungus and any potential host weeds.
7. Control the insects that attack rice (stalk borers, cutworms, tarsonemid mites and rice thrips).

## 8.2. Burkholderia glumae (kurita and tabei) urakami et al.

Taxonomic classification. The genus Burkholderia was established in 1992 and is composed of species previously included in the genus Pseudomonas. By 2009, 59 species had been reported, which are characterized by being Gram-negative bacteria, belonging to the Filo βProteobacteria, having a bacillus shape, an oxidative metabolism, and being mobile. The taxonomic position is as follows:

Phylum: Proteobacteria

Class: Beta Proteobacteria

Order: Burkholderiales

Family: Burkholderiaceae

Genus:

Burkholderia

Species:

Burkholderia glumae (Kurita and Tabei) Urakami et al.

This bacterium is associated with soil, the rhizosphere, and the surface of various plants, where it is considered an epiphyte, without causing damage to the host, but it constitutes an important reservoir that can give rise to pathologies under certain conditions in numerous crops. In addition, this species is the cause of wilting in many crops, such as tomatoes, chili peppers, eggplants, potatoes, and sunflowers.

Lesions and damage. The optimal growth temperature for B. glumae is relatively high (30-35 °C), which explains the reports that indicate that high temperatures, especially nighttime temperatures, during the critical stage favor the development of bacterial panicle blight. Temperature can also act indirectly, as an increase in nighttime temperature generates greater respiration in plants. This leads to an increase in the utilization of photosynthetic products, as well as an exponential decline in activity. This may predispose the plant to attack by B. glumae.

Burkholderia glumae is the bacterium responsible for the disease known as panicle blight or bacterial rot, which affects the panicle of rice, causing significant economic losses, up to 40% of production annually in rice-producing countries.

Several virulence factors have been identified in B. glumae, including: catalase, flagellar biogenesis, a type III secretion system, and a phytotoxin called toxoflavin. However, the main damaging factor is toxoflavin. This phytotoxin acts as an electron carrier between NADH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate + hydrogen) and oxygen, without the involvement of cytochromes, generating hydrogen peroxide.

Toxoflavin is highly toxic to various types of cells, causing tissue damage. The production of the toxin depends on two factors: the biosynthesis of toxoflavin in B. glumae depends on temperature and occurs between 30 °C and 37 °C (temperatures below this range may result in bacterial multiplication, but without the synthesis of the toxin), and on the other hand, the synthesis of toxoflavin depends on the density of the bacterial population.

Therefore, when B. glumae is present on plants at low densities, it does not cause damage to the plant tissue. When the bacterial population has a high density, greater than 1 x 10^8 bacteria per milliliter, the virulence factors are induced, and the degree of damage to the plant is high.

The damage caused by B. glumae in plants depends on its ability to multiply abundantly, which, in turn, depends on environmental conditions and the availability of nutrients. The disease develops only under high-temperature conditions, especially at night, combined with high relative humidity and frequent rainfall. The bacterium can multiply on the surface of emerging panicles and on flowers after they open.

Bacterial multiplication at high densities leads to the production of toxoflavin, which, through the generation of hydrogen peroxide, causes tissue damage and an obstruction of vascular bundles in rice plants, preventing the arrival of essential monosaccharides and disaccharides for the formation of starch in the panicle. The obstruction of vascular bundles reduces the growth of foliage and root tissue in seedlings and induces symptoms of chlorosis in the panicles during the grain formation phase, being responsible for causing bacterial wilting.

In 1989, 900,000 hectares were affected in the island of Kyushu, Japan. Subsequently, in Louisiana, United States, between 1995 and 1998, a severe outbreak occurred, with losses of 40% of the harvest, as well as in other rice-growing areas of the United States. Other countries where this disease has been reported include: Taiwan, Colombia, Vietnam, Sri Lanka, Malaysia, the Philippines, Nepal, Korea, Panama, Indonesia, Thailand, Tanzania, Cambodia, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, Cuba, the Dominican Republic, Venezuela, Bolivia, and Peru.

The main host of this bacterium is O. sativa, but it has also been reported to cause damage in: Allium cepa L. (onion), Solanum lycopersicum L. (tomato), Sesamum indicum L. (sesame), Solanum melongena L. (eggplant), and Capsicum sp. (chili pepper).

Field recognition. Burkholderia glumae is a bacterium that is mainly transmitted through infected seeds, and it is through this that it spreads to different regions, due to the export and import of seeds. It invades germinating seeds, disables the roots and lower sheaths, and thus begins to grow on the plant as an epiphyte. Symptoms appear during flowering, when the bacterium actively multiplies by invading the spikes through stomata or small wounds in the epidermis of the glumes.

Subsequently, it colonizes the spikes and multiplies rapidly using the sugars needed for grain development. There is evidence that this disease is transmitted by seed, from infected plants in the previous year before planting. The bacterium is located in the basal part of the floret and inside the lemma of the rice grain, and can survive within the seed for up to three years.

This disease is characterized in rice by the presence of panicles that are brown or discolored due to a process of chlorosis, the affected flowers stop growing or are aborted by the plant, the affected panicles may have few or all affected flowers, the grains may show different degrees of discoloration depending on the intensity of the infection (Fig. 7).

Burkholderia glumae is a component of the mite-bacteria complex. This disease causes sterility of the spikelets and discoloration of the grains. The bacterium initially colonizes the flag leaf, invades the spikelets, and causes grain rot.

Disease management. Solarization has been tested with satisfactory results to eliminate B. glumae from rice seeds in Panama. The seeds had to be exposed to the sun for 18.35 hours to eliminate the bacteria. The addition of transparent or black plastic covers did not reduce the time of solarization.

With the use of dry heat in seed treatment (50 °C), it was possible to identify several treatments that eliminate the B. glumae bacteria from the seed. Among them, the treatment of one hour of dry heat, followed by 24 hours of rest, stands out. Repeating this procedure twice more. This treatment is the most economical and, at the same time, the most feasible to implement in the existing rice dryers. Both treatments can be equally effective against Pseudomonas fuscovaginae Sheath, another bacterium that has often been associated with S. spinki in Central American countries.

Oxalic acid has proven to be the most effective product so far to inhibit the growth and reduce the population of the B. glumae bacteria in rice cultivation. However, integrated management may be a suitable option for producers. The following sequence of actions is detailed for the integrated management of bacterial blight:

- a. Select tolerant varieties for planting.
- b. Use certified seeds.
- c. Plant during the period of lowest environmental risk.
- d. Allow for crop rotation.
- e. Remove or clear away crop residues.
- f. Promote crop rotation.
- g. Avoid planting in compacted soils, use the recommended density and appropriate fertilization for each variety.
- h. In fertilization, do not exceed the amount of nitrogen and carry out fertilization using organic-inorganic interaction.
- i. Avoid stressing rice plants and do not apply late-season herbicides.

- j. Do not plant during the period of low environmental availability for the crop and high risk of bacterial blight.

In Colombia, it has been demonstrated that the second planting season should be carried out between the months of November and December, so that the flowering of the crop does not coincide with the period of best environmental availability for the bacteria, such as high temperature, low solar radiation, high humidity, and frequent rainfall; which occur between the months of April, May, and June. The yields of farmers who have planted during these recommended dates have improved significantly.

Adjusting the planting schedule (avoiding the overlap of the plant's most sensitive periods with the months of greatest multiplication potential for the mite), utilizing an appropriate variety for this situation, along with other measures, made it possible for the mite – although present in rice fields – not to cause a reduction in yields as drastic as the one observed in the early years of its appearance.

In Costa Rica, it has been aimed to enable the producer to adjust the land preparation and planting cycles to coincide with the period of greatest solar radiation. It is recommended that late-cycle varieties, which generally reach 130 days at harvest, be planted from November 1st to December 15th. For intermediate-cycle varieties, a planting period between December 1st and 31st is suggested.

As for early-cycle varieties, the recommended planting date covers from December 15th to January 15th. This is intended to ensure that the planting dates of the varieties coincide with the critical phase of solar radiation. In addition, these dates allow the producer to have sufficient time, between harvest and planting, to break the biological cycles of diseases, insects, rodents, and other pests that affect the crop. During the rainy season, it is recommended to plant intermediate-to-late cycle varieties between July 15th and August 15th, while early-cycle varieties should be planted between July 30th and August 15th.

9.2 Pathogen control: This involves using varieties that are resistant or tolerant to a specific pest. This is the best strategy and often the most cost-effective for its management. This resistance is classified as differential, vertical, or specific and general, horizontal or inespecific. In the first case, the plant may be resistant to a strain or strain of the organism and susceptible to others. In this type, the inheritance is monogenic or oligogenic, therefore, the process is generally shorter to obtain it, but it is also less stable.

On the other hand, in horizontal resistance, it is independent of the strain or strain of the phytophagous organism, so a variety is resistant to all strains or strains. This makes it more stable. In this type, the inheritance is polygenic and depends more on environmental factors.

The resistance of varieties is the oldest and most persistent basis of pest control. From a practical point of view, a variety is considered resistant if it produces a greater quantity and better quality of its harvest than another variety that grows under the same conditions and is exposed to similar populations of a specific pest.

Genetic resistance should always be considered as one of the alternatives in an integrated pest control program, which can easily be integrated with biological controls, cultural measures, chemical control, and organic practices.

In the face of the presence of S. spinki in Cuba, a program was developed to identify and/or obtain varieties with resistance to the mite and associated pathogens, which cause the grain browning syndrome and rice sheath rot. As a central strategy, the following lines of action were established:

1. Creation and/or standardization of the evaluation methodology.
2. Determination of sources of resistance.
3. Creation of genetic variability.
4. Evaluation of commercial and pre-commercial varieties and advanced germplasm available.
5. Determination of the appropriate management to enhance the expression of resistance.

The studies carried out allowed the establishment of grain staining, sheath staining, and percentage of browning as selection criteria in the methodology for evaluating the resistance of varieties to the grain browning syndrome and rice sheath rot. It was also defined that there should be between 17 and 19 mites per plant to consider the evaluation as conclusive, as the number of mites necessary to produce the susceptibility damage is present. Based on these selection criteria and the methodology proposed by Hernández-Concepción (2004), 2735 materials were evaluated, of which 627 were selected, representing 23% of the total evaluated.

This author identified the following as probable mechanisms of resistance:

Antixenosis. Cultivars were found in which the tarsonemid does not establish, as no population was found in them.

Antibiosis. Varieties or lines were found in which the mite establishes, but only adults with very low presence of eggs and even fewer larvae were counted, so the population cannot reach the level of economic damage.

Tolerance. This is the ability of a plant to withstand the damage caused by the mite. The mechanism is a compensatory reaction of the plant to the damage caused by the mite, coexisting with the pest and partially neutralizing the damage received. In the case of tolerance, it is estimated that it may also exist, but it is difficult to determine, as in many cases it may be masked by escape.

When evaluating short and medium-cycle rice varieties, the IACuba-27, IACuba-28, and Reforma varieties showed promising results, with average values ranging from 1.0 to 18.3 mites per plant. In contrast, the Perla de Cuba variety, with 78.0 mites per plant, showed a higher number of mites. This demonstrates the susceptibility of this variety to the pest.

Also, when evaluating the response of five varieties to S. spinki and the sheath rot caused by the fungus S. oryzae, the INCA LP-5 and Reforma varieties showed the best results in the spring, with a lower number of mites per plant, low incidence of S. oryzae, lower level of grain staining, and higher agricultural yield; while Perla de Cuba and IACuba-28 were more affected by the mite.

In trials with different rice varieties, under Cuban conditions, the LC-88-66, Reforma, IACuba 28, LP-7, LP5, IACuba 27, and IACuba 22 varieties were found to show superior tolerance levels to this pest. In all cases, in spring plantings, S. spinki causes more damage than in winter plantings, shortening the onset of the mite.

As a result of a project funded by FONTAGRO, it was concluded that the development of improved germplasm with tolerance to the main biotic and abiotic factors, with emphasis on the organisms causing the mite-fungus-bacteria complex, is the most pertinent and cost-effective strategy to face this and any other phytosanitary problem in the future. Therefore, the greatest effort and financial and human resources were dedicated to evaluating the germplasm of the countries involved in the project, in order to identify the most tolerant genotypes to these biotic factors.

For this purpose, crosses were made between commercial varieties in the region and tolerant parents from Taiwan. Among the most relevant results were the release, by the IDIAP, of three varieties that in 2008 covered 36% of the planted area. While in the Institute of Agricultural Technology (INTA) in Nicaragua, three promising lines have been identified, with INTA Fortaleza Secano and SENUMISA standing out. Costa Rica registered three commercial varieties (FEDEARROZ 50, CFX 18, and CR 4477).

9.4 Chemical control: Most of the systemic products tested have not been effective, so they have not been a safe way to manage the populations of S. spinki. The best products are from the organophosphate group. When a product is effective, it has the advantage of achieving a rapid and economically viable effect. However, they can harm beneficial organisms, cause imbalance in the agroecosystem, and affect people; if the necessary safety measures are not taken and resistance in the pest is not generated.

Many products have been used internationally to control the populations of S. spinki. The following is a group of products used in different countries:

- China: Chlorfenesulphide + Etrofolan (Isoprocarb), Dichlovos, Thiophanato and Dimetoate.
- India: Parathion, Dicofol and Dimetoate.
- United States (Florida): Parathion and Dicofol.
- Costa Rica: Profenofos, Deltametrina + Triazofos, Abamectins.
- Dominican Republic: Curacron, Dicofol, Abamectina, Hostation, Kelthane, Imidacloprid.
- Cuba: Triazofos (Hostathion 40 EC at 0.15% PC), Endosulfan (Thiodan 50 PH at 0.2% PC) with adherant 810 at 0.15% (more effective for control, with 15 or more days of protection), Ethoprophos (MOCAP 15 G at 0.5% PC), which was very effective for disinfecting rice post, Diafentiuron, Protiofos, Paration metil, Fludioxonilo FS 312, Yunta FS 246, Biomite and Abamectina.

This mite is difficult to control due to its ethological characteristics and its high reproductive potential. Because it occupies the plant, it is almost invulnerable to the chemical and biological products used for its control. Most chemical systemic products are not effective against S. spinki, as has been demonstrated by several specialists.

In Cuba, the best results were found with Triazophos (Hostathion 40 EC at 0.15% PC), Endosulfan (Thiodan 50 PH at 0.2% PC) and Ethoprophos (Mocap) 15 G at 0.5% PC, under controlled and production conditions. However, these products are extremely toxic and have a wide action on natural enemies, which limits their use as a candidate in a pest control program, and their use should be restricted to the maximum. Currently, Thiodan and Etoprofos have been withdrawn from the market.

For this reason, a group of safer chemical products from an environmental point of view were selected, which exerted an acceptable control. Among them were: Biomite 2.0 CE at 2.0 L/ha, Abamectina 1 CE at 12.0 L/ha, Dicofol 2.0 SC at 1.5-2.0 L/ha or Calcium Hydroxide at 2-4 g/L of H2O.

Greater control effectiveness has been demonstrated when acaricides are applied together with a fungicide that controls S. oryzae. With the treatments consisting of the mixture Triazophos+Hexaconazol (1.5+0.7 L/ha) the highest percentage of full grains (67.05%) and the lowest percentage of empty grains (22.95%) was achieved, followed by Hexaconazol (1.0 L/ha) with 62.03% full grains and 27.97% empty grains. The results with these treatments were, in turn, statistically different from those obtained when only the acaricides were applied.

9.4 Integrated Pest Management: Integrated Pest Management (IPM) consists of combining different techniques, giving priority to biological, ecological, and cultural methods, to restrict the use of chemical pesticides to a minimum. This maintains the pest population below levels that cause unacceptable economic damage to the farmer.

For the development and implementation of a Program of Integrated Pest Management for S. spinki, it is essential to have a thorough knowledge of the biology and ecology of the mite. The results of product tests, the effectiveness of the evaluated control methods, studies of the sources of resistance in rice varieties and lines, as well as a set of measures, from legal to cultural, that guarantee the success of the proposal.

The following actions are related, which, individually or as a system, can contribute to reducing the effects of S. spinki and, therefore, those of other possible associated pathogens. These constitute a guide for producers and technicians for the management of the rice mite. However, they need to be adjusted to the local agroecological conditions and the available resources. Also, a training and adoption process of technologies is required, by the producers, to relearn how to cultivate rice on new budgets, where the transition from scheduled applications to the implementation of action thresholds is made.

Among the measures that can be adopted are the following:

1. Prioritize the use of rice varieties that have shown acceptable tolerance/resistance to the effects of S. spinki and the complex of pathogens that cause grain staining; that have been evaluated in the conditions of each country.
2. Recommend the use of short or intermediate cycle varieties, as they offer a shorter time for the population increase of the mite.
3. Use certified seeds, with certainty that they are free of pathogenic organisms, especially those associated with grain staining and/or perform their disinfection before planting.
4. Promote transplanting, which guarantees satisfactory yields, allows disinfection of the post, and thus delays the arrival of the mite to the crop.
5. Practice, whenever possible, crop rotation to ensure the interruption of the mite's life cycle and reduce the inoculum of pathogens.
6. Ensure, whenever possible, that the area to be planted is free of rice plants, crop residues and alternative host weeds, for 25 or 30 days before planting, through mechanical, chemical, or animal grazing methods, which have the same purpose.
7. Maintain rice fields, crop residues and weeds free of rice plants, for the same purpose.
8. Compact the soil, whenever the technology and availability of implements allow, to avoid unfavorable border conditions, delaying the arrival of the mite to the new plantings.
9. Prioritize planting, whenever possible, during the period of the year that offers more unfavorable climatic conditions for the population increase of the mite and pathogens (lower temperatures and relative humidity), with emphasis on the period between November and January.
10. Plant the fields, whenever possible, in such a way as to avoid the spread of the mite through irrigation water, from older fields to younger ones.
11. Adjust the seed rates, so that the planting densities are not higher than 130 kg/ha, ensuring uniform distribution.
12. Fraction the nitrogen applications, with a total dose that does not exceed 130 kg/ha.
13. Monitor the population levels of S. spinki to determine the action thresholds. If possible, the sampling should begin at 20 days after germination until the milky stage, with a frequency of once a week. If not possible, the sampling should begin in the reproductive phase (start of the floral primordium) until the milky stage. In each of the three or four subdivided fields, the areas most prone to infestation by the mite (edge, unfavorable border conditions, entry of machinery) should be determined, and from each area, 10 stems should be taken, to sum a sample never less than 30 stems per sampling. In the stems taken in the vegetative phase, the number of adults in the first and second leaf of the sheath, starting from the upper leaf completely emerged, should be counted. In the stems taken in the reproductive phase, the three first leaves with sheath should be considered. In addition to the presence of the rice mite, the presence of predatory mites should be considered and counted.
14. The action thresholds to be considered for applying chemical products can vary. They may depend on the time of year, variety, growth stage of the crop, and the time of year. However, it is considered that when 5 mites per vein are reached, a control measure should be taken. This control should ensure that when the plant reaches the swelling or panicle stage,

- Minimize the number of mites on the flag leaf, at which point the pest causes the greatest yield losses.
15. Apply acaricidal or insecticidal/acaricidal chemicals with proven effectiveness against S. spinki. If possible, select those with the highest specificity for the pest and the lowest environmental impact and on natural enemies.
16. Apply fungicidal/bactericidal chemicals to control the fungi/bacteria causing grain stain, especially during panicle formation or flowering.

Based on the survey results analyzed, the chemicals that producers prefer, although not the only ones, are:

- a. For seed disinfection: Carbothin, Captan, Imidacloprid, Fludioxonilo
- b. Against mites: Triazofos, Dicofol, Deltamethrin, Abemectin, Imidacloprid
- c. Against bacteria: Azoxistrobina - Cyproconazole, Copper sulfate, Benzothiol, Ixoic acid, Kasugamicina
- d. Against fungi: Tebuconazole, Triadimenol, Carbendazim, Tebuconazole, Azoxistrobina, Triadimenol, Benomil, Azoxystrobin
- e. Against insects: Deltamethrin, Profenofos, Dimetoate, Cypermethrin

## 11. CONCLUSIONS

This document serves as a reference for the Integrated Management of Steneotarsonemus spinki in rice cultivation, which should be contextualized to the conditions and realities of each country. Priority is given to cultural and crop management measures, in order to reduce the effects caused by this pest, with the lowest possible environmental cost.

The validation of the proposed actions in this manual, under local agroecological conditions, through demonstration plots in countries in the OIRSA region, will allow for the necessary adjustments to be made, with the aim of constituting a guide for managing the problem caused by S. spinki in rice cultivation.
