## I. INTRODUCTION

Corn is the most important crop in the basic food basket of the Salvadoran population. According to FAO, per capita consumption is approximately 80.51 kilograms in urban areas and 127 kilograms in rural areas, making it the highest consumption in the Central American region. 95% of the production is destined for human consumption.

According to the General Directorate of Agricultural Economy, during the 2016-2017 agricultural cycle, the area planted with maize was 433,603 hectares (303,522 acres) with a production of 20,316,389 quintals, with an average yield of 46.9 quintals per hectare.

Under suitable climatic conditions or through irrigation, maize is the most productive cereal, and profitability increases when improved varieties are used under favorable conditions and with proper management.

One of the main objectives of CENTA is to develop maize varieties and hybrids that are adapted to the edaphoclimatic conditions in which producers cultivate this crop.

## II. GENERAL INFORMATION

## A) ORIGIN

Maize (Zea mays L.) belongs to the grass family, tribe Zea, and is believed to have originated in the tropical regions of Latin America, particularly in the Zea, Tripsacum, and Euchlaena genera, whose importance lies in its genetic relationship with the Zea genus.

## B) PLANT DESCRIPTION

The maize root system has seminal roots that develop from the radicle of the seed to the depth at which it has been planted. The growth of these roots decreases after the plumule emerges above the soil surface and virtually stops completely in the three-leaf stage of the seedling. The first adventitious roots begin to develop from the first node at the end of the cole.

Technical Guide

This generally occurs at a uniform depth, regardless of the depth at which the seed was planted. A group of adventitious roots develops from each subsequent node until reaching between seven and ten nodes, all below the soil surface. These adventitious roots develop in a dense network of fibrous roots. The adventitious root system is the main fixation system for the plant and also absorbs water and nutrients. Research has found that the seminal adventitious root system constitutes approximately 52% and that the root nodule system is 48% of the total root mass of the maize plant.

The plant stem is robust, formed by nodes and internodes that are more or less distant, has 15 to 30 elongated and embracing leaves that are 4 to 10 centimeters wide and 35 to 50 centimeters long, with a rough, finely ciliated, and slightly wavy edge. From the point where the peduncle that supports the ear emerges, the section of the stem is circular until the panicle or male inflorescence, which crowns the plant.

## C) FLOWERING HABITS

Maize is typically monoecious, with a terminal flowering spike (panicle) or male flowers, and female flowers with pistils, located in lateral buds (ears); therefore, maize produces its economic yield (grain) in lateral branches. As a result of this separation of the ear and panicle, and the phenomenon called protandry in flowering, maize is a self-pollinating species (cross-pollination) and its type of inflorescence has allowed for the production of hybrids with high yield potential and wide adaptation.

## III. EDAPHOCLIMATIC FACTORS

Maize is a plant with a wide capacity to respond to the opportunities offered by the environment and has a high response to the effects of light. Currently, there is a diversity of varieties suitable for cultivation under very different natural conditions than its original habitat.

## ADAPTATION

Maize has good vegetative development that can reach up to 5 meters in height at altitudes above 1000 meters above sea level (masl). In El Salvador, the best yields are obtained in the range of 0 to 900 masl, the plant reaches a height of 2 to 2.65 meters, so these germplasms are considered tropical. The crop grows between latitudes 55º N and 40º S.

## SOIL

Maize adapts to a wide variety of soils where good yields can be obtained if the appropriate varieties and cultivation techniques are used.

In general, the most suitable soils for maize cultivation are medium-textured (sandy loam), fertile, well-drained, deep, and with high water retention capacity.

Maize generally grows well in soils with a pH between 5.5 and 7.8. Outside these limits, the availability of certain elements tends to increase or decrease, and toxicity or deficiency occurs; when the pH is below 5.5, there are often problems of toxicity from Aluminum and Manganese, as well as deficiencies of Phosphorus and Magnesium; with a pH above 8 (or above 7 in calcareous soils), a tendency to present deficiencies of Iron, Manganese and Zinc is observed. The symptoms in the field of an inadequate pH are generally similar to those of micronutrient problems.

## WATER

The lack of water is the most limiting factor in maize production in tropical areas. When there is water stress or drought during the early stages (15 to 30 days) of establishment, young plants may be lost, thus reducing the population density or stagnating their growth; however, the crop can recover without seriously affecting the yield. Near flowering (about two weeks before the emergence of stigmas, and up to two weeks after), maize is very sensitive to water stress, and grain yield can be seriously affected if there is a drought during this period. In general, maize needs at least 500 to 700 mm of well-distributed rainfall during the crop cycle.

Maize is also very sensitive to waterlogging or flooding, i.e., to soils that are saturated and oversaturated. From sowing until approximately 15 to 20 days, waterlogging for more than 24 hours can damage the crop (especially if the temperatures are high) because the meristem is below the soil surface at this time; later, in the crop cycle, waterlogging can be tolerated for periods of up to one week, but the yield is reduced.

IV. HIGH-QUALITY PROTEIN MAIZE (BIOFORTIFIED)

These are maize varieties that have been developed through conventional genetic improvement methods and not through genetic engineering.

The high protein quality of these maize varieties is due to the Opaco-2 mutation, which doubles the levels of essential amino acids Lysine and Tryptophan in the grain, which provides a characteristic for milling, and were initially called "soft maize". This gene was discovered in 1964 at Purdue University, USA.

The International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center (CIMMYT) and the National Center for Agricultural Technology and Forestry 'Enrique Álvarez Córdoba' (CENTA) have been working on maize varieties since the 1970s to select genes that modify the endosperm, to obtain a hard grain type similar to normal maize. Recent work has allowed the development of materials with high protein quality such as the H-59, H-CAS, Oro Blanco, Platino and varieties of free pollination such as Protemás and Dorado (yellow grain).

Since 2011, CENTA, CIMMYT and the Flinders University (Australia) have been working together to develop maize germplasm not only with high protein quality but also with high levels of essential amino acids Lysine and Tryptophan.

## V. RECOMMENDED VARIETIES

Among the various varieties developed by CENTA, the H-53, H-56, H-57 and HQ-61 white hybrids, as well as the CENTA M-3B free pollination variety, can be mentioned. All of these varieties were a good alternative for farmers at the time.

Currently, CENTA has the H-59 and H-CAS white hybrids, the latter in addition to its high yield potential, also has higher levels of essential amino acids Lysine and Tryptophan, so they are called high-protein maize (HPM). The H-CAS hybrid is distinguished by its relative tolerance to asfalto spot and resistance to lodging, as reported by farmers who have cultivated it. Among the white free pollination varieties, CENTA Pasaquina for limited humidity areas, and CENTA Santa Rosa for areas with lodging problems (0 to 30 masl).

Given the importance of this crop, CENTA, through the Basic Grains Program, has dedicated a lot of effort to the agricultural research of this crop. Work has been carried out for more than 40 years, and as a result, the first national hybrids (H-3 and H-5) were developed, which surpassed the local varieties, resulting in an increase in maize production, becoming a leader in the Central American region.

In 2017, the CENTA-AS varieties, grain color yellow and limited humidity tolerance, and CENTA-CS, grain color white and high protein quality, were released.

**VI. SEED**

It is very important to use high germination (at least 85%) and pure seed, characteristics that are guaranteed by seed producers. For hybrid maize, it is recommended to purchase new seed for each planting; while for improved free-pollinating varieties, the grain obtained from a harvest can be used as seed for two or three years, provided it is properly selected.

**FORM AND SIZE**

Maize seeds can be found in round and flat shapes, as well as sizes ranging from small to extra large. It is very important to know that all types and sizes of maize seed have the same genetic characteristics. In the case of hybrids, they possess the potential for hybrid vigor, which makes them have a higher potential for yield. This means that the shape of the seed is not a determining factor for good commercial production.

**SEED QUANTITY PER PLANT**

The amount of seed to use per unit of area can vary depending on the size of the seed and the type of hybrid or variety used. For example, one pound of extra-large seed can contain 1270 seeds, and one pound of small seed of the same hybrid can contain 1910 seeds. This means that with one pound of small seed, we can sow a larger area.

To sow one plant manually, approximately 25 pounds (16 kg/ha) of seed are used; while for mechanical sowing, 30 pounds per plant (19 kg/ha) are used.

**VII. CULTURAL PRACTICES**

**SOIL PREPARATION**

Minimum tillage is a beneficial practice for farmers with sloping or well-drained land, as it reduces erosion; it also allows for greater water retention by not disturbing or exposing the soil to the action of sunlight and wind.

If the weeds are more than 50 cm tall, a weeding is carried out, and 8 to 15 days later, a herbicide such as Glyphosate or Ammonium Glyphosate is applied.

If the soil preparation is mechanized, it is advisable to carry out one pass of a plow, two or three passes of a harrow, and if possible, to level the soil. The harrow passes can be made at 15 or 20 cm depth, depending on the type of soil; the last harrow pass is recommended to be done before sowing.

**SOWING**

In El Salvador, there are 3 sowing seasons:

**FIRST**

This season generally extends from May 15 to 30, for the coastal area (0 to 400 msnm), and from May 15 to June 15, for the intermediate valleys (400 to 900 msnm). These dates may vary depending on the establishment of the rainy season.

**SECOND**

This season, also known as "tunalmil," extends from August 15 to 31, especially for intermediate valleys (400 to 900 msnm) and the Eastern region of the country. During this season, there is a risk that the rainy season will end before the crop reaches its maturity or drying stage, which can result in a decrease in yield.

**THIRD**

This is the one that is carried out in fields that are flooded during the rainy season, which retain enough moisture to be used until the end of the rainy season. The sowing months can vary depending on the circumstances of each area; the season can extend from December to February.

In areas where irrigation is available, the sowing seasons can vary depending on the needs or planning of each farmer, but it is recommended to sow between December 1 and January 15.

**SOWING METHODS**

Sowing can be carried out under two methods or forms:

**MANUAL**

This is carried out especially in fields with slopes greater than 20%, using a hoe or spade to make a hole in the soil and deposit the seed. The spacing between rows oscillates between 0.75 to 0.90 m and between plants 0.40 to 0.50 m, depositing 2 seeds in each one, obtaining a density of 47,750 plants per plant (50,000 plants/ha). The optimal population for satisfactory production is 45,000 plants per plant (65,000 plants/ha), which is obtained with a spacing between rows of 0.8 m to 0.40 while plant and two plants per plant. The spacing between rows can vary depending on whether the farmer sows crops in rotation such as beans or sorghum (maicillo), but especially the topography of the land and the presence of stones.

<!-- image -->

**MECHANIZED OR ANIMAL-DRAWN**

This method is used in fields with flat to semi-flat topography, where both soil preparation and sowing can be mechanized. Also carry out the soil preparation (plowing, harrowing) with machinery, then harrow with oxen and sowing in manual form; leaving a spacing between rows of 0.80 to 0.90 m and 0.40 to 0.50 m between plants, depositing 2 seeds in each one; when the areas are larger, the sowing can be carried out with machinery, with a spacing between rows as mentioned above, and the planter deposits 10 to 12 seeds per linear meter, then a harrowing is carried out to leave a spacing between plants of 0.20 to 0.25 m. In both systems, the density can vary between 35,000 to 45,000 plants per plant (50,000 to 60,000 plants/ha).

Maize is a plant with a rapid growth capacity and high yield that requires considerable amounts of nutrients.

**NITROGEN**

The demand for Nitrogen increases as the plant develops; when the flowering stage approaches, the absorption of this element increases rapidly, so that when the female flowers appear, the plant has absorbed more than half of the total extracted during the entire cycle. High-yielding hybrids require approximately 30 kilograms of Nitrogen for each ton of grain produced.

**PHOSPHORUS**

Although the amount of Phosphorus in the maize plant is low compared to Nitrogen and Potassium, it is an important element for maize nutrition, and the largest concentrations are found in young tissues. This element is also very important for root development. The amount of phosphorus extracted by the plants in normal growing conditions is approximately 10 kilograms per ton of grain harvested.

## POTASSIUM

Maize requires high amounts of potassium. The application of fertilizers based on this element should be carried out in the second and third applications, in a localized (foliar) and incorporated manner.

## FERTILIZATION

Maize is very demanding in terms of nutrients compared to other crops, so in a fertilization plan, it is important to take into account the results of the soil's chemical analysis and its recommendations. This ensures that the plant receives the necessary nutrients and avoids unnecessary expenses, creating a balanced nutritional environment in the soil.

The most recommended method for applying fertilizer is through foliar application and incorporation; although there are others, such as: surface application and band application. For a fertilizer to be effective, there must be adequate moisture in the soil.

## IX. MAIN WEEDS IN MAIZE

A weed is any plant that poses a danger, inconvenience, or causes damage to humans, animals, or, in this case, the maize crop.

The development of the maize crop in the first 30 days is critical, so it is important to ensure that it grows free from the competition of weeds, as these are estimated to be responsible for 10 to 80% of the reduction in yield.

It is important to distinguish between broad-leaved and grassy weeds, as they react differently to herbicides and control methods. Table 6 shows some of the weeds that affect maize cultivation.

## C) MECHANICAL

This involves performing manual tasks using any tillage tool (with a hoe, spade) or mechanized, depending on the type of soil. If weeds are controlled mechanically, the necessary controls should be carried out, especially in the first 30 days of growth, which should be done superficially without damaging the root system of the crop.

## B) CHEMICAL

This involves applying herbicides immediately after planting, post-planting, or post-emergence when the weeds have two or three leaves. This control has the advantage of avoiding damage to the root system of the plants.

When using a herbicide, it is important to consider the crop that will be sown in rotation, as these can have some negative residual effects on the second crop, for example, 2,4-D on beans and Alachlor on sorghum.

It is very important to keep the crop free of weeds even after the second growth, as high populations of weeds can favor a microclimate suitable for the growth of maize stalk fungi.

## X. INSECT PESTS OF MAIZE

From the moment of planting, maize is exposed to the attacks of numerous pathogens (pests), among the factors that favor or hinder the appearance of pests and diseases in the crop are: climate conditions, soil preparation, crop rotation, and weed control, among others.

There is a diversity of insects that attack the crop; for example, the group of stalk borers, which in their larval stage are known as cutworms, soldiers, earworms, and harvesters, and are the ones that cause the most damage; then there are the beetles, which are generally called rootworms, wireworms, cutworms, grain harvesters, and corn earworms; followed by the group of insects that act as vectors of viruses, mycoplasmas, bacteria, and fungi, which in some areas of the country can cause the complete loss of the crop.

Currently, there are maize cultivars that are genetically modified to tolerate or resist the attacks of certain pests. In our country, these cultivars are not currently authorized for cultivation.

## Corn Borer (Dalbulus maydis)

Damage: Adult and nymph corn borers suck sap at the base of the leaves and can cause yellowing, but their main importance lies in that they are vectors of viruses that cause corn curl and fine streaking (Figure 5). Depending on the type of virus the plant is infected with, the damage observed varies; generally, the plants remain small, with the leaf edge turning reddish, and in some cases, this coloration is not present. When the plants reach their reproductive stage, a proliferation of ears occurs without these reaching a commercial size; another symptom is that the foliage shows fine streaking along the central vein (Figure 5.1 and 5.2). Symptoms usually begin to appear when the plantation enters the flowering stage. In El Salvador, the largest reports of the disease are from low-lying areas. The incidence is highest during August and December plantings.

Figure 5. Adult corn borer transmitting the corn curl virus.

## Control

## a) Cultural control:

As much as possible, standardize planting dates to avoid hosts for the vectors.

## b) Chemical control:

Treat the seed with products based on Imidacloprid or systemic products with a long duration to protect the plants for at least the first 15 days, during which the infestation occurs. Apply foliar treatments with products such as: Profenofos 55 EC, Lufenuron 55 EC, Deltamethrin 2.5 SC, Imidacloprid 35 SC, Tiametoxam 25 WG. Given the characteristics and feeding habits of the insect, it is recommended to apply insecticides in the early morning, when the corn borer is most active, and therefore, control is more effective.

## c) Genetic control:

Plant hybrid or improved varieties resistant to corn curl.

## Stem Borer (*Diatraea spp.*)

A moderately important pest. The severity of the damage depends on the age of the plant (Figure 7).

Figure 7. Stem borer in corn.

Damage: The larvae bore into the stems of the plants, causing them to wilt and die, or to develop stunted growth and deformities. When plants are attacked in the first 30 days of the crop, they exhibit wilting and, when the tassel emerges, it easily detaches. The damage is localized and can spread, eventually destroying the crop. The stem boring allows entry for fungi and/or bacteria, which can cause stem rot (Figures 7.1 and 7.2). When the pest attacks at a later stage (before flowering and/or reproduction), it can cause the plants to topple over. It can also bore into the ears, causing what is known as a "dead heart."

Control: Controlling this pest is somewhat difficult, as the damage is not immediately apparent until the plants begin to wilt. The larvae protect themselves as they penetrate the stems. A preventative approach involves using seed treatments with a long-lasting duration. When the damage is identified, an insecticide with a systemic action can be applied to the trunk of the plant using a backpack sprayer with a nozzle that is slightly open, so that the insecticide does not come out as a mist but as a small stream.

## Spider Mites (*Tetranychus spp.*, *Paratetranychus spp.*)

Damage: This pest is a mite, not an insect. The damage can often be mistaken for a foliar disease. They can damage the crop from the seedling stage to maturity. Symptoms appear before flowering or after flowering. The symptom manifests in the leaves, presenting a scorched appearance, concentrated around the central vein with a yellowish-bronze coloration (Figures 8, 8.1, and 8.2). This mite is easily transported by humans from one corn field to another. Reports of damage have been found from the coastal areas to 150 meters above sea level, and near sugarcane crops.

Control: Controlling this pest is difficult, as it resides on the underside of the leaf, and the products to be used are acaricides, not insecticides as is commonly recommended. It is advisable to check the leaves for "scorched" or "burned" appearance when the first symptoms are observed.

The recommended products for its control are:

c. Abamectin 1.8 EC at a rate of 50 cc/200 liters of water.

## Termites (*Rhinotermitidae*)

Damage: Termites are insects that become a pest when they lack a food source. They primarily feed on cellulose, and when they cannot find it, they feed on living tissue. They can attack from the seedling stage, feeding on the roots and causing wilting and death of the plants, or toppling when the attack occurs on plants near harvest. (Figures 9, 9.1, and 9.2). They can also feed on straw, causing the grain to lose quality by reducing weight or detaching from the straw. They have been found inhabiting large trees when they lack a food source or decaying matter.

Figure 9. Termites damaging corn.

Control: To control this pest, it is recommended to treat the seeds with Imidacloprid 70 WG or products with a systemic action. After the seedlings emerge, continue with foliar applications of the same product for at least the first 20 days. Since termites contribute to the decomposition of the straw and its incorporation into the soil, controlling these insects can be a combination of cultural practices with the use of insecticides. To the extent possible, incorporate the straw after the harvest, if incorporation is not possible, till the straw into the field and observe when it is completely decomposed.

## XI. POST-HARVEST PESTS

## INSECT PESTS

There are 13 species of insects that are well adapted to living in stored corn grains and are responsible for the majority of damage to corn grains, both in the field and in commercial storage conditions. These insects are considered first-class pests because they can attack whole, healthy grains. There are 175 species of insects and mites considered secondary, but they can be harmful when abundant and under special environmental conditions such as poor hygiene, high moisture content of the grain, high temperatures, and improper processing, they can become important enemies. The following are the post-harvest insect pests of greatest economic importance.

## CORN BORER ( *Sitophilus zeamais*)

These insects are widely known for the damage they cause to stored grains, and in general, they are called "grain weevils" (*Sitophilus granarius*); the one that attacks rice grains is called "rice weevil" (*Sitophilus orizae*). The corn borer occasionally starts its infestation in the field and, upon reaching the storage, reproduces rapidly (Figures 10 and 10.1).

## CORN WEEVIL ( *Rhizopertha dominica*)

It has a wide distribution around the world due to the damage it causes to almost all grains; both adults (Figure 11) and larvae are voracious and feed on stored products, mainly cereals and healthy grains.

## FLOUR BEETLES

(*Tribolium confusum* and *Tribolium castaneum*)

These two insects are found in all types of flour; they are usually found in dark, humid places where the grain has not been removed for a long period (Figure 12).

They attack a wide variety of products such as grains, flours, baking powder, and other starchy materials. They only feed on grains when they are broken or crushed, or when they have been attacked by weevils, borers, and flour beetles. The most important damage they cause is the contamination of flours and grain-derived products. They reproduce easily in dirty and broken grains.

This pest is abundant and reproduces rapidly in the tropics and warm climates, starting its infestation in the field and continuing to reproduce in the granary because low temperatures favor it (Figures 13 and 13.1); only in the larval stage does it feed on corn and other stored grains.

## FLOUR BEETLE (*Plodia interpunctella*)

This flour beetle is one of the most common and damaging pests of grains and stored products. The infested material is covered with the silk and excrement secreted by the larva, which adheres to the grains and flours (Figure 14). It is the cause of damage to various food products such as: all types of flour, dried fruits, powdered milk, and dried roots, with the larval stage being the most destructive and harmful. They mainly feed on the embryo or germ of the grain. This pest reproduces easily in corn stalks and in corn cobs.

In general, post-harvest pest control can be carried out without using chemicals; however, there are traditional methods such as: exposing the grain to the sun, mixing sand or ash with the grain, fumigating the grain, using garlic in metal silos, etc.; for which it is recommended:

- ·Clean and dry the grain well, with a maximum moisture content of 12%.
- ·Ensure that the storage area and containers are clean, shake the walls and ceilings, and remove old grains before placing new grains.
- ·Store the grain away from damp areas.
- ·Store the grain as far away from the fields as possible, so that pests do not easily reach the storage sites.
- ·Only store whole and healthy grains.
- ·Regularly check the stored grain.

**Technical Guide**

Currently, there are commercially available plastic multi-layered bags that prevent the entry of water vapor and favor the concentration of carbon dioxide, a component that eliminates pests. This special bag allows for the storage of grains for relatively long periods. The capacity of these bags is greater than 100 pounds (45 kg). The bag is filled with grain, leaving space for closing the bag. The key is to create a seal as airtight as possible to eliminate oxygen, which will eliminate any pests that may be present in the grain. Plastic clips are used for a better seal. The filled bag can be stored in a polypropylene bag. This method is for storing small amounts of grain. It is not recommended to store damp or damaged seeds or grains.

When it is desired to store large quantities of grain (more than 10 quintals), a metal silo may be the best option. A practice that is becoming popular is the use of a small piece of candle (paraffin candle). The practice involves filling the silo, leaving a small space to place a lit candle on a small plate inside the silo. The silo is then sealed. The lit candle will consume the oxygen inside the silo, and with it, the pests that may have been inside will die.

## RODENTS

Rodents are pests that are often overlooked and not given the proper attention. Rats and mice pose a serious problem in the handling and preservation of grains and food products.

Rodents pose a serious threat to human and animal health, as they not only feed on stored grains but also transmit diseases such as leptospirosis.

In some cases, rats and mice can attack crops during the corn harvest, causing significant damage (Figure 15).

Figure 15. Damage caused by rats.

<!-- image -->

From the perspective of handling, storage, and preservation of grains and food, there are three types of rodents that are of great economic importance:

- Norwegian rat (Rattus norvegicus) This is the largest of the three and is very strong. It actively searches for grain in fields and builds nests where the grain is stored.
- Roof rat (Rattus rattus). This rat prefers to climb rather than dig. It can climb concrete walls, cables, and trees. In many areas, this rat is the most damaging to stored grain.
- House mouse (Mus musculus). This is the smallest, weighing approximately 16 grams, with a long tail and a pointed nose, and is a grayish-brown color.

A number of prepared baits are available on the market for rodent control, some more effective than others. While homemade baits can be effective, it is always recommended to follow the precautions and handle them responsibly when preparing them.

## XII. MAIZE DISEASES

Maize is susceptible to various diseases that affect the normal development of plants. These diseases are favored by environmental conditions, soil type, susceptibility of materials, and in the case of viral diseases, by conditions that favor the migration, establishment, and survival of vector insects.

Foliar diseases in maize did not represent a significant economic interest until 2008. However, due to the irregularity of rainfall and the resulting delay in the planting season, the introduction of non-Central American cultivars, and climate change, foliar diseases have become economically important, especially the disease known as "black mold".

Foliar diseases caused by fungi are more common in the later stages of the crop (fruiting period, corn), and are only important when they occur before flowering or very close to it, or when they are of an epidemic nature, as is the case with "black mold".

The following diseases are commonly found in El Salvador.

## COMMON RUST (Puccinia sorghi Schwein)

This disease is widely distributed around the world and becomes more important when plants are approaching flowering. It appears as small, powdery, brown or yellowish pustules, scattered on the leaves. When they sporulate, they turn a reddish-brown and almost black color, both on the upper and lower sides of the leaf. Although it can affect the stem and the husk of the ear, the pustules are eruptive (bursting through) in their final stage and release a reddish-brown powder. The infection usually starts on the lower leaves. This disease is favored by temperatures between 16 and 23°C and high relative humidity.

## TROPICAL RUST (Physophella zeae )

Since 2010, this disease has been more prevalent in the country, although its economic importance has not yet been evaluated. It has been more prevalent in plantings after June 15, in areas between 400 and 600 meters above sea level. It can be associated with "black mold" and be more aggressive, and together they can "burn" the foliage completely. In early stages, a white or yellowish spot with a flat surface is observed. As the infection progresses, white or cream-colored pustules develop, mainly on the upper side of the leaves, and rarely rupture when they sporulate (Figures 17 and 17.1). It is generally found on the lower leaves, but in very susceptible materials, it can reach the upper third of the plant, covering a large area of the foliage. It is favored by warm and humid climates. If it occurs before or during flowering, the damage is significant. It causes premature leaf death and reduces yield.

## CURVULARIA LEAF SPOT (Curvularia sp)

It appears as circular spots of 0.5 cm with a necrotic center and a yellow halo with an oily appearance. This fungus produces necrotic or chlorotic spots, yellow in color, observed throughout the leaf, with a large number of translucent yellow spots (Figure 18). It appears in early stages with high incidence and severity. Its effect on yield has not yet been evaluated.

Figure 18. Leaf spot caused by Curvularia.

## EAR ROT (Helmintosporium spp)

This disease starts with the appearance of small, easily recognizable oval spots, then transforms into elongated necrotic areas of 5 to 10 cm in length, appearing in the first leaves, as well as "black mold" (Figures 19 and 19.1). At the end, the lesions merge and can burn the leaves completely. The disease can cause complete leaf burn. The disease is favored by high environmental humidity and temperatures that fluctuate between 18 and 27°C. The disease is not common during the dry season. It can reduce yield when it occurs during flowering or before, causing losses of approximately 50%. The conidia that develop in the lesions give it a dark gray color and can be dispersed by the wind.

Figure 19. Ear rot caused by Helmintosporium spp.

## FALSE MILDEW (Ustilago virens)

This disease is rarely seen in humid, dry, and warm conditions. The symptoms differ from those caused by other corn diseases, as it does not cause ear deformation or infect the cob, unlike true corn smut (Figure 20). Instead, it forms masses of olive green to black spore clusters on the male flower (ear).

Figure 20. False corn smut.

## COMMON SCAB (Ustilago maydis)

This disease is endemic in all areas where corn is grown. It can affect any part of the plant, and is common in the inflorescences. The fungus develops in the affected tissues, forming variable-sized, green to grayish-green pustules (Figure 21). The interior of these pustules is dark due to a mass of black spores, which are the source of pathogen spread.

Infected plants can be easily observed at the edges of fields. The disease is favored by dry conditions and temperatures between 26 and 34°C.

Figure 21. Common scab.

## WOOLY MILDEW (Various species of Peronosclerospora, Sclerospora, and Sclerophtora)

Several species of the genera Peronosclerospora, Sclerospora, and Sclerophtora cause wooly mildew, which is a serious problem for corn producers in many countries, particularly in warm and humid regions. The symptoms depend largely on the pathogen, the age of the crop, and the environment. Some of these pathogens cause ear deformation (Figure 22), which obstructs pollen production and cob formation. This disease can be transmitted through seeds.

Figure 22. Ear deformation.

## BROWN LEAF SPOT (Physoderma maydis)

This fungus attacks leaves, stems, and sometimes even the husks of the cob. It initially appears as small, round, yellow spots distributed across the leaf surface; the cells of the affected tissue break down, and within them, the fungal reproductive structures (sporangia), which are brown, form. Once released, these sporangia initiate a new infection (Figure 23). The tissue easily breaks down, and in severe cases, the stem can bend at the site of the lesion. The fungus survives in crop residues, and the sporangia germinate in the presence of high soil moisture and temperatures between 23 and 30°C. This disease is more common in areas with high rainfall and temperature.

Figure 23. Brown leaf spot caused by Physoderma maydis.

## ASPHALT MOLD (Phyllachora maydis, Monographella maydis, and Coniothyrium phyllachorae)

This disease is found in relatively humid tropical areas. It is favored by temperatures between 17 and 22°C, with a relative humidity above 75%, and high humidity on the leaves during the night and morning, which facilitates infection and the establishment of the pathogens. The disease begins with Phyllachora maydis, with small, shiny, black, and swollen, oval or circular spots with a diameter of 0.5 to 2 mm (Figures 24, 24.1, and 24.2). If the weather conditions are favorable, the black spots are surrounded by a pale green halo, which is already necrotic tissue. Monographella maydis is responsible for the most damage, causing the foliage to appear burned. Monographella maydis can appear two to three days after P. maydis.

Figure 24. Asphalt mold.

P. maydis is a obligate parasite that cannot survive in dead tissue (stubble), and requires living tissue to develop. While Monographella maydis can survive for three months or more, but requires the presence of P. maydis to be pathogenic.

A third fungus, Coniothyrium phyllachorae, is also associated with the asphalt mold complex. Its role in the disease is not fully understood. The ears affected by the disease lose weight, and the kernels become shrunken and loose.

Figure 24.1. Combination of Phyllachora maydis and Monographella maydis.

Figure 24.2. Cob affected by asphalt mold.

## CORN DILL

(Pythium aphanidermatum)

Species of the genus Pythium cause stalk and seed rot; while other species cause rot in seedlings. Initially, the lower nodes become softened and discolored, taking on an aqueous appearance, which weakens the plant and causes lodging (Figure 25). The disease can affect plants before flowering, and they remain alive until the vascular tissue is destroyed.

Figure 25. Corn stalk rot caused by Pythium.

## CORN WILT (Fusarium verticillioides or Gibberella fujikuroi)

This is a disease transmitted by corn earworms (Helicoverpa zea), and the symptoms manifest as the name suggests, with wilting and stunting of the plant, due to shortening of the internodes, excessive branching of the roots, and proliferation of sterile ears, which reduces production and, in severe cases, kills the plant (Figure 26). Control for this disease is addressed in the section on corn pests.

## DISEASES OF THE COB

Other important problems during the post-harvest period are fungi, which contribute to the heating and decomposition of the kernels, due to the metabolism of these microorganisms that grow and reproduce when the environmental factors are favorable, especially temperature and relative humidity.

Fungi begin to appear when the relative humidity reaches 65%, and initially appear as dead or poorly viable kernels, or as live kernels with broken husks.

The fungi produce enzymes that break down the carbohydrates, fats, and proteins of the kernel, and deteriorate its quality. The acidity of the kernels in these conditions increases, and the germination capacity decreases, either slowly or rapidly.

Among the most important fungi in stored grains are the so-called Fusarium moniliforme (Diplodia sp., Aspergillus sp., Penicillium sp., and Rhizopus sp.).

These fungi are capable of invading and causing infections in the field and during storage; they require a relative humidity of 65 to 90% and a high moisture content in the grain (24 to 25%) to be able to grow.

## XIII. HARVEST

This activity should be carried out when the corn reaches physiological maturity; a good indicator of this stage is the presence of the black layer of the grain at the point of attachment of the grain to the cob (Figure 30). It is at this time that the quality of the grain is at its peak, and from then on it tends to decrease at a rate that depends on how it is handled. In our area, the farmer bends the corn plant to reduce the moisture content of the grain, bringing it to moisture levels that allow for threshing and storage without compromising the quality of the grain. The date for carrying out this practice can vary depending on the climatic conditions of each area, the crop cycle, and whether the farmer is carrying out a crop rotation. Generally, the bending can be carried out between 110 and 115 days of the crop.

The harvest should be carried out as soon as possible after physiological maturity, to avoid losses due to fungal decay; infestation by pests (weevils, termites, etc.), or any other factor that harms production. It is very common for the harvest to be carried out with a sickle, followed by threshing with a combine harvester, to take advantage of the sickle; the disadvantage of this practice is that damaged (rotten) cobs are threshed along with healthy cobs, which then mix with the grain, resulting in a deterioration in the quality of the grain.

After the harvest, the corn can be dried in the cob before threshing, or if the cob has a moisture content that allows for threshing without damage, it can be threshed and then dried. The drying can be carried out on concrete patios, under tarps, with black plastic, etc. There are also artificial dryers.

## XIV. POST-HARVEST HANDLING

Post-harvest handling of corn is very important to maintain good quality, both for family consumption and for commercialization.

Worldwide, grain losses in storage are estimated to be around 10% of the total production. In El Salvador, losses are estimated to be around 10 to 25%, due to the attack of various pests (insects, fungi, rodents); in addition, there are other factors that cause losses in the post-harvest phase of corn, including: excessive moisture, impurities, and high temperatures, which, due to lack of knowledge, are not handled properly.

## IMPORTANCE OF CLEANING THE GRAIN

Maintaining the grain clean is important because it does not deteriorate or heat up as quickly, and insects are less likely to reproduce.

## IMPORTANCE OF DRYING THE GRAIN

Drying the grain after harvest is important because it prevents overheating, reduces respiration, reduces fungal growth, and reduces the risk of grain germination in the storage facility.

## PRACTICES FOR GRAIN CONSERVATION

Some recommendations for the good conservation of the grain include:

- Do not break the grain during harvest.
- Separate damaged grain.
- Dry the grain well: Good drying makes the grain more resistant to the attack of insects or fungi; to achieve good drying, the grain should be placed on dry surfaces and in the sun. It is not advisable to dry it on the ground, as it can easily become wet. The desired moisture content for storage is between 10% and 12%.
- Use covered sheds, silos, or other storage facilities.

## TYPES OF DAMAGE CAUSED BY INSECTS TO THE GRAINS

Direct: This refers to the destruction of grain by the insect when it feeds on it, by laying eggs, excrement, or by the insects themselves, which contaminates the grain, making it dusty, dirty, and unsuitable for human consumption.

Indirect: This refers to the heating of the grain caused by the metabolism of the insects, which causes a bad smell due to the development of microorganisms.
