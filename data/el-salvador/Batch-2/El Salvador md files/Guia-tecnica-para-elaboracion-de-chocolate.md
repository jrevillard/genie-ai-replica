## INTRODUCTION

This guide has been created with the aim of understanding the different ways to process cocoa into products beyond the traditional block. The text was written by the Agroindustry Program at CENTA, based on information collected, experience from processors, and our own, with the support of people from various institutions.

In El Salvador, chocolate in blocks is likely the most accessible and popular cocoa product, and many people dedicate themselves to its production, either manually or using automated methods. However, to stand out in the chocolate market in the country, it is important to innovate processes and produce more advanced products, aimed at more demanding consumers. Also, to increase production capacity or reduce costs, it is advisable to take advantage of the available technologies.

The main suggestion of this guide is to eliminate water from the ingredients of chocolate. The practice of grinding with water in the traditional block-making process reduces its shelf life and appearance. For making chocolate bars, coatings, or truffles, it is not necessary to add water; on the contrary, it is important to avoid moisture entering the process.

This guide is primarily an introduction to semi-automated cocoa processing, carried out in El Salvador, with the purpose of sharing basic information about chocolate production at an intermediate level, under local conditions.

White Chocolate

It is made with cocoa butter (at least 20%), milk powder, and sugar. Strictly speaking, it is not chocolate.

The taste of chocolate and its texture (how it feels in the mouth) depend on the processing, the formulation, and the type of cocoa used. Like almost all agricultural products, there are many varieties of cocoa that differ in their taste, color, aroma, and chemical composition.

The cocoa beans are the seeds of the Theobroma cacao tree. Each seed consists of two cotyledons and a small embryo of the plant, all covered by the skin (shell). 30 to 50% of the dry weight of the bean is fat, known as cocoa butter. The amount of fat and its properties, such as its melting point and hardness, depend on the cocoa variety and the environmental conditions in which it grew. Similarly, the content of proteins and the stimulating components that are appreciated in chocolate: theobromine and caffeine.

<!-- image -->

We distinguish three main groups of cocoa, with different characteristics:

## Forastero

This is a group of varieties from the Amazon rainforest. They have a high fat content, are bitter and dark due to components called polyphenols. They give a strong chocolate flavor, so they can be used in low percentages. This type of cocoa is used to obtain cocoa butter.

They are of Mesoamerican origin, less bitter, so they are distinguished by special aromas such as nuts, fruits, flowers, etc. They are less dark, with some varieties having seeds that are even white, and the flavors are more subtle and delicate.

Criollo. These are of Mesoamerican origin, less bitter, so they are distinguished by special aromas such as nuts, fruits, flowers, etc. They are less dark, with some varieties having seeds that are even white, and the flavors are more subtle and delicate.

Trinitario. These are a cross between the previous varieties and have intermediate characteristics in terms of flavor and aroma. Because they are less bitter than Forastero, Trinitario can be used in high percentages of the final chocolate.

The seeds undergo fermentation through the action of microorganisms such as yeasts and bacteria in a process called fermentation, which causes various chemical changes both in the pulp surrounding them and within themselves. These changes initiate the development of chocolate flavor during the process and the change in color of the seeds. There is also unfermented cocoa, which is washed to clean the seed from the pulp (or mucilage) that surrounds it. There are ways to recognize this type of cocoa, which is mainly used for products with medicinal properties and for the bar.

The seeds are dried and sent to the processing plant to be arranged as raw material for processing.

Due to the genetic differences of the plants, the conditions in which they have been cultivated, and the methods used in the fermentation and drying processes, the flavors vary considerably. Therefore, experts in the field have designed a graphical representation to represent them. Circles with the flavors around, with varying levels of detail. To determine the flavor of a batch of cocoa, it is roasted and ground to obtain a liquid mass (paste), which is tasted by one or more tasters, similar to how coffee is tasted. In addition to the normal flavors and smells, cocoa can acquire undesirable smells or tastes such as moldy, earthy, metallic, smoky, or other products.

When purchasing cocoa, it is important to be aware that there is a wide variety and to verify the characteristics before purchasing. Once purchased, the use and process should be adjusted to the quality of the cocoa. It is said that: "There is no bad-tasting cocoa, all types of cocoa have a specific use. However, there is cocoa that has been poorly fermented or poorly dried, and these are quality defects."

Currently, there is growing interest in additional cocoa characteristics, such as its origin (where it was grown), agricultural practices, and hygiene measures (organic cocoa and its safety), as well as the characteristics of the companies or organizations that sell it (working conditions, the type of organization, and its social responsibility). Therefore, there is a trend towards certifying the quality of cocoa and its suppliers, as well as demanding this type of qualification from buyers.

Another trend is the research and emphasis on medicinal properties that certain components of cocoa have, such as polyphenols, which have antioxidant capabilities. Therefore, cocoa is considered a functional food; in addition to nutrients, it also provides health benefits to consumers. This can provide a double benefit to chocolate and is an important advertising and commercial argument. However, these functional ingredients may be lost or degraded during the process, in preparation, or during digestion, so it is important to handle the topic with sufficient knowledge and caution.

The most appreciated ingredient in cocoa is probably theobromine, which, similar to caffeine (which cocoa also contains), generates a feeling of joy and satisfaction, and also stimulates and encourages consumption, similar to what happens with coffee in most people. This benefit has been exploited since the beginning of its human consumption to extend the physical and mental capabilities of warriors, priests, and rulers who had privileged access to cocoa. With the popularization, and increased production and availability, chocolate has become a natural, tasty stimulant appreciated in most Western countries. The content of theobromine and caffeine in chocolate depends on the type of cocoa and the proportion used. Unlike other active molecules, theobromine is more stable, so it is quite safe to generate the desired effect when consuming chocolate. Whether this can cause any type of physiological or mental addiction is another matter. From a commercial point of view, this is important, as it motivates the purchase, just as it does with coffee.

## Raw materials, ingredients, and additives

Cocoa and sugar are the main ingredients of chocolate. The higher the percentage of cocoa, the better the chocolate; however, to use high percentages of cocoa, it must be of very good quality, because any unpleasant or undesirable taste or smell will be easily noticeable. When the percentage of cocoa is low, its quality is not as critical.

Depending on the cocoa content in the mixture, there is chocolate with almost no cocoa up to slightly less than 100% cocoa. Often, the percentage of cocoa is advertised on the packaging for commercial purposes.

## It is important to consider that:

In the artisanal chocolate production, the tablet, water is added to facilitate grinding and to obtain a moldable paste at room temperature. As the amount of water is reduced, the tablet will have a longer shelf life, because fungi will have less opportunity to grow on the product. However, for the production of other types of chocolate, water should not be added. Even a small amount of water in the paste will make it sticky and difficult to handle, and it will not melt easily, so it should be worked as dry as possible.

To give a special flavor, you can add cinnamon, vanilla, salt, nutmeg, pepper, and others such as nuts, peanuts, dried fruits, etc.

Chocolate bars and coatings contain cocoa butter. True chocolate does not contain vegetable fat or cocoa butter.

"Commercial" cocoa varies greatly in flavor and quality, so it is important to buy dry cocoa, well fermented, with uniform husk, size, and color, without mold or strange odors, without being broken or chipped, and without garbage. Depending on the final product, a cocoa with a fine aroma or, on the contrary, a bitter cocoa is preferred.

When roasting and removing the husk of the cocoa, this source of contamination and bacterial load is eliminated or reduced. Normally, there is no other step after roasting that controls microorganisms, so it is important to avoid contamination. Sugar, if from the factory and well packaged, normally does not represent a source of contamination.

The same applies to the other ingredients, which must meet the requirements for human consumption, and be processed with good manufacturing practices.

## Receiving cocoa, analysis and quality control

The quality of the raw material is something that requires a lot of attention, especially with cocoa, due to the wide variety of flavors and other characteristics it presents. Each batch should be known for its homogeneity, physical aspects such as moisture, size, cleanliness, and obviously, what concerns the flavor, such as the type of cocoa, its sensory potential, how it has been fermented, and whether it has any defects.

When receiving, it is advisable to immediately identify the batch, for example, marked on the bags or with a label. In addition, it is necessary to record its origin, date of entry into the warehouse, the quantity, and the characteristics declared, such as the harvest month, and subsequently the results of the quality analysis. To take samples, there are special tools and methods, which are not always available. The essential thing is to obtain a representative sample and to determine the relevant characteristics. Common sense and experience help a lot, as well as a system for managing the data, for example, cards, a book, or something digital. It will also be necessary to have a place with some equipment and utensils for determining the quality, and a person dedicated to performing this task.

<!-- image -->

In the chocolate industry, it is common to mix different batches of cocoa beans to "match" the final product's flavor. Even on a small scale, it is important to prevent variations in the raw material from significantly affecting the product's flavor, as consumers generally don't like something to taste different from what they expect. Mixing and gradual changes are a good solution to variability in raw materials if done in an organized and documented way, so that the processes can be repeated or corrected.

Another reason for combining different types of cocoa is to expand the range of flavors and aromas found in the final product. "Single-origin" chocolate, made from cocoa beans from a single location, is sold. However, most chocolates are made from blends of different types of cocoa beans, as they create more "complex" sensations in the mouth.

Something very important in a food product is its traceability, which refers to being able to know where the ingredients used come from. To do this, it is necessary to keep a record of what enters the process, either by batch or by day, which requires having the raw materials clearly identified in the warehouse.

When looking for ingredients for a specific process, it is obviously preferred to find the most suitable ones in terms of flavor or content. In addition, there are other criteria to consider, such as: their cost, the turnover of products in the warehouse, the available quantity, their shelf life, and general planning criteria. An interesting detail is that if the cocoa is freshly fermented, it is not recommended to process it immediately, but to store it for one or two months.

For all of this, the handling of raw materials is very important, and care for them should start from the beginning. Good practices in the warehouse include the use of platforms, appropriate packaging (to prevent moisture from entering the cocoa), ventilation, pest and insect control, and general cleaning and hygiene.

## PRE-GRINDING OF INGREDIENTS

To avoid grinding, it is recommended to pre-crush the cocoa beans or nibs to a size of about 2 millimeters. This can be done in a nixtamal mill with loose discs or in other equipment with knives, hammers, or rollers.

Similarly, it is recommended to grind the sugar to obtain a "glas" type powder in a knife mill (like a blender), disc, or hammer mill, to facilitate the subsequent grinding and refining process. Glas sugar is sometimes available and may contain a small amount of starch, which can interfere with the process.

## Grinding of cocoa

Grinding reduces the size of the particles to obtain a liquid cocoa mass, called paste, mass, or "liquid."

For chocolate bars, the paste is obtained in a single pass with all the ingredients in the nixtamal mill; however, for finer chocolate, it is necessary to grind the cocoa first and then add the other ingredients in subsequent passes.

<!-- image -->

The use of special discs allows cocoa to be ground without adding water, to obtain cocoa mass or paste. Water is the enemy of chocolate, so the cocoa must be well dried and contact with water should be avoided. The challenge of grinding with discs is to prevent the mass from overheating (no more than 50 °C). Therefore, it should be fed in small batches, use appropriate discs, and not compress them too much. There are also special hammer or pin mills for cocoa, which are necessary for larger quantities.

Based on the amount of ground cocoa, sugar is added according to the recipe (20, 40, 60% etc.). Other ingredients are also added. The mixture is made in the same mill or in special mixing or chocolate-making equipment.

## Refining

To further reduce the size of the cocoa and sugar particles, special equipment is used, typically with stones or balls, in a batch process that can take hours or even days. This results in a chocolate that is so fine that it dissolves on the tongue, with particles smaller than 1 micrometer (30 micrometers). There are also small-scale equipment available.

To check if it's sufficiently fine, you can test it by tasting or between your fingers in a liquid state. You can also use a conventional micrometer, diluting the liquor with a little oil if it tends to solidify.

During refining, changes occur in the flavor due to the slow evaporation of certain volatile components, so the process takes from hours to two or several days. ## Tempering (also known as tempering or tempering)

Tempering is a thermal process that increases the stability of the cocoa butter when it solidifies, which improves the shine and prevents the subsequent appearance of sugar or fat crystals on the surface of the chocolate, known as "blooming."

The basis of tempering is that when hot cocoa butter cools, the molecules pass from the liquid state to a solid state, organizing themselves into crystals. This process is done in different ways, depending on the temperature at which it occurs. The desired form is crystals that melt at a higher temperature. By using a thermal treatment (a temperature curve) and/or by adding (seeding) crystals of the desired shape and a growth period, it is possible to ensure that most of the crystals, when the chocolate solidifies, will be of the desired shape and that the chocolate has a good temper. Obviously, when melting and solidifying again, the chocolate loses its temper and its desired characteristics, unless appropriate methods are used.

There are different manual tempering techniques; there are also a large number of machines that work with different methods to carry out this operation, continuous, by seeding, by thermal difference, etc.

On the Callebaut-TV website, there are instructional videos with different devices and methods.

## TECHNICAL GUIDE FOR

The "making chocolate from scratch" method says: pour the liquid chocolate into a metal bowl, and from this pour approximately one-third into another metal bowl. In the last, stir the chocolate with a spoon until it starts to form balls, then pour this back into the other two-thirds and stir everything until the mass is smooth again. Then pour the chocolate into a silicone or plastic sheet, on a marble table or into molds.

## Molding, filling or shaping

To give chocolate an attractive presentation and combine flavors, you start with tempered chocolate before it cools. There are molds, usually made of hard plastic, that allow you to make figures and fill the chocolate with other foods. It is always important to handle the chocolate at the correct temperatures to avoid losing its temper. Molds can be filled with a piping bag or by pouring over and cleaning up excess with a spatula. Molds are cleaned without water. Other tools used are a microwave oven, a vibrating table, and a refrigerator at 12°C, where a "thermal shock" is applied to be able to demold, and another at 18°C to rest the final product.

**Consumer Expectations, Quality, and Standards**

Consumers are increasingly seeking artisanal chocolate bars with higher cocoa content, less sugar, longer shelf life, and hygienic handling to ensure their safety.

For the registration of chocolate products, the Ministry of Health (MINSAL) has specific requirements, such as the analysis of artificial colorants, fatty acid profile, and the presence of *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Salmonella sp.* More information can be found at http://usam.Salud.Gob.Sv/archivos/pdf/alimentos/determinaciones\_analiticas.Pdf or by calling 2205 1611 or 34.

According to the CODEX standard (CODEX STAN 87-1981, rev.1-2003), chocolate bars are classified as "sweet chocolate" if they contain at least 30% cocoa by weight. If they contain less than 30%, they are classified as "table chocolate" and must contain a minimum of 20% cocoa, at least 11% cocoa butter, and may contain sugar particles larger than 70 micrometers.

**Requirements for Sanitary Permits and Registrations**

The formal market requires that products have a Sanitary Registration number from MINSAL, and there are several requirements to obtain this registration. Information on the process can be found on the MINSAL website, and you can inquire with the nearest health unit about the specific requirements regarding sanitation and hygiene. Consulting before starting to build or modify spaces or install equipment will help avoid unnecessary modifications or improvements that the inspector may require if they find conditions that do not comply with the applicable regulations. In addition to the sanitation of the facilities and equipment, the application of Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) and the maintenance of certain records, analyses, and checks of raw materials, hygiene conditions, and operators will be required. ## Economic Aspects

Any productive activity intended for sale must be planned in technical and economic terms, in order to project the costs, revenues, and potential profits that will result from implementing the plan.

This manual does not aim to delve deeply into this topic, however, it would be incorrect to explain the processes without emphasizing the importance of the economic aspect.

Unless the cocoa processing is for self-consumption or for educational purposes, if it does not generate profits, the activity will not be sustainable, and it will not be possible to recover the initial investment.

When selling chocolate, it is important to consider that the competition from imported and domestic products is quite strong, so the price, quality, and sales strategies are very important to ensure a sufficiently large sales volume to cover expenses.

It is important to consider the projected sales volume when selecting equipment, so that it is used efficiently, i.e., not left idle. It is also important to consider the effects of scale, so that it is usually much cheaper to process in larger batches, so the best option is to invest in slightly larger equipment than strictly necessary. This will also allow for growth in the future, which is another objective of almost any business.

## General Conclusions Based on the Examples

The total cost of a chocolate bar produced and sold on a small scale, as described, is comparable to the cost of a commercial chocolate bar purchased in a supermarket, in an attractive package and with all the promotion that large brands offer.

To be competitive, it will be necessary to minimize costs and work as efficiently as possible; and on the commercialization side, to differentiate the products and add value in a way that large companies cannot.

The effects of scale are significant and are evident in the cost per pound. More than all the costs in labor, depreciation of equipment and energy, they decrease considerably as the production volume increases.

It is expected that the costs of sale per pound will also be lower if more is sold, due to the lower cost per pound in distribution, promotion and commercialization.
