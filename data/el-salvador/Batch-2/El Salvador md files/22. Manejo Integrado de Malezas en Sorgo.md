Sorghum for grain is one of the most important and extensive crops in Mexico and throughout Latin America, due to its hardiness and wide adaptability to a wide range of environments. One of the main factors limiting sorghum production is poor weed control, both of broadleaf or dicotyledonous weeds and of grasses or sedges. The first step in designing integrated weed management programs is to correctly identify the species associated with sorghum. Integrated weed management in sorghum should consider the application of appropriate control practices to avoid direct damage from weed competition for light, water, and nutrients. In sorghum, the critical period of weed competition has been identified as the first 30 to 35 days of development for most hybrid varieties. A study conducted in Tamaulipas, Mexico, showed a reduction in grain yield of 20, 39, and 57% when wild sunflower competition with sorghum was allowed during the first 4, 6, and 8 weeks of crop development, respectively, with a maximum reduction of 79% in grain yield after 12 weeks. Meanwhile, sorghum yield increased significantly as the period without competition from wild sunflower increased, and it required a period of 25 days without competition from this weed to achieve a yield similar to that obtained in the treatment without competition.

The basis of integrated weed management is cultural practices that make sorghum more competitive against weeds, such as: wet planting, using narrow rows, planting date and density, using "starter" fertilizers at planting, and crop rotation. Achieving a uniform and vigorous sorghum field from its early stages will significantly reduce weed establishment. In addition, producers typically perform 2 to 3 hoeings to control weeds associated with sorghum, which are complemented by the application of herbicides and, in some cases, manual weeding.

Atrazine, a herbicide dating back to the 1960s, is the most widely used herbicide for pre-emergence (PRE) control of sorghum and weeds. It is common to apply atrazine at the time of planting to prevent the establishment of weeds and their early competition. The use of atrazine at a rate of 1.0 kg ai/ha is very effective in plots with a history of heavy infestations of annual broadleaf weeds. However, it is important to be careful with the dose applied to sandy soils and with less than 1% organic matter, as heavy rainfall can cause atrazine to leach and damage sorghum. When dealing with plots with problems of grassy weeds, it is advisable to mix atrazine with acetochlor and s-metolachlor herbicides, which have greater action on annual grassy weeds. The use of these herbicides requires that the sorghum seed be treated with the fluxofenim protectant, which inhibits their toxic effects on sorghum by increasing the action of the enzymes that metabolize these herbicides.

Hormonal herbicides, primarily 2,4-D and dicamba, are the most commonly used for selective control of annual and perennial broadleaf weeds in post-emergence (POST). These herbicides can be applied selectively to sorghum at a development stage of 3 to 6 leaf ligules (V3 to V6), as using them outside this application window can cause damage to the crop. Bentazon and carfentrazone are POST contact herbicides that have effective control of some broadleaf weeds. Prosulfuron is a POST herbicide from the sulfonylurea family that has good control of weeds from the Asteraceae family, such as polocote, chayotillo, and bitter weed, and is characterized by its low application rate, short persistence, and low volatility, which minimizes the possibility of damage to non-target crops. The appropriate use of herbicides should include the appropriate equipment, good quality water, correct calibration of the sprayer, the use of adjuvants when indicated on the herbicide label, the use of personal protective equipment for those handling the application, and proper disposal of herbicide containers.
