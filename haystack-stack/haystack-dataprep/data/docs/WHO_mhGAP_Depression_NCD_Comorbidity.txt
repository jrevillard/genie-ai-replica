WHO mhGAP — Depression Assessment and Management in Primary Care
Source: WHO Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) Intervention Guide, Version 2.0
Reference: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240084278
Focus: Depression in patients with non-communicable diseases (diabetes, hypertension, CVD)

1. ASSESSMENT — HOW TO IDENTIFY DEPRESSION

Screening questions (ask ALL patients with chronic diseases):
- "During the last 2 weeks, have you been feeling down, depressed, or hopeless?"
- "During the last 2 weeks, have you had little interest or pleasure in doing things?"
If YES to either → full assessment

Diagnostic criteria (at least 2 weeks duration):
- Persistent depressed mood (sadness, emptiness, hopelessness) — most of the day, nearly every day
- Loss of interest or pleasure in activities previously enjoyed
- Plus several of: fatigue, poor concentration, feelings of worthlessness or guilt, sleep disturbance (insomnia or oversleeping), appetite/weight change, psychomotor agitation or slowing, recurrent thoughts of death or suicide
- Causes significant difficulty in daily functioning (work, household, relationships)

Differentiate from:
- Normal grief or sadness (time-limited, doesn't impair all areas of functioning)
- Adjustment disorder (symptoms follow a specific stressor, resolve within 6 months)
- Bipolar disorder (history of manic episodes — elevated mood, decreased sleep, increased energy, risky behavior)
- Medical causes: hypothyroidism, anemia, medication side effects (some BP medications, steroids)

2. SEVERITY CLASSIFICATION

MILD depression:
- Few symptoms beyond the minimum required
- Mild functional impairment
- Can still manage daily tasks with effort
- Treatment: psychosocial interventions FIRST, no medication initially

MODERATE depression:
- Several symptoms present
- Considerable difficulty in daily functioning
- Treatment: psychosocial interventions + consider antidepressant medication

SEVERE depression:
- Most symptoms present, including some at marked intensity
- Major functional impairment (cannot work, care for self, or maintain relationships)
- May have psychotic features (hallucinations, delusions)
- Treatment: antidepressant medication + psychosocial interventions + consider specialist referral

3. PSYCHOSOCIAL INTERVENTIONS (FIRST-LINE FOR ALL SEVERITIES)

Key psychoeducation messages:
- Depression is a common illness — it does NOT mean weakness or laziness
- Negative thoughts (worthlessness, hopelessness, guilt) are SYMPTOMS of the illness, not reality
- Recovery IS possible with proper support and treatment
- If having thoughts of suicide — tell someone immediately

Practical interventions:
- Identify and address social stressors (financial, relationship, health, loss)
- Reactivate social connections: encourage patient to reconnect with family, friends, community
- Resume previously pleasurable activities: even small ones (walking, visiting, cooking)
- Maintain regular sleep/wake schedule: go to bed and wake at same time daily
- Increase physical activity: 30 minutes walking daily improves mood significantly
- Eat regularly despite appetite changes: even small meals on schedule
- Engage with trusted people: family, CHW, Imam, Alkalo — social isolation worsens depression
- Participate in community activities: Bantaba gatherings, prayer groups, women's kafos

Problem-solving approach:
1. Select ONE problem that is bothering the patient most
2. List possible solutions together (even imperfect ones)
3. Choose ONE solution to try this week
4. Review next visit: did it help? Adjust if needed
5. Move to next problem when ready

4. PHARMACOLOGICAL TREATMENT

WHEN TO START MEDICATION:
- When psychosocial interventions alone are insufficient after 4-6 weeks
- For moderate-severe depression from the start (alongside psychosocial)
- When functional impairment is severe
- When patient specifically requests medication

FLUOXETINE (SSRI — first choice in primary care):
- Starting dose: 10 mg daily for 1 week
- Then increase to 20 mg daily
- If no response after 6 weeks at 20 mg: increase to 40 mg daily
- Maximum dose: 80 mg daily (adults), 40 mg (elderly and adolescents)
- Elderly starting dose: 10 mg, titrate to 20 mg maximum 40 mg
- Adolescents (12+): 10 mg, increase to 20 mg if no response in 6 weeks, max 40 mg
- Take in the MORNING (can cause insomnia if taken at night)
- Takes 2-4 weeks to start working — patient must understand this
- Continue for AT LEAST 9-12 months after symptoms resolve
- Common side effects: nausea, headache, insomnia, restlessness, sexual dysfunction
- Serious: bleeding risk increased with aspirin/NSAIDs, hyponatremia in elderly
- Available on WHO Essential Medicines List — should be available at health centres

AMITRIPTYLINE (TCA — second choice, use with caution):
- Starting dose: 25 mg at bedtime
- Increase by 25-50 mg each week
- Target dose: 75-150 mg daily (minimum effective 75 mg in adults)
- Maximum: 300 mg daily (adults), 100 mg (elderly)
- Take at BEDTIME (causes sedation — can help insomnia)
- CONTRAINDICATED in: cardiac disease, seizure history, bipolar disorder, children/adolescents
- DO NOT prescribe to patients with cardiovascular disease (arrhythmia risk)
- DO NOT prescribe to suicidal patients (overdose can be FATAL)
- Side effects: sedation, dry mouth, constipation, urinary difficulty, blurred vision, weight gain, orthostatic hypotension
- Serious: ECG changes, cardiac arrhythmia, seizures
- Avoid in elderly (falls risk from hypotension and sedation)

IMPORTANT MEDICATION RULES:
- Start with ONE medication at the LOWEST dose
- Never combine two antidepressants (serotonin syndrome risk)
- Never stop suddenly — taper gradually over 4 weeks when discontinuing
- If mania develops (elevated mood, racing thoughts, decreased sleep): STOP antidepressant immediately — may indicate bipolar disorder
- SSRIs may temporarily increase suicidal thoughts in adolescents/young adults — monitor weekly for first 4 weeks

5. SUICIDE RISK ASSESSMENT (assess at EVERY visit)

Ask directly:
- "Are you having thoughts of ending your life?"
- "Have you made any plans to harm yourself?"
- "Have you ever attempted suicide before?"

If YES to any:
- Stay calm, listen without judgment
- Do not leave the patient alone
- Ask about means (pills, rope, pesticide) — remove access if possible
- Involve a trusted family member or friend (with patient's permission)
- If actively suicidal with a plan: refer to hospital IMMEDIATELY
- Do NOT prescribe amitriptyline (lethal in overdose) — use fluoxetine
- Provide limited supply (1 week at a time)
- Schedule follow-up within 1 week

Risk factors for suicide:
- Previous suicide attempt (strongest predictor)
- Current plan or access to means
- Social isolation
- Chronic pain or illness (diabetes, hypertension)
- Substance use (alcohol, drugs)
- Recent loss (bereavement, divorce, job loss)
- Male gender + older age

Protective factors:
- Strong family and community connections
- Religious faith and community (Imam, mosque, church)
- Having dependent children
- Access to mental health care
- Willingness to seek help

6. FOLLOW-UP SCHEDULE

After starting treatment:
- Week 1-2: brief contact (phone, CHW visit) — check adherence, side effects, suicidal thoughts
- Week 4: full assessment — is mood improving? Adjust medication if needed
- Week 6-8: review response — if no improvement on adequate dose, consider increasing or switching
- Monthly thereafter until stable
- After symptom resolution: continue medication for 9-12 months minimum
- When stopping: taper over 4 weeks, not abrupt

At each follow-up assess:
- Current mood (better, same, worse?)
- Sleep, appetite, energy, concentration
- Medication adherence and side effects
- Functional status (can they work, care for family?)
- Suicidal thoughts
- Social support and stressors
- For NCD patients: disease management (are they taking BP/diabetes meds? Checking readings?)

7. WHEN TO REFER TO SPECIALIST

- Severe depression with psychotic features (hallucinations, delusions)
- Active suicidal ideation with plan
- No response to 2 adequate antidepressant trials (6-8 weeks each)
- Suspected bipolar disorder (manic episodes)
- Substance use disorder complicating treatment
- Pregnancy or breastfeeding with moderate-severe depression
- Child or adolescent with depression
- Uncertainty about diagnosis

8. DEPRESSION AND CHRONIC DISEASE (NCD COMORBIDITY)

Depression is common in NCD patients:
- Diabetes: 2-3x higher risk of depression. Depression worsens glucose control (HbA1c rises 0.5-1.0%)
- Hypertension: depression associated with medication non-adherence and worse BP control
- Heart disease: depression doubles risk of cardiac events post-MI
- COPD: depression increases exacerbation frequency
- Cancer: depression common at diagnosis and during treatment

Bidirectional relationship:
- Depression → poor medication adherence → worse NCD outcomes → more depression
- Breaking this cycle requires treating BOTH the depression AND the NCD simultaneously

Practical integration:
- Screen all NCD patients for depression at every visit (2 screening questions above)
- When depression is present, address it alongside NCD management — not separately
- Medication interactions to watch:
  * Fluoxetine: generally safe with diabetes and BP medications
  * Amitriptyline: AVOID with cardiac disease, can worsen orthostatic hypotension (already common with BP meds)
  * Both antidepressants may affect glucose levels — monitor more frequently
- Exercise benefits BOTH depression and NCDs — emphasize physical activity
- Social support (Bantaba, CHW visits, family) helps both conditions
- Align medication schedules: take antidepressant at same time as other daily medications
- CHW role: can monitor both mood and NCD readings during home visits

GAMBIAN CONTEXT:
- Depression often presents as physical complaints (headache, body pain, fatigue) rather than sadness
- Stigma around mental illness is significant — use non-stigmatizing language
- Say "feeling overwhelmed" or "the sickness of the mind" rather than clinical terms
- Imam and prayer support can complement (not replace) medical treatment
- Community support (Bantaba, kafos, family) is a major protective factor
- Fluoxetine should be available at health centres (WHO Essential Medicines List)
- Many Gambian patients attribute depression to spiritual causes — respect this while gently introducing the concept of treatable illness
- CHWs can be trained in basic screening (2 questions) and follow-up
- Suicide is taboo to discuss openly — requires gentle, private, non-judgmental approach
- Seasonal pattern: rainy season isolation in rural areas may worsen depression
