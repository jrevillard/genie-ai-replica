## 1. Introduction

The purpose of this guide is to encourage reflection and analysis on the opportunity for fruit producers to dehydrate part of their harvest. This document presents clear and practical technical and economic information, with the hope that, with this publication and information from other sources, and with specialized advice, producers considering investing in the dehydration of their fruits can make the best decisions.

While this guide focuses on the technical aspects, it is important to start with economic and commercial considerations, as these are the most important when planning and running a business. Investments in the commercial dehydration of fruits are significant and require thorough analysis before starting a project.

There are regions around the world that dedicate a large part of their fruit cultivation to dehydration for export. However, in our country, this activity is not very developed, and the opportunities for local marketing are limited. Precisely because of these conditions, it is important to have a comprehensive perspective and think about how to start with dehydration, knowing the technical aspects, identifying the target market, and ensuring that the economic projections generate positive results.

Typically, it will be necessary to find a solution that is tailored to the local situation and the identified buyers, rather than simply copying successful models from other places. Therefore, the need to choose, decide, and define based on the necessary knowledge of what it entails to produce dehydrated fruit, and to enter the market.

## 2. Benefits and challenges of dehydrating fruits for producers

The term "dehydrated" refers to the reduction of water content in the fruit, a process also known as "drying" or "drying" (in English), which is carried out until the remaining water content in the fruit is no longer sufficient to support the growth of microorganisms such as fungi and bacteria. These can grow on fresh or insufficiently dried fruit, feeding on it and gradually destroying it. Refrigeration can temporarily stop the growth of microorganisms, for periods ranging from weeks to months, depending on the type of fruit. In contrast, dehydrating or drying the fruit to a safe water content allows it to be preserved for an extended period, and the weight and volume are significantly reduced. The benefits of dehydration are summarized in Table 1, as well as the main challenges in doing so for commercial purposes, and some disadvantages of dehydrated fruits.

## 4. Introduction to Dried Fruit Production

With a customer-focused approach, the planning of an economic activity should begin by defining the benefit or value that will be offered to the buyer and consumer, in this case, the person who will consume or use the dried fruit.

What the buyer of dried fruit may require includes:

- Price that aligns with their budget and perceived value
- Quality: presentation, taste, nutrition, safety
- Attractive, convenient, and appropriate packaging
- Volume that meets their needs
- A consistent supply of various products
- Prompt and timely delivery, if applicable
- Novelty, prestige, trend, nostalgia, feelings

Once you understand the desires and needs of customers, and have defined the value you will offer them, you can start thinking about the resources needed to produce and sell dried fruit, of course, if there is sufficient demand in the market visualized, and if the organization can fulfill it with the business.

List of resources to consider for dried fruit production:

- Fruit: Quantity, quality, appropriate timing
- Personnel: For manual operations, machine operators, inspection, administration, sales, maintenance
- Energy: Electricity, fuel, solar
- Raw materials: Water, sugar, additives
- Equipment: To generate the quality and volume needed to sell
- Packaging: That meets its functions and requirements at an acceptable cost
- Storage
- Transportation
- Marketing, advertising
- Buildings, access
- Financial resources

The use of these resources implies costs, which are quantified to estimate the selling price. This is compared to the price in the market or what a satisfied customer would be willing to pay. If the cost is higher, the idea is discarded or modified.

Another important aspect of the business is that it typically forms part of an interdependent chain of actors, being buyers in the previous link and suppliers in the next. A chain works well when there are equitable relationships and cooperation.

Alternatives to a long chain are local sales and direct sales to consumers via the internet.

## Fruit Humidity

To determine and compare the amount of water in the fruit and thus describe the drying process, two indicators of humidity are used:

Moisture content (base) = weight of water x 100 / total weight

Water is distributed throughout the fruit, but it can be represented as if it were separated. Moisture content (base) and moisture content (dry) would be represented as follows (Figure 6).

<!-- image -->

It can be observed that the moisture content (base) does not exceed 100%, and it is lower than the moisture content (dry) for the same product.

Moisture content (dry) has no upper limit and results in a greater difference between high water content, such as in fresh fruit. For example; dehydrated at 90% to 80% (base) seems low, however, in dry base, it is equivalent to a reduction of 900% to 400%, and the total weight is reduced to 50% (see formula on page 63).

For normal communication and commercial purposes, moisture content (base) is used. However, for describing processes, making calculations and predictions of drying speed, moisture content (dry) is also used. There are formulas to convert one to the other; however, the objective here is simply to clarify the terms.

To determine the moisture content of food, laboratory methods are used, in which the food is completely dried and the percentage of moisture is calculated by subtracting the final (dry) weight from the initial (before drying) weight to obtain the amount of water that was in the food, then divided as in one of the two formulas described above.

Standard methods take 24 hours in an oven at 108°C, however, there are also devices that do it in less time to obtain quick drying results in industrial settings.

## Air Humidity

Since air is normally the medium for transporting water in a dehydrator, it is necessary to be able to describe its characteristics.

The concentration of water in the air has a maximum that depends on the temperature: the higher the temperature, the more water it can contain. There are tables and graphs that indicate the maximum for each temperature (Fig. 9, psychrometric chart).

Since there is less water vapor in the air than the maximum, the air is said to have a relative humidity of, for example, 60%, when the amount of water is 60% of the maximum that the air can contain at the given temperature.

Relative humidity is the most practical to measure, for example, with physical or electrical instruments, and can be seen as an indicator of drying power. At the same temperature, air with 30% relative humidity has a greater drying power than air at 50% relative humidity.

The characteristics and behavior of air when heated, cooled, or drying objects in its path can be known through the psychrometric chart, a very useful tool for analyzing air conditions.

## Methods and equipment for drying:

-  The most well-known method is evaporation, where the water present in liquid form leaves the food in gaseous form, such as water vapor, which is invisible.

Evaporation requires heat, as water in vapor form contains more energy than in liquid form. This energy must be supplied from the outside in order for the evaporation to continue. At the same time, the vapor must be removed to prevent the environment from becoming saturated with moisture.

In drying, heat and water are transferred in opposite directions. The heat source can be air heat, radiation, or conduction.

The material absorbs heat, the water evaporates, and the air carries away the vapor.

Figure 10. Evaporation with heat source: air (top),

<!-- image -->

radiation (middle), conduction (bottom)

-  In a solution of sugar or salt (osmosis)

Water has a tendency to move towards areas where there are more elements that attract it, such as salt and sugar in high concentration. Since the concentration of sugar in the fruit is relatively low, between 5 and 15%, it is possible to extract the water from the fruit by placing it in a sugar solution greater than 25% (syrup or sugar).

Figure 11. Representation of water extraction from fruit by osmosis

<!-- image -->

At the same time, the fruit absorbs sugar, making the dehydration process slower, which eventually stops, leaving a significant amount of water inside the fruit. Therefore, osmosis dehydration is used as a first step, before air dehydration (Figure 11).

-  Sublimation under vacuum (lyophilization)

A modern technique is lyophilization, where the fruit pieces are first frozen as quickly as possible to maintain the fruit's cellular structure. Then, it is transferred to a sealed container, from which the air is evacuated by a vacuum pump. The vacuum causes the frozen water to turn into a gas at an acceptable speed, dehydrating the fruit in one or two days, depending on the size (Figure 12).

##  Vacuum frying

In this method, the low pressure reduces the temperature at which water boils, so the product is fried without darkening or changing flavor. Subsequently, the oil is drained. It is used for banana, potato, and coconut chips.

##  Concept: Water and Humidity Balance

Figure 13. Evaporation in a tray

<!-- image -->

The simplest case of evaporation is pure water in a tray. The water evaporates until the tray is completely empty.

<!-- image -->

When drying a wet material, the water is not always on the surface, but inside the material's pores. This can be compared to evaporating water in a tube: initially, the steam easily escapes, but eventually, the steam has to move to the open air, making the drying process more difficult and slower (Figure 14).

Figure 14. Evaporation of water in a tube

The examples presented above describe the evaporation of pure water. However, fruit contains a mixture of water, sugar, starch, proteins, fibers, salt, and other components with an affinity for water. Therefore, the water is not free but is attracted by these other elements, requiring more energy to evaporate.

The ability of water to evaporate from a solution, compared to the ability of pure water to evaporate, is called the water activity (a w) and is expressed on a scale of 0 to 1, or as a percentage if it is to be compared to the relative humidity of the surrounding air.

Figure 1 shows the water activity (a w) at different concentrations of salt and sugar. As the concentration increases, the water activity decreases. The graph illustrates this effect, which is more pronounced for salt than for sugar.

Figure 1. Reduction of a w with increasing concentration

<!-- image -->

When drying a solution, the concentration increases, reducing the water activity (a w). If the relative humidity of the air remains the same, the difference (a w - HR) decreases, making the evaporation process slower. It can even stop when the water activity of the solution reaches the relative humidity of the surrounding air. EVAPORATION

Figure 15. Evaporation from a solution in water

<!-- image -->

This is because when a solution in water or food is exposed to air, and the relative humidity (HR) of the air is equal to the water activity (a w), there is no net evaporation into the air; there is equilibrium. When a w and HR are different, there is a transfer of water, from the higher percentage to the lower percentage.

- a) If HR is lower than a w, water evaporates from the fruit and goes into the air
- b) When HR is equal to a w, there is equilibrium
- c) If HR is higher than a w, water vapor from the air condenses on the fruit

<!-- image -->

Logically, reducing the relative humidity of the air used for drying increases the drying speed, and the equilibrium is reached at a lower a w, resulting in a lower final water content.

Another effect is that evaporation cools the fruit. If the a w of the fruit is 1 or 100% (pure water), the temperature would be that of the wet-bulb temperature, as defined in the psychrometric chart. As the a w decreases, the difference between the fruit's temperature and the air temperature decreases. At the end of the process, the fruit's temperature approaches the air temperature.

When plotting the total weight (container and water) from Figure 13 as a percentage of the initial weight, it is observed that in the case of the tray with pure water, a straight downward line is formed until the water has completely evaporated: then the weight no longer changes, and the line remains horizontal, indicating the weight of the empty container.

In the case of pure water in a tube (Fig. 14), the line curves, because the evaporation process becomes slower over time, as the steam has to escape from inside the tube.

In the case of a solution in a tube (Fig. 15), the weight decreases even more slowly, and the curve stabilizes above the weight of the tube, because there is still solution, as the evaporation stops when the water activity reaches the relative humidity of the surrounding air.

## a. Composition and structure of the fruit

Fruits, as vegetable tissues, are composed of water, carbohydrates, acids, proteins, fat, fiber, and minor elements such as vitamins and minerals. The percentages vary greatly, and can be found in tables* with the composition of fruits, in different states, such as those from the INCAP (Institute of Nutrition of Central America and Panama).

An important aspect is that many fruits contain air inside, in pores and channels between the cells. This is demonstrated by their ability to float in water, having a density less than 1 g/cm 3. These pores influence the drying process, as they create spaces through which water can move.

For water to evaporate, it must pass through pores in the membrane, making the process slower. ## b. Changes during ripening

'Climatic' fruits are those that show significant changes in their chemical composition, texture, and color. Examples include mangoes, papayas, bananas, guavas, and melons, peaches, and plums.

To reduce susceptibility to damage during harvesting and transport, this type of fruit is harvested before it is fully ripe. When the desired flavors and aromas of ripe fruit are desired, it is recommended to apply controlled artificial ripening, in order to synchronize the fruit with its optimal state for the process at the planned time. Completely ripe fruit is not ideal, as the texture becomes softer, the pores fill, and the sugar concentration increases, which makes the dehydration process slower and can cause color changes during drying.

The optimal state depends on the process and the desired result. It is important to define this for the fruit and for the use of the dehydrated fruit, and to be able to control it through harvesting practices, artificial ripening, temperature control, and ethylene concentration during storage prior to processing.

Non-climatic fruits change less and more slowly; however, their proper storage and handling always affect the final quality.

## c. Stages in drying

Combining what is illustrated in graph 3 and figure 16, dehydration proceeds with:

- a) Initial heating of the fruit, with heat absorption and little evaporation
- b) Evaporation of surface water at a temperature close to the bulb temperature
- c) Migration of water through pores between cells and surface evaporation
- d) Migration of water through pores between cells and evaporation between cells
- e) Migration of water intra-cellularly towards pores between cells and evaporation between cells. The temperature of the fruit approaches the drying air temperature.

In practice, two or more phases may occur simultaneously, making it difficult to distinguish them in a graph of water activity or weight during drying. It is logical that the most available water evaporates first, and that during drying, the distance between the source of water and the edge of the fruit increases, as does the concentration of soluble solids. Therefore, at the end of the dehydration, evaporation occurs at a higher temperature, is slower, and requires more heat.

<!-- image -->

In commercial processes, the equilibrium state is not reached, as this would take longer with less dehydration, resulting in reduced efficiency and increased costs without benefit. Once the moisture has been reduced sufficiently, the heat source is turned off and the process is allowed to cool.

## d. Undesirable changes

Defects in the dehydrated product can include:

1. Browning or enzymatic darkening of the fruit
2. Browning due to high temperature
3. Moisture entrapment (case hardening)
4. Change in texture and shape
5. Loss of flavors and aromas
1. Enzymatic browning (due to oxidation)

When the fruit is cut, the cell structure opens, and the components that were previously separated are mixed and exposed to the air. Primarily, polyphenol oxidases are responsible for enzymatic browning reactions that occur during processing and the preservation of fruits. These enzymes catalyze the reaction of phenols with oxygen, forming pigments that have a brown, red, or black color, depending on the fruit. The presence of metal oxides promotes browning.

To minimize the effect, the enzymes can be destroyed by heat (blanching), acidified with citric acid, or by avoiding contact with oxygen, for example, with antioxidants such as sodium bisulfite or ascorbic acid. Also, use sharp stainless steel knives. During drying, the enzymes are deactivated; however, they are not destroyed.

Examples of fruits that are very susceptible to oxidation include bananas, avocados, grapes, peaches, apples, and pears.

## 2. Drying with High Temperatures

Primarily, fruits high in sugar can darken at high temperatures, near a heat source, and when most of the water has evaporated, for example, on the edges and tips of dried fruit. Maillard reactions between proteins and sugars occur slowly at room temperature, but are rapid at higher temperatures. Fruits sensitive to high temperatures include citrus fruits and pineapples. The reaction can continue slowly during storage.

To prevent enzymatic browning, the temperature to which the fruit is dried should be limited, primarily at the end, when the water activity is below 70%.

## 3. Moisture Encapsulation (Case Hardening)

Another reason to limit the air temperature used for drying is that when drying very strongly at the beginning, a hard layer forms around the fruit piece, sealing in the moisture and stopping the drying process. This occurs because strong surface evaporation draws soluble substances from the inside out to the surface, where they concentrate and dry, forming an impermeable film. The problem with this is that the moisture later allows the growth of fungi.

To avoid this phenomenon, do not exceed the temperature and air speed at the beginning, until the surface has dried and the evaporation occurs from the inside. Once a hard layer has formed, it can only be corrected by cutting the pieces in half to create a new surface, free of the layer.

## 4. Change in Texture and Shape

When water is removed from the tissues, the cells lose their pressure, like a balloon deflating. Therefore, dried fruit becomes soft or spongy. Sugar, either added in powder form or through osmotic pre-drying, makes the fruit softer and sometimes sticky. Conversely, fruits low in sugar become hard and brittle at the end of the process. Because the dried fruit is not uniform, the pieces of fruit deform and lose their original appearance.

The method that best preserves the original appearance is freeze-drying, because in this method, the fruit and its shape are frozen before water is removed. For other methods, it is important to control the drying conditions and the final point.

## 5. Loss of Flavors and Aromas

High temperatures can cause the decomposition of certain molecules and the formation of others. Therefore, the fresh fruit flavor is rarely the same in the dried form. Along with the evaporation of water, volatile components such as aromas and some flavors present in fresh fruit are lost.

Only with very advanced techniques is it possible to recover the volatile components and return them to the dried product.

## e. Preservation and Shelf Life of Dried Fruits

Since food is dried to preserve it, it is important to achieve a shelf life of at least six months. To do this, it is important to understand the mechanisms by which dried fruits lose quality over time. By ensuring appropriate conditions from the drying process to the moment of consumption, there should be no quality problems.

## Heat Sources, Distribution, and Efficiency

In the classification of equipment (Table 7), the different energy sources were mentioned. In small equipment and for direct-sun drying, natural convection is sometimes used, without an electric fan. However, for larger installations, it is necessary to actively transport the heat generated to the fruit, and ensure that it is exposed to the drying effect of hot air.

When the fruit pieces to be dried are placed in trays, it is important to avoid overlapping and to rotate or change the position of the trays periodically to obtain uniform drying. An air speed of 0.5 to 2 meters per second is used to draw off the steam.

Good air distribution is essential to achieve fast and even drying. The most commonly used method is a parallel airflow, with a fan running continuously.

To prevent the accumulation of steam in the drying air, a chimney or air outlet is placed in the environment. The same amount of air from the environment enters the system in another place. The fresh air absorbs heat and the ventilation is regulated to minimize energy consumption.

Energy efficiency is expressed as:

energy used in evaporation, or in kg of water evaporated = SMER, total EEV of energy used, kWh of energy used

Energy efficiency depends on several factors such as the equipment, the fruit, the drying conditions and the environment. ## Solar radiation and its capture

The oldest drying methods are solar, and are also currently used, due to environmental and economic considerations, it is advantageous to take advantage of this source. The energy of solar radiation that falls on a surface on the ground is very variable and can reach almost 1 kW per square meter. In cloudy conditions, the radiation from the sky is lower, but is always usable. In addition to being a source of heat for drying, radiation can generate electricity through photovoltaic panels, which allows you to activate a fan or a monitoring or control system.

The disadvantages of using solar energy for dehydration are:

- a) Variable from day to day and during the day, absent at night
- b) Requires a lot of space for collectors
- c) Ultraviolet radiation destroys colors and vitamins present in the fruit

Despite the limitations, it is important to consider the opportunity, perhaps with the necessary measures to minimize its disadvantages, such as:

For periods with little or no solar radiation:

-  Store heat or energy, with a system for later use
-  Have a second source of energy

## To minimize the necessary space:

-  Concentrate and transport the heat to the fruit, in a compact location
-  Utilize free or unused spaces: roofs, facades, parking lots

## To minimize the damage from ultraviolet radiation:

-  Use indirect solar drying systems (in a black box)
-  In direct solar drying, use plastic with UV filter

Solar energy collectors consist of a covered chamber with a translucent material such as glass or plastic, a channel where the air passes, and an absorbent material, typically black in color, which can be solid, perforated, or porous. A layer of insulating material is placed at the bottom to prevent heat loss downwards.

In a basic system, the air moves by natural convection; the hotter air rises, being replaced by cooler air.

In a simple dryer (figure 22), the heated air from the sun passes through the fruit to be dehydrated, and then exits the device through a chimney or opening at the top. The "one-time" use of the air means that part of the captured heat is not used to dry the product, but is released as dry and hot air from the device.

## 7. Importance of the technical aspect for achieving quality

Considering the technical aspect in more detail, the following points are presented, along with possible measures and recommendations to achieve a final product with the best possible acceptance by consumers. "Quality" aims to satisfy the customer and should be specified, as it can be different for each individual. The use of appropriate technologies helps to achieve and improve quality.

For dried fruit, this typically includes the following aspects:

-  Product moisture and uniformity
-  Presentation: color, shape, surface appearance
-  Sensory: taste, aroma
-  Texture
-  Ease of rehydration
-  Safety and limits of microbial contamination, absence of insects
-  Shelf life
-  Hygiene
-  Absence or correct use of preservatives and artificial colors

## Moisture and uniformity of the dried product

For prolonged preservation without the risk of fungal growth, the critical factor is the water activity (a w), which should be below 0.65 (65%). This is a very useful indicator, as it is universal, while the maximum water content based on weight varies for each product and also depends on the sugar content (Brix) and other characteristics of the batch.

Large producers verify water activity as a drying target, rather than reducing weight. There are laboratory and portable equipment, and for samples, it is possible to use a food quality laboratory. In practice, it is important to learn the characteristics of the well-dried product, such as pliability, touch, and texture. By knowing the product well, it can be used as a target or standard the final moisture; however, this is not entirely safe. The final water content is determined by the difference in weight when completely dried (24 hours at 108 °C or with another method).

<!-- image -->

Figure 29. Water activity meter, laboratory and portable

<!-- image -->

Another aspect is that few dehydrators produce products that are completely uniform. There are always areas where the drying process is slower. Larger pieces of fruit can also be more humid when everything else is already ready.

To reduce this inconvenience, it is important to let the moisture of the pieces equilibrate before packaging, by placing them without pressing in a hermetic container or plastic bag, closed, at room temperature. It is not advisable to leave it in the dehydrator, as the product may absorb moisture, and the equipment is more useful for dehydrating. In about five days, the moisture will distribute from the more humid pieces to the drier ones, through the air between them.

If condensation is observed inside the container or bag, it definitely means that the product needs more drying time. If a hygrometer with a probe or sensor on an extension is available, the relative humidity of the air inside the container can be measured, which should be below 65%. (Note: Simple hygrometers quickly lose their calibration. It should be checked if the reading is correct, and if necessary, adjust the reading)

After four days to a week of rest and final observation, the product is packaged, more compact to save space, and in its final packaging.

## Presentation: color, shape, surface appearance

Normally, the consumer cares about both the visual and the taste. During drying, there are always changes, and the final appearance depends on the way the process is carried out and handled. To achieve attractive colors, shapes, and appearance, the following can be done or attempted:

-  Use fruit in optimal maturity, with good color
-  Select raw materials, removing fruit or parts with damage
-  Use sharp knives and stainless steel in peeling and cutting
-  Cut uniformly, to the appropriate size
-  Apply blanching, antioxidants, and acid to fruit susceptible to enzymatic browning
-  Consider the use of appropriate colors
-  Place the pieces well on the mesh; without overlapping
-  Do not expose fruit of a delicate color to UV light from the sun
-  Avoid excessive heat to minimize browning due to excessive heat

## Sensory: taste, aroma

To have good taste, the fruit should be of a suitable variety for drying, with a high concentration of ingredients that impart flavor, such as sugars and acids.

There is dried fruit with added flavorings, for example:

-  Sugar (by osmotic treatment or in dry form)
-  Citric acid or ascorbic acid (vitamin C)
-  Chili spice
-  Other flavors

Consumers can add these additives to their liking, and the additives are declared on the label.

As for aroma, when combined with water, the volatile substances evaporate and are lost. There is not much fresh fruit aroma left, but there should not be any unpleasant odor at the end. Dryers with diesel or biomass burners in poor condition can produce a smoky smell.

## Texture

The sensation when touching or eating the dried fruit is important and depends on the fruit's own consistency and the residual water content, among other factors.

To increase firmness, a treatment with calcium can be applied, through a bath in a solution of calcium chloride (CaCl 2). Calcium forms bridges between the pectin molecules of the cell wall, making it more resistant to deformation.

## Ease of rehydration

In certain uses, consumers need the dried fruit to quickly absorb water when exposed to moisture, for example, in cooking or baking recipes. This characteristic depends on the drying method, with the best results being achieved with freeze-drying, as it maintains the fruit's porosity.

Compliance with the first two criteria is to ensure that consumers will not get sick or suffer any other type of health problem. To prevent this type of risk, good manufacturing practices are applied, as described in the Central American Technical Regulation (RTCA 67.01.33:06 Food and Beverage Industry. Good Manufacturing Practices. General Principles.) and in the standard NTS 67.00.245:13 Code of hygiene practices for dried fruits and vegetables, including edible mushrooms. The regulation that establishes the tolerance is in RTCA 67.04.50:08 Food Microbiological criteria for food safety, subgroup 4.2.

The presence of Escherichia coli is due to deficiencies in human handling during packaging, as there is usually no further thermal treatment. This can be prevented by using gloves and personal hygiene. The origin of Salmonella is in the field, due to lack of hygiene and disinfection in the initial stages of the process or low drying temperature. It can survive in dry environments and has been detected in commercially dried fruits that caused salmonellosis outbreaks.

The presence of insects, their larvae, webs, or excrement is completely unacceptable; however, it is easy to find after storage, when there was a pest infestation of stored products during drying or before packaging. The most common is the Mediterranean fruit moth (Plodia interpunctella), which usually lays its eggs in dry food, and then its larvae feed on the dried fruit and form webs and pupae.

## Shelf life

To allow for transportation and subsequent use, shelf life is an obvious requirement for dried fruit. A minimum of 6 months to 1 year is required; however, adverse conditions such as humidity, high temperatures, or damage from insects or rodents can compromise the quality. Therefore, the indication "store in a dry and cool place" should be on the packaging. There should never be any signs of mold or insects, although slight changes in color, taste, or aroma may occur over time.

The ability to purchase enough for several months of use is important. However, opened packaging does not provide security. It is recommended that consumers use resealable packaging, such as ziplock bags or plastic containers with airtight lids, to prevent the ingress of moisture or insects.

## Hygiene measures

Although not part of food safety, finding a hair, button, piece of paper or plastic, or other foreign object in the dried fruit will disappoint the buyer, and they may not buy again.

Common hygiene measures for personnel in contact with fruit are:

- Frequent hand washing, without rings or jewelry, or long, painted, or acrylic nails
- Use of hairnets, masks, gloves
- Use of aprons, boots, and a bath for washing shoes
- Regular medical examinations for personnel

## For equipment and facilities:

- Cleaning and disinfection before and after use of equipment and utensils
- Pest control (insecticides, rodenticides and traps for capture, screens on doors and windows, plastic curtains)
- Cleaning and disinfection of work surfaces
- Regular cleaning of floors, ceilings, and surroundings

To comply with the requirements, it may be necessary to present personnel training certificates, procedures, the plan, and the record of control measures, both for food safety and personal hygiene.

## Use of preservatives and artificial colors

The additive most commonly used in white or light-colored dried fruit is sulfur dioxide, due to its antioxidant and preservative properties. Several countries have their own regulations for the maximum content. When using it, it must be included in the list of ingredients on the packaging, as some people need to limit their consumption of foods containing sulfites for medical reasons.

When it is necessary to use it, the maximum permitted limit must be respected. The FAO defines it for various food groups in its Codex Stan, and for dried fruit, it is a maximum of 1 gram per kg of dry fruit, measured as SO2. The exceptions are: apricots: 2 g, blanched raisins: 1.5 g, dried coconut: 0.2 g and dried coconut with oil removed: 0.05 g per kg (note 135 in table 17).

## Regarding the final drying point

For the first batches, the necessary time for drying is unknown. Drying tests are performed, and when it is considered that the drying process is nearing completion, samples are taken every hour at different points, until it is certain that the process is complete.

The samples are allowed to cool completely and are observed to determine the number of hours of drying required to reach the final point.

## Criteria or methods:

-  Fold, cut, and squeeze the piece, it should be dry in the center, not sticky
-  Place cut pieces in a clear plastic bag, seal, and leave in the refrigerator: there should be no water droplets inside.
-  Measure water activity if you have the equipment (it should be less than 0.65 for the given product)
-  Determine residual water content with an oven or suitable equipment, and compare the result with the maximum established in shelf life tests, or it should correspond to an aw of less than 0.65 for the given product.

For safety, it is better to dry a little more than to leave the fruit with excess moisture. If it is not possible to measure the humidity accurately, an extra time is added. However, at the end of the drying process, the risk of browning due to heat is higher. To prevent part of the product from darkening due to toasting, the temperature of the thermostat is reduced and the product closest to the heat source is moved or rotated.

## Options to minimize energy consumption and speed up drying

-  Thermal insulation and leak-proof to prevent heat loss
-  Ventilation control; only the necessary amount
-  Heat recovery in a counter-flow exchanger between the air leaving and the air entering the dryer
-  Heat recovery from the hot final product, passing the cooling air to the next batch of cold fruit, to heat it and start the drying process
-  Use of a continuous dryer, counter-flow air
-  Use of equipment with a heat pump
-  Utilize availability of organic combustible material
-  Use of solar energy

## 9. Importance of the technical topic for profitability

In commercial operations, profitability is almost always the main goal, and the use of appropriate technology is a way to increase it. The profit per unit sold is the selling price minus the total unit cost, of which the production cost is a major component (see Figure 6, page 13). The "Guide for Investment Decision-Making in Fruit Processing," available on the CENTA website, presents a methodology for estimating and comparing the profitability of fruit processing options (see link on page 62).

Many aspects influence the cost of the dried fruit, including:

-  The fruit, its variety and state (% pulp yield, % moisture)
-  The chosen process
-  Characteristics of the equipment (capacity, efficiency, cost, maintenance)
-  The cost of energy sources
-  The way the equipment is operated (% of normal load, efficiency, downtime)
-  The cost of labor, which is an important part, with options to make it more efficient, using appropriate tools and equipment
-  The cost of other raw materials and packaging
-  Optimal use of investments: appropriate capacities, without bottlenecks, working long periods and seasons, if possible without closing
-  The scale of operation: Generally, larger operations are more efficient and result in lower production and distribution costs. In addition, each production set has its break-even point, the minimum sales and production quantity to achieve profits. Working with little product is expensive.

Recording the use of raw materials, energy, labor times, and other, allows you to calculate the actual unit cost, to compare with the budgeted cost and with previous batches. Above all, during the first batches or changes in raw materials or processes, it is important to update the cost, to analyze it and to be able to make the best decisions to optimize the process and adjust the selling price if necessary and possible. - Secondary: Groups primary packages: corrugated cardboard box
- Tertiary: Facilitates transportation and storage: pallet

The packaging is chosen and designed based on the consumer and how they will use the product. You can take examples from existing products on the market. Attractive packaging for the final consumer is not cheap, sometimes it costs the same or more than the fruit it contains. For use as an ingredient in cooking or baking, the packaging must be suitable and affordable.

In that sense, it is advisable to buy existing bags and attach a label. Packaging manufacturers can make them to order and with printing; however, their minimum order is several thousand, requiring a significant investment and at least ordering large quantities, it becomes expensive. For secondary packaging, it is also advisable to buy available boxes in small and medium quantities.

For the case of dried fruit, the primary packaging should prevent the entry of moisture and oxygen into the product and protect against punctures, insects, and rodents.

Laminated plastic with a barrier against moisture is preferable, as ordinary plastic has micropores through which steam and gases can pass. Ideally, it should be resealable by the consumer. Sometimes trays or cardboard boxes are used, with a transparent plastic film on top.

The most hygienic and with the greatest guarantee for the buyer is the sealed bag with the minimum amount of air included. More advanced is vacuum packaging or modified atmosphere packaging.

## 11. About designing a plant for drying fruit

Few entrepreneurs can start in facilities built for processing; however, for existing buildings, it is important to know the requirements and recommendations. It is normally a process of continuous improvement, according to the demands of the buyer, the instructions of the health inspector in the operating permit process, and depending on the availability of resources.

## Main points to consider:

- Availability of potable water
- Handwashing sink at the entrance
- Prevention of insect and rodent entry, screens, curtains
- Washable floor with sanitary curves on walls
- Washable walls (oil paint or ceramic)
- Low ceiling or concrete or easy-to-clean sheet roof
- Logical flow of materials, without crossing between raw material and finished product
- Sufficient light and ventilation
- Bathrooms according to the number of workers, separate from the main building or with access only from the outside
- Changing rooms and dining area for employees
- Drainage and proper disposal of wastewater
- Places to store and handle waste
- Appropriate warehouses and cold rooms
- Appropriate access routes

Processors should be familiar with Good Manufacturing Practices. Following these practices helps to avoid the need for subsequent modifications and speeds up the process of obtaining a permit.

## 12. Organizational, economic, logistical, and legal aspects

Every business, no matter how small, has many invisible but essential elements in addition to physical processes and facilities. The focus on the product can sometimes lead to neglecting or ignoring issues that can limit or prevent the proper development of activities and the business.

## For example:

- Organization of personnel (organigram, functions)
- Ability to work in a team
- Knowledge of procedures
- Training of personnel
- Work environment, motivation
- Integration of marketing with production and purchasing
- Legal aspects related to labor, accounting, taxes, etc.
- Favorable relationships with other actors in the supply chain
- Economic situation in agricultural production and among buyers

This guide is not intended to delve deeply into the organizational aspect. However, it is important to mention that the success of a business requires more than just technical ability and knowledge.

## 13. General conclusions

From a technical perspective, dehydration is constantly evolving, with innovations and improvements. This is due to scientific research, the use of new materials and equipment, automation, and advances in processes and distribution logistics. This creates new opportunities for starting a dehydration business, of course, with a good understanding of the technical aspects.

The process is not so complex that it is only suitable for countries with a large agro-industrial capacity. However, the disadvantage is that there are already several large producers competing to maintain or expand their presence in the market. Therefore, it is important to note that it is often easier to produce than to sell.

Despite the technical focus of this guide, it is important to emphasize the importance of conducting a market study first to investigate commercialization opportunities and to define the target market, sales strategies, the value to offer to the buyer, transportation logistics, advertising methods, and other aspects that as a fruit producer are often unknown.

This also leads to another important aspect: the association and organization of fruit producers to gather volume and continuity in the offer, strengthen their negotiating power, and attract investment and support from outside.

With a well-founded commercial and organizational foundation, defining the business plan becomes logical and concrete, rather than a dream or something invented out of thin air. To create a business plan, the primary production aspect and the definition of the processing must be considered, but we should never forget that the technical aspect is part of the plan; it is not the first or the most important.

The humidity is measured in the air above the solution in both bottles (sealing well the neck), and the result is recorded along with the real relative humidity (75% for common salt and 32.5% for magnesium chloride).
