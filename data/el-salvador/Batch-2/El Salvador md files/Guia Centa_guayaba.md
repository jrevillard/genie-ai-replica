## INTRODUCTION.

The production of guava as a large-scale crop in El Salvador is relatively recent, as the Technical Mission from Taiwan introduced the Taiwanese Variety 1, and, along with the Fruit Program of CENTA, encouraged visionary producers, training them in agronomic management and fruit processing, and then providing them with high-quality genetic material and credit to start planting. The first reports of the cultivation of Taiwanese guava in the country date back to 1998. To date, around 125 hectares are cultivated, mainly along the Salvadoran coast, from Chutilla in La Libertad to Puerto Parada in Usulután, in low-lying areas such as San Julián and Caluco in Sonsonate, Nueva Concepción in Chalatenango, and San Miguel; in intermediate areas such as Zapotitán, Chalchuapa, Santa Isabel Ishuatán, San Pedro Puxtla, and Cojutepeque. Due to the support provided by CENTA and the Technical Mission from Taiwan, there is currently a high demand for information on experiences in El Salvador regarding pests, diseases, and other issues related to the cultivation.

## I GENERAL INFORMATION.

## 1.1 ORIGIN AND DISTRIBUTION.

A plant native to Central America, common in the hot areas of tropical America. It is reported in the western Indies since 1526, was introduced to Florida in 1847 and before 1886 it was already common in more than half of that state. Spanish and Portuguese colonists brought it to Guam and the Eastern Indies. It was soon adopted as a crop in Asia and in the hot areas of Africa. It is believed that it came from Egypt, passed through Palestine, Algeria, and the Mediterranean coast of France.

## 1.2 USES.

It is mainly cultivated for fresh consumption, but with the development of the agro-industry, many products have been found that can be produced from this fruit, including: juices, jams, jellies, frozen, sorbet, gelatin, and a product known in the Caribbean as guava "cascos". It is also used to obtain a concentrated product, which is the base for the bakery and confectionery industry.

This fruit also has medicinal uses, it is used as an astringent, against diarrhea, and against indigestion, it prevents hair loss, against jaundice, against wounds and swelling, as a parasite, and against rheumatism.

## 1.3 MARKET.

The global production of guava is around 1.2 million tons, with India and Pakistan accounting for 50%, Mexico for 25%, and the rest by other countries such as Colombia, Egypt, and Brazil.

## NATIONAL DEMAND

Fresh fruit. The local market for fresh fruit is in municipal markets, supermarkets, and in fairs. The harvested fruit is cleaned and packaged in trays or loose in the field for distribution. The Alimentos Laboratory of CENTA has developed value-added technologies for the fruit and transferred this technology to small and medium-sized producers attended by MAG-CENTA-FRUTALES and Extension Agencies throughout the country.

## GLOBAL DEMAND

Guava juice. The global market for guava juice is small, around 6,000 tons/year, with the main importers being North America, Europe, and the Middle East.

The demand is stable: more active in North America than in Europe, where the taste/aroma of guava is not within the consumer's acceptance range. Guava juice is marketed as a puree and concentrated puree, and is mainly used in mixed fruit juices and nectars. There is also demand in the baby food industry. Exporting countries use different varieties, but generally, it can be said that:

Brazil exports red guava juice, Malaysia and South Africa export pink guava, India and Egypt export white guava. Guava for processing is harvested when it reaches full maturity.

Guava puree contains 8° to 11° Brix, and concentrated guava puree contains 20° Brix. Preservation is generally carried out by freezing in 200 kg steel drums or aseptic packaging (e.g., in a drum).

The global market for guava puree and concentrated puree is approximately 6,000 metric tons, calculated at 20° Brix. The demand in the European market is more or less stable, mainly due to the fact that the aroma/taste of guava is not entirely to the taste of European consumers.

European and American markets generally prefer pink guava with few seeds. Arab markets prefer white guava, sometimes with seeds.

## 1.5 CLIMATIC REQUIREMENTS

It grows very well in places with temperatures between 16-34°C, with a relative humidity of 36 to 96%, conditions that make it cultivable from sea level up to 800 meters above sea level, and with an annual rainfall of 1000-1800 mm. There are reports that in Colombia some varieties have been cultivated up to 1,700 meters above sea level and in Venezuela up to 1,800.

## 1.6 SOIL REQUIREMENTS

Traditionally, guava plants have been found in poor soils, which is why producers tend to cultivate them in places where other crops do not develop very well, but the

improved varieties require special conditions in order to fully express their genetic potential, it has been found that the soils that favor them are franco to franco-clayey with a good content of organic matter, while sandy soils are not very favorable, manifesting in slow and weak development. The pH is in the range of 6 to 7; it is a fairly tolerant plant to humidity, but not for prolonged periods.

## 1.8 MORPHOLOGY

ROOT.
A main pivot root that provides good anchorage, giving rise to secondary roots, which proliferate in large numbers and are located near the surface of the soil. In deep soils, secondary roots can acquire the thickness and length of the main root, reaching up to 4 meters in depth, which is why plants propagated by cuttings or grafting do not suffer from leaning, as these roots provide good anchorage. The root zone is located from 0 to 30 centimeters in depth. Guava plants can tolerate humidity for short periods, but if the time is prolonged, they can die from suffocation.

STEM.
Initially herbaceous, with serrations and a green color, as it matures it becomes woody and the serrations disappear, becoming a brown color. It reaches heights of 3 to 9 meters, but with modern fruit cultivation practices, systematic pruning is applied to maintain a height of 1.5 to 3.0 meters, in order to facilitate cultivation. The bark is thin and smooth, and when it ages, it peels off in sheets, containing between 13 and 15% tannins.

The plant grows from the buds. In some of the nodes (every third or fourth node of the new branch), floral buds develop, which give rise to the flowers that subsequently turn into fruit.

**2. LEAVES**

These are lance-shaped, dark green in color, with abundant pubescence on the underside and oil glands. They develop in pairs on each node and are arranged oppositely. Young leaves are upright, which reduces the exposure to sunlight, and when they mature, they turn horizontal to have greater exposure to the sun.

**2. FLOWERS**

In some of the nodes, in addition to the leaf and the vegetative bud, a floral bud also appears. This occurs exclusively on young branches. These are hermaphroditic flowers, gathered in racemose inflorescences. The female reproductive system consists of a hypogenous ovary, plurilocular, which can have three to five lobes, formed by five syncarpous carpels, with a simple style, with a length of 1.2 to 1.5 centimeters, and presents a light green color in the stigma, which is subcapitate. Guava flowers do not have nectar glands. The stigma remains receptive for 40 hours after the opening of the flower.

The male reproductive system is composed of an average of 350 free stamens. Pollen release begins before the flower opens, which causes a 26% self-pollination, the rest is pollinated by cross-pollination, mainly by bees. The viability of the pollen is an average of 33 hours.

**2. FRUIT**

It is a berry, which, depending on the variety, can be round or pear-shaped, with a green-yellow to pale yellow skin, white, yellow or pink pulp, weighing between 50 grams to 1,000 grams. It has a double sigmoid growth, meaning it has two points of maximum growth: at the beginning of development and after the maturation of the seeds. Internally, the fruit has four or five lobes, in which the seeds are located, for a total of 218 to 375 seeds. The seed viability decreases rapidly, and can lose up to 60% in one year. Due to its respiration rate, it is classified as a climacteric fruit.

**2.19 PROPAGATION**

**2. ASEXUAL**

Through this type of propagation, it is ensured that the plants obtained have the same characteristics as the original plants. To obtain the best results from orchards established with plants propagated asexually, it is necessary to select as mother plants those that present high yields, sweet fruits, tolerance to some pests, diseases or adverse conditions, and all those characteristics that make them stand out from other plants in the group.

There are different methods of asexual propagation, including budding, stem cuttings, and grafting.

**A) Budding**

This consists of selecting healthy and mature branches, of a coffee color, to which a cut of 1 cm is made on the bark. A product containing hormones that promote rooting, such as Proroot or Rotone, is applied to this cut, then it is covered with a mixture of soil and straw in a ratio of 1:1, and then covered with plastic or another material. The advantage of plastic is that it allows you to see when the roots appear. When this moment arrives, a cut is made below the roots and the plant is planted in a 9 x 12' bag.

This method is not very complicated, but it requires that the substrate be moistened periodically. However, it is not very convenient for mass propagation, as it requires a lot of material to obtain a plant.

**B) Stem cuttings**

This method uses young branches, which are those with vigorous growth and sometimes with a reddish color. These are cut into pieces of two nodes, the leaves of the lower node are removed, and the leaves of the upper node are cut in half. Then, longitudinal cuts are made on the lower part of the cutting, and a hormonal product is applied. Then, the cuttings are placed in rooting substrates, such as sand. The key to success in this method is to ensure a high relative humidity, for which the rooting substrate can be covered with plastic or the entire process can be carried out in a plant propagation unit with subirrigation. Generally, the appearance of roots is observed in 21-30 days, and the plants are then transplanted into bags.

**C) Grafting**

For this method, plants with rootstock and scion are required. These are produced using seeds of local varieties with tolerance to adverse soil conditions, poor soils, pests, waterlogging, etc.

**2.10 NUTRITION**

A nutrition program is based on providing the nutrients in a form that is readily available at the right time and in the appropriate amount to achieve optimal growth, differentiation, and maturation of the crop. In addition, it is the one that, along with a good climate and crop management, allows the maximum expression of the genetic potential of that particular plant.

It is observed that the concentration of elements is higher in the fruits than in the other organs, with the exception of calcium, which concentrates more in the branches and a small amount passes to the fruits due to the low mobility of this element within the plant. It is also observed that it is quite demanding in calcium and magnesium. Therefore, in soil analyses, these elements should be included.

## Nitrogen

This element has high mobility within the plant, so its deficiency is only visible in the older leaves. Nitrogen is essential for the synthesis of chlorophyll, and as part of the chlorophyll molecule, it plays a role in the process of photosynthesis. A lack of nitrogen and chlorophyll means that the plant will not use sunlight as a source of energy to carry out essential functions such as nutrient absorption. Nitrogen is also a component of the plant's vitamins and energy systems.

In guava cultivation, nitrogen applications should be balanced with phosphorus and potassium, as high amounts of nitrogen can make the plant vigorous with rapid growth and increased yield, but the fruit quality decreases as it becomes less sweet, loses uniformity in ripening, becomes flaccid, and may fall from the plant. The deficiency of this element is identified as yellowing of the older leaves, short internodes, fewer leaves than a normal plant, and slow growth.

When fertilizing during the rainy season, it is very important to take into account the nitrogen contribution from the rain, especially electrical storms, as this can cause an imbalance with potassium.

## Phosphorus

This is another element with high mobility within the plant, and the symptoms of deficiency appear in the older leaves. Its main function is to stimulate root development, which is why it is essential to apply at the time of planting and when it is necessary to help replenish roots after an attack by nematodes or root-knot nematodes; in addition, it provides energy during photosynthesis and the transport of carbohydrates, and it is a regulator of all plant cycles. ## Potassium

This is the element most required by guava and other fruit plants, and it performs several functions in plant growth. It is essential for photosynthesis and respiration, helps with the translocation of photosynthesis products to various parts of the plant, and actively participates in the process of cell division, regulating sugar availability. It gives vigor and resistance to diseases and low temperatures, and controls the opening and closing of stomata.

It has high mobility within the plant, so the symptoms are first observed in the older leaves, which show necrosis on the margins and tips. The plant has low yield, fruits with little sweetness and soft texture, which leads to a shortened post-harvest life. Excess in the soil blocks the fixation of magnesium and calcium.

## Calcium

This is an essential nutrient in cell walls, regulating nutrient absorption, strengthening roots, and, along with potassium, is responsible for giving fruits greater firmness. This element has low mobility in the xylem and even lower in the phloem, which means that foliar applications have a relatively low effectiveness. Its deficiency manifests in the tender leaves.

## II CROP MANAGEMENT.

## 2.1 PLANT PRODUCTION.

Propagation should be asexual, the most used technique in El Salvador is grafting, although budding can also be used; in addition to budding and stem cuttings in a smaller scale.

In the case of grafting, it is recommended to use guava or guava that grows in the orchards as the rootstock, the seed is collected in the middle of the rainy season and the seedlings are prepared, then when the seedling reaches 2-3 cm, it is transplanted to a 9' x 14' x 300 mesh black bag.

The plant is grafted when it reaches a thickness of a pencil (6 months after transplanting), the cutting with the buds is collected from a highly productive plant, once cut it can last for 3 days, maintaining it with adequate humidity.

Three weeks after the grafting, the bud begins to sprout. At this point, the plastic is removed to allow its development. After another two months, the plant is ready for planting in its final location.

## 2.2 PLANTING.

The recommended spacing is 3.75 m between rows and 3.0 m between plants, for a density of 622 plants per manzana (a unit of area, similar to an acre). The best time to plant is at the beginning of the rainy season, although it can be done at any time, as irrigation is required. This is a crop that must be established exclusively with irrigation.

In sloping areas, soil conservation practices and works such as trench planting, level curves, individual terraces, and hillside ditches should be carried out, along with the use of live hedges.

The planting hole will be 0.4 x 0.4 x 0.4 meters or larger, and 20 grams of nematicide insecticide, 100 grams of formula 18-46-0, and 20 pounds of organic fertilizer should be placed at the bottom of the hole.

Before starting the application of fertilizers, a complete soil analysis should be carried out to determine the appropriate doses and products, and if necessary, lime should be applied to correct the pH.

Fertilization is carried out monthly, and the plan should include the application of calcium, magnesium, boron, iron, and zinc, according to the recommendations of the soil laboratory. The guava plant should be fertilized every month with the quantities and type of fertilizer mentioned in table #. It is recommended to alternate applications of formula 0-0-60 with potassium nitrate and/or Sulpomag, the latter provides potassium, magnesium, and sulfur, but in soils with acidity problems (pH below 5.5), it is better not to use this fertilizer, nor ammonium sulfate, as they make the soil more acidic. In addition to nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, the plant requires other minerals. The soil analysis will indicate which elements are present in limited quantities in the soil, and applications of these should be made to prevent any deficiency problems. ## 2.4 FORMATION PRUNING.

One week after transplanting, a cut is made to the main branches, ensuring that they are 25 centimeters long. This cut stimulates the lateral buds, which sprout and give rise to new branches (secondary branches). From these, 2 to 3 are selected, depending on the vigor of the plant, and they are spaced evenly. Then, they are allowed to grow to 30 cm, and then they are cut, leaving them at 25 cm. These also produce shoots that turn into branches (tertiary branches), and again, 2 to 3 branches are selected from each tertiary branch. This process is repeated three times (lasting about six months), resulting in 8 to 12 branches. At the same time as these pruning, the central branches should be removed to allow an open crown. Branches that cross each other from one side to the other, low branches, spindly branches, and those that come out vertically from other branches (i.e., in a straight line) should also be removed. Figure 8.

Generally, improved guava varieties produce their floral buds between the third and fifth node, but this doesn't always happen, and they may appear above the tenth node. This leads to wasted time, and there's also a risk that the branch will break due to being too long. Therefore, pruning is carried out to shorten the branch to five nodes. It's best to prune when the five nodes are visible and there are no flowers.

## 2.6 Pruning for Health

This involves removing any branches that show signs of disease or pest attack, broken branches, and those that cross each other.

## 2.7 Pruning for Renewal

As the plant grows, it becomes more difficult to monitor for pests and diseases, apply protective treatments, and bag the fruit. Also, if the pruning for shaping hasn't been done properly, the plant can develop branches that are too long, resulting in what is known as a "grafted" plant. Therefore, pruning is necessary to reduce the height of the crown. This pruning involves making cuts of 10-15 cm on secondary or tertiary branches to lower the height of the crown.

## 2.8 Thinning/Fruit Reduction

This activity, also known as thinning, involves removing the tips of the fingers on a branch, usually after the fruit has set (after four sets of leaves have appeared). The goal is to prevent nutrients from being wasted on the fruit, so they remain in the fruit to allow it to grow larger. Figure 9.

## 2.9 Fruit Selection and Thinning

Each branch typically produces two to six fruits, of which one is selected. The deformed and upright fruits are removed, as they are likely to be damaged by the sun. The selected fruit is the one that is hanging down, covered by the leaves. This process is carried out in two phases: the first before the flowers open, leaving one flower on each side of the node; the second when the fruits are about 2 centimeters in size. Figure 10.

## 2.10 Bagging

Immediately after the second thinning, a spray is applied to the fruits with a fungicide and insecticide to protect them. The next day, after the recommended waiting period for the protective treatments, a white plastic bag is placed around each fruit. These bags are 8' x 12' x 100 mesh (low density) or 10' x 14' x 125 mesh, and they provide good protection from sunlight, reducing the risk of sunburn. Some producers also place a polypropylene mesh around the bag to prevent the fruit from touching the bag, further reducing the risk of sunburn, although this does involve an additional cost of $0.05 per fruit. ## 2.11 Pest and Disease Control

## 1. Blind Beetle, Currunco, and Chicote (Phyllophaga spp. Anemola sp)

This is a pest that causes significant damage to the roots and can completely destroy the plant. It also causes damage indirectly by creating wounds that allow fungi or bacteria to enter.

## Life cycle:

*   **Eggs:** Laid in the soil at a depth of 2 to 10 centimeters, they are white and elongated, becoming spherical shortly after being laid. The incubation period is 10-14 days.
*   **Larvae:** Live underground, feeding on plant roots. The larval stage lasts for 1 to 2 years.

Fig. 12. Larval stage of Phyllophaga sp.

<!-- image -->

## 2. Guava Fruit Fly (Anastrepha striata)

## Life cycle

The life cycle develops in three environments: vegetation, the fruit, and the soil. Adults live on the host plant or nearby plants, spending most of their time there. After mating, the female lays eggs inside the fruit, where the larvae feed on the pulp. There are three instars (stages). The third instar larvae leave the fruit and burrow into the soil, where they pupate. The adults emerge from the puparium after a few days, restarting the cycle. The larval stage lasts from 13 to 28 days, while the pupal stage lasts from 14 to 23 days.

The male adult can live for 14 to 319 days, while the female's lifespan is 13 to 134 days. The pre-oviposition period is approximately 13 days. Females lay 10 to 110 eggs per batch in ripening fruit. The depth of the oviposition varies from 5.1 to 8.2 mm.

## Control

The most effective way to control fruit flies is by bagging the fruit with plastic or paraffin-coated paper bags, which are placed when the fruit is 2 centimeters long.

Collecting damaged fruit.

Placing Lafarel-type traps with guava juice, hydrolyzed protein, or sugar-sweetened water.

Applying organophosphate insecticides, such as Malathion, avoiding exceeding the permitted tolerance levels.

## 3. Aphids and Scale Insects ( *A. gossypii* and *Myzus persicae*)

## Life Cycle

Winged females of scale insects can invade guava plants from the nursery stage. They reproduce parthenogenetically and viviparously, meaning that they only give birth to live females, which are already born alive. The duration of a generation depends on the temperature and can last up to 10 days in warm climates. A single female can give birth to up to 100 nymphs.

Fig. 14. Adult Aphid

<!-- image -->

## Habits and Damage

Both adults and nymphs live in colonies on the underside of the terminal leaves of the plants. They feed by sucking sap and inject a toxic saliva that causes curling, reducing the vigor of the plant. Because they suck more sap than their bodies can handle, they cover the leaves with a sugary liquid, which serves as a substrate for the development of a fungus called *Capnodium sp*, which affects the normal function of photosynthesis in plants.

## Physical-Mechanical Control

Use yellow traps with oil, with dimensions of 1x0.5 mt. Place at least 16 traps per tree in the surrounding area, with emphasis on the direction where the wind blows.

## Chemical Control

For the control of scale insects, the following insecticides are recommended:

- Imidacloprid: 200 cc per tree
- Malathion: 57% CE 1 liter per tree
- Rescue (acetamiprid) 20 sp: 200 grams per tree

<!-- image -->

4. Ticks: *Eotetranychus sp*, *Panonychus sp*, *Tetranychus sp*, *Oligonychus sp*

## Life Cycle

Ticks have a short life cycle and rapidly increase their populations. The eggs of these arachnids are spherical and are laid one by one on the underside of the leaves. The female lays 4 to 6 eggs per day, during a period of one month, and they hatch in about 3 days. The nymph stage has 3 instars, in which the first has 3 pairs of legs and the remaining 4 pairs. They complete their nymphal period in 5 to 10 days.

Adults measure from 0.5 to 0.7 mm in length and can have a pink-green or orange-red color. The time it takes to complete a generation is between 9 and 21 days, and they are generally dispersed by the wind.

## Habits and Damage

Both adults and nymphs feed by sucking sap, mainly on the underside of the leaf, near the main and secondary veins, and live under a silk web when numerous. Due to their feeding, they cause a white or yellowish, bronzed spotting, which can distort and curl the leaves at the tips. In the fruit, it causes a decrease in its commercial value.

When the damage is very severe, the leaves fall off and the shoots may die. The damage caused by ticks is increased during the dry season, which is when they have favorable conditions for reproduction and their damage can be mistaken for the symptoms of a virus.

## Control Method

Since ticks are very small organisms, careful sampling is necessary, mainly during periods of drought and the dry season. Sampling will allow for timely control and prevent further damage to the crop.

When deciding to control them, the following products are recommended:

Abamectin:

1.8% CE 20 ml/liter

Sulfur 80%:

1 kg per tree

Torque:

120 gr per tree

Oberon:

250 cc per tree

## DISEASES

## 1. Anthracnose ( *Colletotrichum gloeosporioides*)

This is a disease that mainly appears from flowering, attacking flowers and fruits. In flowers, it causes mummification, leaving the flowers black, and in fruits, the symptoms are the appearance of watery, sunken spots, in the form of concentric rings of brown color, which, as the fruit ripens, spread and deepen.

## Control

The presence of this disease should be monitored from the beginning of flowering, and control can be decided according to its incidence in the crop.

For its control, any of the following fungicides can be used:

Trimiltox Forte: 1 to 1.5 kg per tree

Cycosin 50% F: 0.5 to 1 liter per tree

Saprol 20 CE: 0.75 liters per tree

Amistar 50: 150 grams per tree

## 2. Pestalotiopsis (Pestalotia sp.)

Symptoms: Brown spots with a dry appearance, appearing near the scar of the sepals, and occurring in both the dry and rainy seasons, favored by the high humidity generated inside the plastic bag used to protect the plant.

Fig. 15. Fruit affected by Pestalotiopsis

Distribution: Appears during both the dry and rainy seasons, although a higher incidence has been observed between February and April.

## Management

Use bags that facilitate the release of water vapor produced by respiration.

Apply preventative systemic products such as: Azoxystrobin 150 g/tree, Carbendazim 350 cc/tree

Prune to remove excess foliage to facilitate air circulation in the plantation.

Implement an appropriate fertilization plan that reduces the susceptibility of the fruit to fungal attack, with adequate levels of potassium and calcium.

## 2.12 WEED CONTROL

Weed control is carried out manually, mechanically, and chemically. Paraquat at a dose of 1.5 liters per tree, or Glyphosate 32.6% at a dose of 1.5-2 liters per tree, can be used. Before using any product, consult the local regulations.

## 2.13 IRRIGATION

Water should be provided during the dry season in the rainy season and throughout the dry season, although there is currently no data on daily water requirements. However, some producers have been applying between 60 and 120 liters of water per plant per day. This depends on the type of soil and environmental conditions such as temperature, wind, solar radiation, as well as the age of the plant. Given the volume of water applied, the most recommended irrigation system is micro-sprinklers, but drip irrigation can be used where necessary. Subsurface irrigation is not recommended due to the risk of spreading nematodes and guava is very susceptible to them.

Fig. 16. Micro-sprinkler (70 Lph)

## 2.14 HARVESTING

For the Taiwanese varieties, the harvest index is determined by the change in skin color from green to pale yellow, and after a four-month period after pre-harvest. At this point, the fruit has a longer shelf life and a juicy, crisp texture. It is generally recommended to harvest in the morning, avoid exposing the fruit to direct sunlight, and avoid any impact during harvesting, internal transport, cleaning, sorting, and packaging, in order to minimize internal bruising, which reduces post-harvest life.

Sorting is based on size and appearance according to the destination market. Currently, supermarket quality standards require fruit of the first grade with the following characteristics: weight between 0.42 and 1.2 pounds, a round shape with a tolerance of 10% for long fruits, and without mechanical or insect damage.

Fig. 17. Fruit prepared for delivery to supermarkets.

## 2.15 MARKETING

The fruit is marketed in 25-pound boxes. For delivery to supermarkets, each fruit is placed in a protective mesh (foam), and for the informal market, this is not usually done.

Currently, some supermarkets offer the convenience of delivering the fruit to a collection center, where they take care of the distribution to the sales outlets of the chain. Others require the producer (supplier) to distribute the fruit to all the sales outlets assigned to them.
