## I. INTRODUCTION

In El Salvador, bean cultivation is part of food security, as the consumption of beans provides protein, fiber, and minerals. Each resident in our country consumes approximately 17.32 kilograms per year. According to the DGEA - MAG, in the 2016-2017 agricultural cycle, 177,498 hectares of beans were planted, with a production of 2.6 million quintals and a productivity of 15.0 qq/ha.

CENTA, through research, aims to generate varieties with high productivity, adapted to climate change, and with low susceptibility to diseases that limit the production of this crop. The economic importance of the new varieties lies in their high yield potential, which allows them to surpass local varieties. Regarding the social benefits, it can be said that due to the productivity of the varieties, producers can obtain greater surpluses, which allows for greater availability of grain for rural families and in markets, contributing to producers having better incomes. The fact that these germplasm have tolerance to diseases that affect the crop, such as the common mosaic virus and the yellow mosaic virus, allows for the reduction of the use of agrochemicals, contributing to minimizing environmental contamination.

With this technical guide, we aim to help producers and technicians to implement better management practices for the new bean varieties and thus achieve higher yields in their crops.

## II. BOTANICAL DESCRIPTION

The bean plant is annual, herbaceous, and is cultivated primarily for obtaining the seeds and grains, which have a high protein content, around 22%.

## ROOT

In the early stages of development, the root system consists of the embryo's radicle, which subsequently becomes the main or primary root. A few days later, secondary roots appear, developing in the upper or neck region of the main root. Tertiary roots and other subdivisions, such as root hairs, develop on the secondary roots, and these are found at all points of root growth.

Although generally distinguished, the root system tends to be fascicled and fibrous in some cases, but with a wide variation, even within the same variety.

Phaseolus vulgaris exhibits nodular formations on the lateral roots of the upper and middle parts of the root system. These nodules are polyhedral in shape and have an approximate diameter of 2 to 5 millimeters. They are colonized by bacteria of the genus Rhizobium, which fix atmospheric nitrogen, contributing to the plant's ability to meet its nitrogen requirements.

## STEM

The stem can be identified as the central axis of the plant, consisting of a succession of nodes and internodes; it is herbaceous and has a cylindrical or slightly angular section; it can be erect, semi-erect, or prostrate, depending on the growth habit of the variety.

## LEAVES

They come in two types: simple and compound. The cotyledons constitute the first pair of leaves, providing the plant with storage substances during germination and emergence, and they carry out the first carbohydrate synthesis through photosynthesis in their chloroplasts. They have a short lifespan, and the second pair and the first true leaves develop in the second node; they are simple, opposite, and serrated. From the third node, compound leaves develop, which are alternate, three-lobed.

They have a petiole and a rachis. They exhibit variation in size, color, and pubescence, and this variation is related to the variety and the environmental conditions of light and humidity.

## FLOWER

The flower contains the plant's sexual organs. Male flowers, called stamens, and female flowers, called pistils. Pollen is produced in the stamens, and when it falls on the pistils, it produces the seed or grain.

The calyx is a funnel-shaped tube at the apex, which divides into five lobes, two of which are partially joined; the rose-purple to almost white corolla consists of five unequal petals, with the outermost petal being the largest and most prominent, called the banner. The bean flower does not open until it has been pollinated, which is why it is classified as an autogamous plant. There are essentially two types of color in bean flowers: white for red and purple bean varieties, and black for black bean varieties.

## SEED

The seed is the reproductive unit of plants. It is the product obtained after flowering and other phenomena that occur within the flower. The seed comes in various colors, depending on the variety: red, black, white, yellow, and others.

## DEVELOPMENT STAGES OF THE BEAN PLANT

The development of the bean plant comprises two successive phases: vegetative and reproductive.

The vegetative phase begins when the seed has favorable conditions for germination, and ends when the first floral buds appear. During this phase, the majority of the vegetative structure that the plant needs to initiate its reproduction is formed.

The reproductive phase ends when the grain reaches the maturity level necessary for harvest; although this phase is predominantly reproductive, during it, indeterminate varieties continue to produce vegetative structures (CIAT 1985), albeit with less intensity.

## VEGETATIVE PHASE

## Stage VO: Germination

The VO stage begins, in which the seed has sufficient moisture for the start of the germination process. The seed initially absorbs water, and the phenomena of cell division and biochemical reactions occur within it, releasing the nutrients from the cotyledons. Subsequently, the radicle emerges (usually on the side of the hilum). Then, it becomes the primary root as secondary and tertiary roots appear (Figure 1). The hypocotyl also grows, with the cotyledons remaining at ground level. This marks the end of the germination stage.

## Stage VI: Emergence

This stage begins when the cotyledons of the plant appear at ground level (Figure 2); a bean crop is considered to have entered this stage when 50% of the population reaches this situation. After emergence, the hypocotyl straightens and continues to grow until it reaches its maximum size. When it is completely erect, the cotyledons begin to separate, and it becomes apparent that the epicotyl has started to develop.

## Stage V2: Primary leaves

The primary leaves of the bean plant are simple and opposite, located in the second node of the main stem; when fully developed, they are generally in a horizontal position, although they have not reached their maximum size (Figure 3). During this stage, the rapid vegetative development of the plant begins, during which the stem, branches, and trifoliate leaves will be formed. The trifoliate leaves are alternate. At the beginning of this stage, the first trifoliate leaf begins to grow. The cotyledons lose their shape at this point, becoming arched and wrinkled. The growth of a trifoliate leaf involves three steps: initially, the leaflets are still joined and increase in size; then, they separate, and finally, they unfold and extend in a single plane.

Figure 3. Primary leaves of a bean plant

<!-- image -->

## Stage V3: First trifoliate leaf

This stage begins when the plant presents the first fully opened and flat trifoliate leaf (Figure 4). When 50% of the plants in a crop present the first trifoliate leaf unfolded, this stage begins. A leaf is considered unfolded when the leaflets are located in a plane. The leaf has not yet reached its maximum size, and both the internode between the primary leaves and the first trifoliate leaf are short, as is the petiole of the trifoliate leaf; for this reason, when stage V3 begins, the first trifoliate leaf is located below the primary leaves. Subsequently, the petiole grows, and the first trifoliate leaf overshoots the primary leaves; the second trifoliate leaf has already appeared, and the cotyledons have completely dried and, generally, fallen off.

Figure 4. First trifoliate leaf of a bean plant

## Stage V4: Third trifoliate leaf.

This begins when the third trifoliate leaf is fully developed (Figure 5). In a crop, the stage V4 is considered to have begun when 50% of the plants exhibit this characteristic. Similarly to the first trifoliate leaf; this is considered fully developed when the leaflets of the leaves are in a single plane; you can observe that the leaf is still below the first trifoliate leaf. From this stage, some vegetative structures, such as the stem, branches, and other trifoliate leaves, which develop from the triads of buds found in the leaf axils of the plant, even the primary leaves and cotyledons, can be clearly distinguished. The buds at the lower nodes of the plant generally begin to develop when the plant initiates stage V3, i.e., when the plant has the first trifoliate leaf fully developed.

When there are an average of three or four fully developed trifoliate leaves on the main stem, the first branch will have already formed the first node with a trifoliate leaf. In this way, the development of other branches on the stem and other trifoliate leaves continues.

Figure 5. Third trifoliate leaf of the bean plant

## REPRODUCTIVE PHASE

## Stage R5: Pre-flowering.

This begins when the first flower bud or the first floral cluster appears (Figure 6). In a crop, this stage is considered to have begun when 50% of the plants exhibit this characteristic. In a particular variety, the development of floral buds begins at the last node of the stem or branch. In contrast, in indeterminate varieties, at the beginning of this stage, the clusters are observed at the lower nodes.

Figure 6. Pre-flowering stage of the bean plant

## Stage R6: Flowering.

This stage begins when the plant exhibits the first open flower (Figure 7) and, in a crop, when 50% of the plants exhibit this characteristic. The first open flower corresponds to the first floral bud that appeared. Once the flower has been pollinated and is open, the petals wither and the pod begins to grow; as a consequence of the growth of the pod, the petals wither and either fall off or remain attached.

Figure 7. Flowering stage of the bean plant

## Stage R7: Pod formation.

This begins when a plant exhibits the first pod with the flower petals hanging or detached (Figure 8), and in cultivation, when 50% of the plants exhibit this characteristic. In plants with a determinate growth habit, the first pods are observed on the upper part of the stem and branches; the remaining pods appear downwards. Conversely, in plants with an indeterminate growth habit, the first pods form at the lower part, and the appearance of the others occurs upwards. The formation of the pod initially involves the development of the valves. During the first 10 or 15 days after flowering, the main growth occurs in the length of the pod, with little growth in the seeds. When the valves reach their final size and maximum weight, the filling of the pods begins.

Figure 8. Pod formation stage of the bean plant

## Stage R8: Pod filling.

Stage R8 begins when 50% of the plants start to fill the first pod, beginning the active growth of the seeds or grain. The pod grows after flowering until the 10 or 12th day, its weight until the 15 or 20th day, and the seeds reach their maximum weight at the 30 or 35th day after flowering (Figures 9 and 10).

At the end of the stage, the grains lose their green color and acquire the characteristic colors of the variety.

Figure 9. Pod filling stage of the bean plant

Figure 10. Bean plant in pod filling stage

In some genotypes, the valves of the pods also begin to pigment. The distribution of pigmentation, whether uniform, in stripes, or otherwise, depends on the genotype. Typical pigmentation of the valves usually appears after the start of pigmentation of the seeds. At the end of this stage, the beginning of defoliation is also observed, starting with the lower leaves, which turn chlorotic and fall off. The timing of the defoliation also depends on the genotype.

## Stage R9: Physiological maturity.

A bean crop enters this stage when at least one pod begins to discolor and dry, in 90% of the plants in the population (Figure 11). The yellowing and falling of the leaves and all parts of the plant continue; as the pods dry, they lose their pigmentation (Rosas 2012).

## III. BEAN GROWTH HABITS

The main morphological and agronomic characteristics that help define the growth habit of the bean are:

- The development of the terminal part of the stem, which allows it to be classified as determinate or indeterminate.
- The number of nodes.
- The length of the nodes and, consequently, the height of the plant.
- The ability to climb.
- The degree and type of branching. It is necessary to include the concept of guide, which is defined as the part of the stem and/or branches that protrudes above the foliage of the crop (Rosas 2003).

## DETERMINATE GROWTH HABIT

Type I: Arbustive determinate growth habit: The stem and branches end in a developed inflorescence. When this inflorescence is formed, growth stops.

## Indeterminate Growth Habit

Type II A: Indeterminate, bushy growth habit: upright stem without climbing ability, but ending in a short spur. Branches do not produce spurs.

Type II B: Indeterminate, bushy growth habit: upright stem with climbing ability, ending in a long spur.

In addition, other organs such as leaves, branches, roots, and flowers are present at each node on the stem.

In conditions of irregular rainfall below the water requirements of the crop, yields tend to decrease dramatically if they coincide with the flowering and pod filling stages. Water consumption can vary depending on the type of bean crop used.

## Temperature

The bean plant grows well between average temperatures of 15 to 27 °C, which generally prevail at elevations of 400 to 1500 msnm. Currently, varieties have been developed that can tolerate high temperatures and can be cultivated at lower and higher elevations.

## Light

The main role of light is in photosynthesis, but it also affects the phenology and morphology of a plant through light-mediated reactions.

## Soil Requirements

Bean crops require fertile soils with good organic matter content; the most suitable soil textures are loamy and sandy, these soils allow for better aeration and drainage, an important factor for better root development and nodule formation. This crop does not tolerate compacted soils, poor aeration, and water accumulation.

The optimal pH (acidity or alkalinity) fluctuates between 6.5 and 7.5; within this range, most of the nutrients in the soil are present in maximum availability to the plant. The bean tolerates pH up to 5.5, although below this limit, it generally shows symptoms of aluminum and/or manganese toxicity.

However, the susceptibility of local varieties to the main pests that attack bean cultivation in the country warrants recommending the use of improved varieties developed by CENTA (Table 2).

## VI. AGRONOMIC MANAGEMENT OF BEAN CULTIVATION

## PLANTING SEASONS

For bean cultivation in El Salvador, three planting seasons are recognized:

May season: The decision to plant in this season depends on the behavior of the rains. It is recommended to wait until the soil moisture reaches at least 20 cm in depth before planting. This season typically occurs between May 25th and June 5th. In this season, the predominant planting system is monoculture.

August season: This planting season is generally conditioned by the physiological maturity of corn, as it is the prevailing relay system. Currently, its implementation coincides with the period from August 15th to September 15th.

November season: This season is carried out under irrigation or residual moisture. It is recommended to plant between November 15th and December 15th, in order to escape two important pests of the bean crop: the whitefly and the Empoasca (Bemisia tabaci and Empoasca kraemeri), and the incidence of fungal diseases is lower. This season is the most recommended for seed and grain production.

## PLANTING SYSTEMS

## Bean only or monoculture

It can be done at any planting season and consists of planting the bean alone, with spacings of 50 to 60 cm between rows and an average of 10 plants per square meter (Figure 12).

## Bean in association

It can be sown in association with all crops in which there is no competition for light. In El Salvador, the most common association is with corn or with sugar cane. Also with perennial crops in their first years of planting.

The corn-bean association is recommended to be done at the same time, but if there are delays in any of the crops, the planting period of the second crop should not exceed 5 days (Figure 13).

When associating bean with sugar cane, it is recommended to do it with second-cut sugar cane, after the cane has been harvested.

The spacing between plants depends on the distance between the rows of sugar cane, generally 2 or 3 rows of bean are sown between 2 rows of sugar cane. The spacing between bean plants is 10-20 cm, placing 2 seeds per hole.

## CULTIVATION CHARACTERISTICS

Bean sowing should be done in loose and well-drained soils, with good organic matter content, and with a depth of more than 30 cm. As much as possible, it is recommended to avoid sowing in soils that are easily compacted or that form crusts when they dry, as these affect the proper development of the plants.

## PREPARING THE SOWING AREA

The yield of bean plants can vary greatly depending on the proper preparation of the soil. The following recommendations are suggested:

## Monoculture Planting

For sloping land, monoculture planting should begin with weed control, either manually or chemically. Proper management of crop residue is crucial for minimizing pest and disease problems.

For flat land, soil preparation should begin with a single plowing (every three years) and two harrowing passes.

When using animal traction, it is recommended to apply a herbicide 5-12 days before planting, depending on the herbicide used.

## Rotation

If beans are planted in rotation with maize, weed control between the maize rows should be done manually or chemically before the maize is harvested.

On sloping land, planting should be done along the contour lines to reduce soil erosion and nutrient loss. To increase bean and other crop yields, and to conserve soil and water, it is recommended to use contour ridges and minimum tillage.

## SEEDING

Due to the presence of seed-borne diseases in bean crops, it is recommended to use high-quality seeds. Planting with good seeds increases the chances of a good harvest. (Rosas, 2003)

If the quality of the seed is unknown, or if it has been stored for a long time, a germination test should be performed 2-3 weeks before planting.

## PEST CONTROL: INSECTS AND DISEASES

It is important to identify the insects and diseases that affect the crop, for effective control.

## Insect pest management

Bean crops are attacked by insects from planting to harvest and storage. The use of different practices that contribute to prevention and/or control is required, in a rational and timely manner.

## Soil insect pests

These pests cause damage to roots and seedlings. The presence of these insects can be reduced by preparing the soil, making deep tillage and turning the soil over, so that the pests are exposed and dried out by the sun or consumed by birds. Chemical control is also considered (see Table 4).

## a) Blind Beetle: (Phyllophaga sp) and other genera

These are large, C-shaped larvae with a brown head and legs (Figure 16). They feed on the roots of many plants. In bean cultivation, this pest is more common when the soil has been cultivated with grass or cereals. The critical level for this insect is one larva per 30 x 30 cm soil sample and a depth of 20 cm. It is recommended to use chemical treatment on the seeds, or to apply granular insecticides in the planting furrow.

Figure 16. Blind Beetle Larva

## b) Leaf-Cutting Beetles (Agrotis ípsilon)

Damage is observed in plants that have been cut at the base of the stem or have leaf damage in seedlings. The critical level for this pest (Figure 17) is one affected plant for every 20 plants sampled. It is suggested to treat the seeds with chemicals or apply insecticides when the larvae are small, preferably in the afternoon.

Figure 17. Leaf-Cutting Beetle Larva

## Defoliating Pests

To reduce the presence of these pests, it is very important to keep the fields free of weeds.

## a) Slugs (Vaginulus plebeius)

Slugs are mollusks that cause damage to beans in the early weeks of cultivation (Figure 18). The critical level is one slug per square meter, in the first planting, and one slug per two square meters in the second planting. Manual control is recommended at night. During the day, use trash traps where this pest can hide, placing trash bags every 10 steps and killing the slugs found inside the trash.

Figure 18. Slug Pest

## b) Chrysomelid Beetles: (Diabrotica sp and Cerotoma sp)

Adults are oval-shaped, shiny, with bright and varied colors, some with spots or stripes, about 0.5 cm long, with thin legs and long antennae (Figure 19). Larvae are pale, brownish-dark on both ends and have small legs, close to the head, and live in the soil. Adults make round punctures in the leaves and flowers. Larvae chew on the roots and nodules. If the pest appears during cultivation, it should be controlled with chemicals until before flowering (a maximum of 2 applications).

The critical level for this insect is one beetle for every two plants sampled in the same area, from germination to the appearance of two trifoliate leaves.

Figure 19. Chrysomelid Adult

There are various genera of the order Lepidoptera that attack bean cultivation, in the 3 planting seasons. The most common are the false looper (Trichoplusia ni) and the woolly bear (Estigmene acrea).

Damage: The larvae eat leaves and pods. High populations of larvae can significantly reduce yields.

A bean crop that is already established can tolerate up to 30% leaf loss (defoliation).

Critical level for woolly bear (Figure 21): Thirteen larvae for every 10 plants or damage to the leaves greater than 20%.

## Sucking Pests

## a) Green Bean Weevil (Empoasca kraemeri)

The most critical period includes from emergence to flowering. Affected plants have yellow leaves with the edges rolled downwards. Strong attacks cause stunting and reduce flowering and pod formation. The critical level is one green bean weevil per plant (Figure 22), or three or more nymphs per every 10 plants sampled.

In areas with severe infestation, it is recommended to use chemical treatment on the seeds, or to control when the critical level of infestation is reached (the number of adults or nymphs depends on the stage of development).

Figure 22. Green Bean Weevil

Female insects lay their eggs in a solitary manner within the tissues of flowers, leaves, or young stems. The incubation period varies depending on the temperature, ranging from about 4 days at 26°C, with a high mortality rate at high temperatures and low relative humidity.

When the larvae emerge, they exhibit negative phototropism, locating themselves on the underside of leaves, inside the floral cavity, in the leaf axils, on buds, or in any protected area of the plant.

The larvae reach their maximum development when they stop feeding and seek a place to pupate, typically in leaf litter, plant debris, or in the upper few centimeters of soil.

Reproduction can be sexual, although many species are parthenogenetic (a form of reproduction without the involvement of a male).

The optimal biotic temperature is between 25 and 30°C. Low ambient relative humidity negatively affects the survival of immature stages.

## c) Whitefly (Bemisia tabaci)

Adults are very small, measuring approximately one millimeter. They have two pairs of wings. They are found on the underside of leaves. When the foliage is moved, they fly quickly.

The adult female lays up to 160 eggs on the underside of the leaves. Their eggs are oval and tiny. The nymphs are pale yellow, oval, and flattened. They have no legs or wings and appear as scales. Fully developed nymphs measure less than one millimeter. The insect completes up to 15 generations during each crop.

## Damage

Nymphs feed by sucking sap from plants. Affected leaves show scattered yellowish spots and become wrinkled or curled.

The insect causes more damage as a vector of viruses than through direct feeding (Figure 24). The whitefly transmits geminiviruses, such as the yellow mosaic virus, the chlorotic mottle virus, and the dwarf mosaic virus.

## Control

Critical level: Manage with zero tolerance due to its role as a vector for many viral diseases. Implement control measures from the first sighting of a whitefly in the crop.

Genetic: Plant virus-resistant varieties.

Cultural: Remove plants with virus symptoms, weeds, and other susceptible crops such as cucumber, tomato, soybean, tobacco, cotton, etc. Avoid planting during dry periods when the infestation is more severe. Use living barriers of maize or sorghum.

Chemical: Use chemical treatments only when necessary to control the vector, not the disease itself.

## d) Aphids, Scale Insects (Aphis sp)

Adults and immature stages (nymphs) are the same, differing only in size. They are very small insects (2-2.5 mm), with soft bodies and pear-shaped bodies. They are yellow, green, pink, grey, or black, depending on the species. Some adults have wings. These insects can be found throughout the plant, preferring the underside of leaves (Figure 25).

## Damage

Scale insects are sap-sucking insects. Both adults and nymphs suck sap from leaves, shoots, stems, and flowers. Their saliva is toxic. Affected leaves curl and become wrinkled and eventually fall off the plant. This damage weakens the plants and causes them to become stunted. Severely affected plants turn black. This is due to the growth of the fungus *Fusarium* in a honeydew excreted by these insects. They are important vectors of various viruses, such as CMV (common mosaic virus) and the Rough mosaic virus.

## Control

Critical level: For winged aphids, four insects per five sampled plants. For wingless aphids, one group of insects per two sampled plants.

Cultural: Plant high-density crops in areas with many aphids. Remove infected bean plants and other susceptible crops such as cucumber, tomato, soybean, tobacco, or cotton within the field. Avoid planting during dry periods. Plant living barriers of maize or sorghum.

Biological: Ladybugs (Hippodamia convergens and Cicloneda sanguinea), as well as the insects called "aphid lions" (Chrysoperla externa), are voracious aphid predators. Braconid wasps (Lysiphebus testaceipes and Diaretiella repae) are parasitoids and are commercially available.

Chemical: If necessary, use systemic chemical treatments.

## DISEASE MANAGEMENT

A number of diseases caused by viruses, fungi and bacteria affect the bean crop. The following are the most important:

## VIRUS-CAUSED DISEASES

The most common mosaic virus and the yellow-green bean mosaic virus are the two main viral diseases affecting the country.

## a) Common Mosaic Virus (CMV)

In El Salvador, varieties developed by CENTA are resistant to this virus. It is characterized by plants showing symptoms on the leaves, known as mosaic, which manifests as light green areas on the leaf blade and dark green areas on the veins; the leaves become distorted due to curling and/or folding inwards (Figure 29). Plants with severe symptoms also exhibit stunted growth. The common mosaic virus is primarily transmitted by aphids and by infected seeds; it appears under diverse agroecological conditions and at intermediate temperatures (18-25°C). Under high-temperature conditions (>28°C), symptoms caused by necrotic strains of the virus can appear, consisting of a hypersensitivity reaction, expressed as the death of tissue from the youngest trifoliate leaves to the rest of the plant.

Figure 29. Plant affected by common mosaic virus

<!-- image -->

## b) Yellow-Green Bean Mosaic Virus (GYMV)

Transmitted by the whitefly (Bemisia tabaci), this is the most important viral disease in El Salvador, and its incidence has increased over time, with the potential for more severe effects due to climate conditions. It is not transmitted by seed. The symptoms of yellow-green bean mosaic are very characteristic: the leaves show a strong yellow color (Figure 30). If the plants are affected before flowering, premature flower abortion and deformation of the pods occur. The seeds show spots and deformities, and their weight decreases. Plants affected from very early stages may show severe stunting and fail to produce pods. Losses due to this virus in susceptible varieties can reach 100%.

The yellow-green bean mosaic affects crops grown in areas below 1,200 meters above sea level, where temperatures are higher, and the sources of inoculum and the vector populations are abundant.

The best management strategy is to use resistant varieties (Figure 31), such as: CENTA San Andrés, CENTA Pipil, CENTA NAHUAT, CENTA CPC, CENTA CHAPARRASTIQUE and CENTA EAC.

## DISEASES CAUSED BY BACTERIA

Common Bacterial Blight (Xanthomonas campestris pv. phaseoli).

This disease causes irregular, watery spots on the underside of the leaves. The affected areas become flaccid and are initially surrounded by a yellow-green margin (Figure 32), which then turns into necrotic tissue of brown color. Stems and pods can also be infected. In the pods, the symptoms are small, wet spots that gradually turn brown, and then dark with reddish edges.

Most commercial varieties are susceptible to the disease, so attention should be paid to its management, including the use of improved varieties that show an intermediate reaction to the disease and a better response to a rational and economical chemical control. ## DISEASES CAUSED BY FUNGI

The main diseases affecting bean crops in El Salvador are: downy mildew (Peronospora bryantii), angular leaf spot (Phaeoisariopsis griseola), anthracnose (Colletotrichum lindemuthianum) and bean rust (Uromyces appendiculatus).

## a) Downy mildew or Bean Rust

This is the most important fungus in El Salvador, both due to its widespread distribution and the damage it causes in reducing bean yields (Figure 33).

Downy mildew is favored by rainy, moderate to high temperatures (average of 25-26°C) and high relative humidity. The main sources of inoculum are the oomycete and mycelium, present in the soil or in contaminated crop residues, dispersed by the action of raindrops. The sporangia of the fungus can also cause infection when dispersed by the wind from neighboring fields. Infected seed can transmit the pathogen to other fields and act as a primary source of inoculum.

Downy mildew attacks the foliage, stems, branches and pods of the bean plant, and at any stage of the crop's development. In infection by oomycete and mycelium, the symptoms appear as small, circular, watery lesions of 1-3 mm in diameter, which, as the infection develops, acquire a brown color bordered by a dark margin. The spots on the leaves acquire a grey-green to dark brown color, giving the appearance of being caused by scald. The mycelium of the fungus grows to other parts of the plant, forming a fan or web-like pattern.

The leaves stick together and severe defoliation occurs. Young pods can be completely destroyed, while in mature pods, the lesions merge and cause severe damage and death. The fungus can also infect the seeds. Another less common type of lesion, caused by sporangia, are necrotic spots of 2-3 mm in diameter, in which the necrotic tissue can detach, forming the lesion known as 'eye of the rooster'.

Figure 33. Plants with symptoms of downy mildew

<!-- image -->

The management of downy mildew consists of using clean seed free of the pathogen, removing previous crop residues, rotating with non-host crops, and covering the soil with organic matter (rice bran, corn husks or dead leaves) to reduce the effects of splashing caused by rain. In moderate attacks, the use of systemic fungicides is recommended; in severe attacks, chemical control is very costly. Although there are no varieties with high resistance to this disease, it is recommended to use tolerant varieties with an erect architecture to reduce the incidence and damage caused by the fungus, such as the CENTA EAC variety.

## b) Angular Leaf Spot or Bean Rust

This disease is mainly present in areas and times with moderate temperatures (16 to 28°C, optimal 24°C) and high relative humidity alternating with short periods of low humidity. The inoculum comes from the previous crop. Transmission can occur by contact of the seedling with the infected residue at the time of emergence, splashing from rain on the residue, or by spores of the fungus transported by the wind from neighboring fields. Seed transmission is relatively low, but represents a potential hazard depending on the level of damage to the pods.

Symptoms may initially appear on the primary leaves, and become generalized in the plants after flowering or the beginning of pod formation. When the lesions are well established on the foliage, they are typically angular on both sides of the leaves.

In severe attacks, the leaves turn yellow and die, resulting in premature defoliation of the plants. On the stem, branches and petioles, the lesions are brown-red, with elongated edges and dark centers. On the pods, the spots are oval or circular, with brown-red centers and sometimes reddish edges (Figure 34).

The prevention and control of angular leaf spot can be achieved by using clean seed from fields free of the disease, removing previous crop residues, and rotating with non-host crops. The application of fungicides should be carried out at the beginning of the attack, as a complement to the aforementioned measures. Although the level of resistance of commercial varieties is low, it is recommended to use improved varieties that tolerate the disease.

## c) Anthracnose

The infection and development of the pathogen are favored by cool temperatures (13-26°C, optimal 17-18°C), and high relative humidity in the form of moderate and frequent rainfall. The primary sources of inoculum come from crop residues, infected seeds, and diseased plants from neighboring plots. The spores are dispersed by rain and wind.

Anthracnose can affect any aerial part (except flowers) and at any stage of development; although severe symptoms are observed on petioles, leaves, and pods. On foliage, symptoms are visible along the veins of the leaf blade, consisting of brick-red to purple lesions. The lesions can be observed on cotyledons when the inoculum comes from the seed or from crop residues; and as the infection spreads, on petioles, stems, branches, and pods (Figure 35).

Infections on pods are common and appear as sunken, rounded, with margins delimited by black rings with reddish-brown edges.

The management of the disease is based on removing crop residues, using clean seeds, and applying chemical control with effective products and limited applications.

Most local varieties are susceptible and should not be planted in areas or during periods of high disease incidence.

Figure 35. Leaves and pods with symptoms of anthracnose

## d) Rust

Rust is a disease favored by moderate temperatures (17-27°C) and high relative humidity. The spores are transported over long distances by wind. Crop residues, and some weeds, can harbor the fungus (in the form of viable uredospores or teliospores) and serve as a primary inoculum. This fungus is not transmitted by seed.

The symptoms typically appear as circular chlorotic or yellowish spots, in which the typical symptoms then develop, which are the reddish-brown pustules (uremias) that appear on the leaves (Figure 36). Under severe infection and favorable conditions, the pathogen can develop several generations in a single growing cycle.

A pustule contains thousands of uredospores, and can be surrounded by a chlorotic or necrotic border, depending on the physiological race, the variety, and the environmental conditions. A severe infection can cause a reduction in yield.

The prevention and control of rust is carried out by removing crop residues and rotating with non-host crops. Chemical control is effective if carried out in the early stages.

It is recommended to use resistant varieties to this disease; however, genetic resistance may be relative due to the appearance of new physiological races of the disease as a consequence of the high variability of the causal agent.

## VII. HARVEST MANAGEMENT

## CUTTING, DRYING AND THRESHING

In the first season (May-June): the plants are harvested when they reach physiological maturity, are cut and then threshed or winnowed. If there is a lot of rain, they need to be dried under cover or in a shed, that is, the bunches of plants are placed upside down on wires placed on posts at a height of 1-1.5 m above the ground, to facilitate drying.

In the second season, the plants are cut and piled in rows with the roots facing up, for 1-2 days. Then, they are threshed in the same place to prevent loss due to scattering during transport. It is recommended to do this on tarpaulins or sacks to reduce losses. The moisture content of the grains reaches a level of 15%, at which point they acquire their typical color, although this can change during storage, depending on the variety. The biological cycle ends and the crop is then ready for harvest.

## VIII. POST HARVEST

## PRE-DRYING

Pre-drying is the drying of the beans in the field, this activity is carried out when the weather is dry and consists of harvesting the bean plant. If the weather is sunny, the pods will dry in 3 to 4 days, becoming brittle and ready for threshing.

The activity of pre-drying carries a high risk of crop loss due to rain when the plants are in contact with the soil, causing germination and contamination by fungi, resulting in loss of quality and viability.

## Drying technology in cords or stakes

This technology uses sunlight and wind to dry the harvested beans. It consists of hanging the beans on cords tied to stakes in the field. The experience of producers indicates that when it rains, the water drains easily.

Process: Once the plants have been harvested, they are cut, tied together, and hung (Figures 37 and 38).

## Technology for pre-drying of beans with plastic

**Process:** After the beans have matured, they are left to dry for about 7 days before harvesting. Then, they are grouped into several bundles. The width of the bundles should be such that the plastic covers them completely (1.5 meters). This harvest should be placed in the highest part of the field. If there is poor drainage, the harvest should be placed on straw, sticks, stones, or any drainage structure to prevent moisture loss.

The plastic is placed on the bundles, starting from the center and working outwards, leaving a 20 cm gap so that the beans can air out and the rain does not fall directly on them (Figure 39).

Wooden stakes are driven into the plastic on both sides. One side of the twine is tied to the plastic, and the other end is looped to allow it to be placed in the stakes, which makes it easier to uncover the plastic when it is not raining. If it is cloudy but not raining, the bundles should be uncovered to prevent overheating and to avoid affecting germination, especially if the beans are for seed.

Figure 39. Technology for pre-drying beans with plastic

<!-- image -->

To cover 1 meter of harvested beans with plastic, you need 40 meters of plastic. This plastic should be 1 mm thick and 2 meters wide.

## HARVESTING

Harvesting involves beating the dry plants with a wooden branch to allow the pods to open and release the beans. This activity is done on a sunny or cloudy day in a suitable area of the field. The beans are then threshed and bagged. For this activity, plastics, tarpaulins, or sacks are used (Figure 40). At the time of harvesting, the bean grain has an average moisture content of 18 to 20%, which needs to be dried further for sale or storage.

<!-- image -->

## DRYING

Once the beans have been threshed and dried, they must be transported to the producer's home, where they should be placed in the sun to reduce the moisture content to 13% (Figure 41). To do this, producers use black plastic tarpaulins. If the beans are stored with a moisture content of more than 14%, the grain will heat up, which can cause losses as the grain becomes wrinkled, develops mold, and pests develop, thus reducing the quality of the grain. Rapid drying (excessive drying) of the grain causes the grain to harden and physical damage, which can reduce the quality of the grain. It is important to avoid watering the bean grains directly in the soil, as this increases the risk of contamination and affects the quality and therefore the price of the grain, in accordance with the new market policies.

Figure 41. Drying of bean grains

<!-- image -->

## STORAGE

Once the beans are dried (13% moisture), they should be bagged for immediate sale. If they are to be stored for a few days, the bags should be stacked on wooden pallets and kept away from the wall to ensure adequate ventilation and prevent overheating, which can harden the grain.

To prevent insect damage to stored grain, producers have experimented with the following methods:

Applying ashes from cow manure to the bean bags, tying them up, and storing them. Alternatively, storing the beans with straw. It is recommended that the beans are well dried and that the bags are not moved until they are used. It is emphasized that the quality of the grain for storage is fundamental, as is the moisture content. If the grain is of low quality, it is preferable to sell it immediately. Storing the grain in metal silos (Figure 42) allows for larger volumes of grain to be stored in bulk and without the risks of moisture, insects, and rodents. While the grain is available in the market, phosphamine tablets (aluminium phosphate) can be used, with one tablet for every 4 quintals of beans. If stored in a metal silo, the tablet should be wrapped in a cloth and placed on the grain, and then sealed. If stored in a plastic bag, half a tablet wrapped in a strip should be placed in each bag, and then covered with plastic.

## IX. TECHNICAL GLOSSARY

Population density: The number of plants in a given area.

Runoff: The action of excess rainwater that flows downhill, carrying away soil particles.

Harvest estimation: A method for determining the production of a crop before harvest.

Physiological maturity: The stage of the plant when its grain has completed its development.

Micro elements: Nutrients that plants need in small amounts.

Host plants: Plants that attract and harbor certain insects and diseases.

Post-flowering: After flowering.

Pre-flowering: Before flowering.

Straw: Cut and dried vegetation left on the soil surface.

Sprinkling: The action of raindrops lifting soil particles.

Sandy soils: Soils with an equal proportion of sand, clay, and silt.

Susceptible: Having low defenses.

Texture: The arrangement of sand, clay, and silt in a soil.

Vigor: The ability of a seed to germinate and develop into a seedling.

Resistance: The ability of an organism to overcome, to a certain extent, the effect of a pathogen or other harmful factor.

Tolerance: The ability of a plant to withstand the effects of a disease without dying, suffering serious damage, or losing the crop. It is also the amount of toxic residues that can be tolerated in the edible parts of plants.
