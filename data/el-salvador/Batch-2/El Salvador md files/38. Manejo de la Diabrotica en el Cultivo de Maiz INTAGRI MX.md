## Managing Fall Armyworm in Corn Cultivation

Fall armyworm, also known as the larval stage as corn earworm, is a soil-dwelling pest that often damages along with the corn earworm. These are distinct species that make up the fall armyworm complex in corn, including *Diabrotica balteata*, *D. virgifera virgifera*, *D. virgifera zeae*, *D. longicornis*, and *D. undecimpunctata*. These species often attack other crops as well, which in turn serve as hosts in many cases. The importance of this pest in corn is so significant that yield reductions of 1 to 2.5 tons have been calculated, which in many regions represents 70% of the production. The distribution pattern of fall armyworm species is often similar, but in general, it has been reported that *D. balteata* predominates in tropical and subtropical areas, while *D. virgifera*, *D. longicornis*, and *D. undecimpunctata* are

## Damage

The damage caused by fall armyworm in early stages often necessitates replanting due to the high mortality rate of plants. When it attacks in later stages, the plant becomes weak and yellowish, and often falls due to lack of support, as its roots are damaged or its development is poor. It may even exhibit symptoms of water stress despite adequate moisture. The damage caused by this pest can be differentiated from that caused by the larvae and that caused by the adults.

Damage caused by the larvae. In the larval stage, fall armyworm primarily feeds on the roots of corn, especially the main roots, reaching a loss of 80 to 90% of the root system. In some cases, they can even bore into the stem. The damage caused by their feeding leads to the development of curved or tilted stems ('goose necks'), which predisposes the plants to lodging (making harvesting difficult) and possible root rot due to pathogens entering through the wounds caused. In addition, it causes a delay in development due to limited nutrient and water absorption, reducing yield and tolerance to drought.

Damage caused by the adult. Adult fall armyworms typically feed on the margins of the leaves and can penetrate young leaves. The damage to adult plant leaves is rarely economically significant, but this depends on the intensity of the attack, the age of the plant, and the stress the plant experiences at the time of the attack. These insects also feed on the stigmas and pollen, which can lead to poor pollination and, as a result, poor grain formation when there is a high level of infestation by adults (presence of up to 10 adults per stigma mass). After the pollination period, their damage is less relevant. On the other hand, the species *D. longicornis* and *D. virgifera*

are vectors of the virus that causes mottled chlorotic of corn and bacterial blight of corn.

The damage is more severe in years with high humidity or in the following year after a year with high humidity, as well as in areas that have been flooded. Fertile soils are also an indicator of potential problems with fall armyworm due to its attraction to early vegetation that develops in them, which is generally preceded by flooding or heavy rainfall.

## Life cycle

The life cycle of fall armyworm varies depending on the species, as well as on the temperature and humidity conditions that prevail. Various reports describe cycles ranging from 42 to 56 days from egg laying to adult oviposition.

Egg. Females can lay between 600 and 800 eggs during their lifetime. These eggs are white to yellow in color, measuring 0.6 mm in length and 0.35 mm in width. These eggs are laid in the soil near the roots of grasses and weeds, preferably in moist, organic, or clay soils. In some species, these eggs can remain dormant for up to 8 months, hatching in the first rains or heavy irrigations, when the corn plants are typically small.

Larvae. They have a soft, slender body, white to pale cream in color, with a head and the end of the body dark brown, with lengths ranging from 2 or 3 mm to 1.5 cm and widths of 0.2 to 0.51 mm. They reach maturity in 15 to 30 days, passing through three instars. In the last instar, they have two phases: an active phase and an inactive phase, known as the prepupa.

Pupa. The larva, after completing its development, stops feeding and enters the prepupal phase of its larval period, forming an earthen cell to pupate, remaining for 6 to 10 days in conditions of intermittent rain before the adult emerges. The pupa has a length ranging from 4 to 7.5 mm. The appearance of the pupa is that of an adult with wings and rudimentary legs.

Adult. Depending on the species, they can live from 26 to 70 days. They are of variable size, ranging from 6 to 9 mm, and their coloration corresponds to the species. They typically lay their eggs at depths of 10 to 30 cm, and depending on the species and latitude, they can have from one to three or four generations per year.

## Integrated Management

Since the greatest damage is caused by the larvae, which are found in the soil, the main control measures are directed towards this area. Controlling adults also helps to reduce the levels of infestation for the next cycle.

Sampling. It is recommended to carry out sampling before and after planting to detect the most appropriate time for control. Sampling is carried out in 30 x 30 x 30 cm plots, and the action threshold is to find 2 larvae in 10 sampling sites reviewed 25 days after planting. Adult sampling is carried out with yellow sticky traps placed on the top of the plant or on stakes. Applying a treatment is justified when an average of 5 adults per plant is observed, and the crop is flowering with less than 50% of plants. It is important to emphasize that even in the absence of fall armyworm, it is important to have safety measures in place.

Cultural control. Deep plowing before planting exposes the pest to natural enemies such as birds or solar radiation, and also allows the eggs laid on the surface to die by desiccation or cold during the winter. Also, crop rotation with non-host species is the best, avoiding mainly crops such as soybeans, in which fall armyworm can develop and increase its infestation. Eliminating crop residues or weed hosts, along with crop rotation, helps to break its life cycle and thus reduce infestation levels. Some authors recommend early planting and intercropping with beans to escape the severe damage caused by fall armyworm; however, the results of these studies have been shown to be inconsistent. A practice that takes advantage of the attraction of adults to corn flowering is the late planting of corn strips, which serve as a trap crop for its control, although the results have been shown to be inconsistent.

Biological control. As biological control agents, various articles report the use of the ant *Solenopsis geminata*; two parasitoids of adults, *Celatoria diabroticae* and *C. compressa*; and as predators, *Castolus tricolor*, *Repipta Taurus*, *Zelus spp.*, *Chauliognathus sp.*, among others. These natural enemies are not efficient controllers, but they help to reduce the population. Also, fungal entomopathogens for the control of fall armyworm have been reported, such as *Metarhizium anisopliae* and *Beauveria bassiana*, but their effectiveness has not been significant, but they open the way for further research on these organisms. The nematode *Heterorhabditis bacteriophora* has also been used successfully to control fall armyworm in some countries. Biological control of this pest is still in its early stages and requires further research on its effectiveness at the local level.

Radical bio-stimulation. Stimulating the development of the plant's root system can help to withstand the severe attacks of fall armyworm.

Chemical control. The use of insecticides with systemic action increases the effectiveness of controlling fall armyworm. Some insecticides used for its control are bifenthrin, chlorpyrifos, tebufos, tefluthrin, clotianidine. In practice, applying insecticides to the soil at planting is the simplest, most economical, and most reliable method for its control, as many times the control of the adult is more expensive because it requires applications by airplane or with tractors with high throw. Often, for the adult, a single treatment is not sufficient, so another treatment is required, which increases the cost of control.

Seed treatment. It is common to treat corn seeds before planting with products such as fipronil, imidacloprid, and tebupirimphos. However, the treated seed is more like a protection measure for the root than a tool to reduce the levels of this pest, so it is recommended as a method when fall armyworm levels are low or medium, as the effectiveness of the products, regardless of which one is used, is not always satisfactory for controlling the pest due to its dynamics in the soil, having an effective period of 20 days. From this period, other control measures must be implemented.

The use of one or another measure will depend on the population dynamics, the history of the land, and the agro-climatic conditions. It is important to emphasize that it is not necessary to wait to see symptoms to act on this insect, but a preventive strategy is needed that avoids yield reductions due to the damage caused by fall armyworm. It is also important to consider that the results of any control measure taken will be observed until the next cycle.
