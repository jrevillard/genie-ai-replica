## Introduction

Maize and beans are the staple crops for the Salvadoran family's food security. Approximately 383,000 manzanas (units of area) of maize are cultivated in the country, with an average yield of 48 qq/manzana, resulting in a total production of over 18 million quintals. El Salvador is one of the largest producers of maize in Central America per unit of area, but has not yet achieved self-sufficiency. Per capita consumption is around 80 kg/year, making it the country with the highest maize consumption in Central America, after Guatemala.

In El Salvador, since 2005, the agro-environmental conditions have not been as favorable for achieving satisfactory yields in basic grains. Among the biotic limitations that have negatively influenced maize cultivation, the foliar diseases caused by fungi, primarily the asphalt mold complex, rust, and leaf spot caused by Cercospora sp, are mentioned. The symptoms of these diseases in the field are often confused by technicians and farmers.

The country does not have a national guide to the main diseases of the crop, which allows technicians and farmers to identify them. Therefore, the purpose of this document is to provide a quick guide to identify and differentiate the asphalt mold complex from other foliar diseases that damage maize cultivation.

This guide compiles the experiences of researchers from the Basic Grains Program and the Plant Pathology Laboratory at CENTA, and has been reproduced thanks to the project 'Alternatives for managing the asphalt mold complex in maize cultivation in Central America' by the SICTA Network.

## ASPHALT MOLD DISEASE

In El Salvador, this disease has been the most economically significant in recent years, affecting corn crops, where it has appeared most frequently in municipalities such as San Juan Opico, Caluco, Sensuntepeque, Cojutepeque, Armenia, among others; areas located between 400 and 600 meters above sea level.

## 1) Causative Agent

It is a combination of three fungal microorganisms: Phyllachora maydis, Monographella maydis, and Coniothyrium phyllachorae.

Phyllachora maydis is a obligate parasite that cannot survive in dead tissue (stubble), requiring living tissue to develop. While Monographella maydis can survive for three months or more in dead tissue, but requires the presence of P. maydis to be pathogenic.

The disease begins in the first leaves, two to three days after infection, if the environmental conditions are favorable, the second fungus appears, and the disease can become uncontrollable.

## 2) Stages of Asphalt Mold Disease

Early stage: When the disease appears in very early stages, starting from 35 days after planting, the damage begins with the appearance of small, slightly raised black spots. At this stage, it is important to be very vigilant, as the infection can spread to the upper leaves and the rest of the crop; therefore, the disease must be controlled with the application of specific fungicides. At this stage of the crop, specialized equipment (preferably a motorized pump) is required for the application of the fungicide, while observing all safety measures.

Flowering period: If the disease appears during flowering, the yield can be seriously affected if the variety and/or hybrid is completely susceptible. The ears lose weight and the grains become flaccid and loose (Photo 6). At this stage of the crop, chemical control is not practical, it is uneconomical and risky for the operator's health.

## CONDITIONS THAT FAVOR THE DEVELOPMENT OF THE DISEASE

## 1) Climatic

In our country, the climatic factor that most influences the development of the disease is relative humidity above 75%. The literature reports that temperatures between 17 and 22°C favor the development of the disease, but in our area, during the crop cycle in corn areas, these temperatures are not observed.

## 2) Planting dates

Field experiences have shown that planting between May 15th and June 15th reduces the presence of the disease. Logically, the planting date is determined by the establishment of the rainy season. In areas below 100 meters above sea level, planting after June 15th does not favor the development of the disease.

## 3) Fertilization

Excessive fertilization with Ammonium Sulfate, Urea, or Ammonium Nitrate causes the crop to become very susceptible to the disease. It is recommended to perform soil analysis for proper fertilization.

## HOW TO PREVENT OR CONTROL THE DISEASE

## 1) Cultural Control

The International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center (CIMMYT, 2012) recommends crop rotation and incorporating weedy residue to prevent the presence of the disease in the next planting cycle. However, in the country, according to observations made by maize technicians from the Basic Grains Program at CENTA, these practices are not always effective in some areas, as in areas where the previous year's infection was severe, the following year did not occur. An example of this occurred in the town of San Cristóbal, in the Cuscatlán department, where there was no incorporation of weedy residue or crop rotation (photos 9 and 10); conversely, in areas where these practices were carried out, the disease appeared regularly.

For El Salvador, it is suggested to maintain planting dates mentioned earlier (May 15 to June 15) to reduce the risk of the disease appearing.

## DISEASES THAT FREQUENTLY CONFUSE WITH ASBALTO SPOT

In the country, other diseases are present that, frequently, technicians and/or farmers confuse with asfalto spot, such as:

## 1. Helminsthosporium sp. leaf blight

This disease begins with the appearance of small, slightly oval spots, easily recognizable, which then transform into necrotic and elongated areas that appear on the first leaves, just like asfalto spot. At the end, the lesions merge and can completely burn the leaves. If the infection occurs before or during flowering and the conditions are optimal, it can cause significant economic damage. The disease can even cause the complete burning of the foliage.

Photos 13 and 14. Different symptoms of Helminsthosporium leaf blight.

## 2. Cercospora sp. grey leaf spot

The lesions begin as small, regular, elongated, grey-brown spots, growing parallel to the veins. If the disease appears during flowering, it can affect the yield, as the grain filling is impaired. The infection can be increased by association with Helminsthosporium and the asfalto spot complex.

## 3. Curvularia sp. leaf spot

It appears as circular spots of 0.5 cm with a necrotic center and a yellow halo. This fungus produces small, necrotic or chlorotic yellow spots, observed throughout the leaf blade. A large number of translucent yellow spots are observed. Its effect on yield has not yet been evaluated.

Photos 16 and 17. Leaf spot caused by Curvularia sp

<!-- image -->

## 4. Stenocarpella (Diplodia) leaf spot

The fungus can affect the leaves and ears; the initial symptoms are irregular, dark brown spots with a yellow halo, in the center of the lesion, a circular, light brown spot can be seen, giving the appearance of a "chicken's eye"; as the infection increases, the spots advance on the leaf blade parallel to the veins, reaching up to 45 cm in length. The leaf damage caused by Stenocarpella can be confused with Helminsthosporium leaf blight. Stenocarpella can cause damage to the foliage, stem, and ear.

## 5. Tropical Rust caused by *Physophella maydis*

Since 2010, this disease has appeared more frequently in the country, although its economic importance has not yet been evaluated. It has been more prevalent in crops planted after June 15th, in areas between 400 and 600 meters above sea level. It can be associated with asphalt spot and is more aggressive, and can completely "burn" the foliage. The damage caused by tropical rust is unmistakable; experience shows that if it appears before or during flowering, the damage is significant. It causes premature leaf death and reduces yield.

## 6. Damage caused by mites (*Tetranychus urticae*)

Frequently, both technicians and farmers often confuse the damage caused by asphalt spot with that caused by mites, which can appear 25 to 30 days after planting, as well as after flowering. The symptom manifests as burned-looking leaves, with a bronzed yellow coloration, concentrated around the central vein, but without any lesions or spots. This mite is easily transported by humans from one crop to another, and its control is difficult, as it is found on the underside of the leaves (photos 22 and 23), and the products used to combat it are acaricides, not insecticides, as is commonly recommended in agricultural services. It is advisable to check "underneath" the leaves when these lesions ('scorched' or 'burned') appear, to ensure that they are caused by mites. Reports of damage have been received from coastal areas to 150 meters above sea level, and near sugarcane crops.
