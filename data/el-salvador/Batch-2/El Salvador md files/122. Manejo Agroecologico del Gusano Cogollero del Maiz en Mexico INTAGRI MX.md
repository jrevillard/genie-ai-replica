The earworm Spodoptera frugiperda (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) is an insect that has evolved alongside corn over the years and is present throughout most of the American continent, and particularly in Mexico. It has a strong preference for consuming grasses, such as corn and sorghum, but its host plants can be up to 186 species from 42 families (Casmuz et al., 2010).

Previously, it was considered a pest that only attacks the collar of corn plants in tropical and subtropical areas, generally below 1,600 meters above sea level. Now, it is possible to see this insect causing not only damage to the collar, but also defoliation of the corn plant, shredding or stripping its stems, and even consuming the silks; in addition, significant infestations can be found in temperate climates up to 2,200 meters above sea level, as has been observed in various regions of Mexico. If the conditions are favorable, such as temperature, food availability, absence of its natural enemies, and lack of management actions, this pest can cause losses of between 50 and 100% in crops like corn.

<!-- image -->

The damage is caused by the larvae, which can reach high populations due to the number of eggs that each female lays in the crop. These eggs are present from the moment the small corn plants emerge. It is possible for each female to lay several clutches in the crop, and each one can contain more than 200 eggs. Three to five days after the eggs are laid on the plant, the small larvae hatch simultaneously and disperse, causing damage at this stage that is not immediately noticeable.

The larvae go through six stages of development, each approximately 3.5 days long, the first three being the best stage for managing the pest in a simpler way and without highly toxic products. When the larvae complete their development, they emerge from the plant and fall to the ground, where they cover themselves with soil, build a cocoon, and form a pupa, which can remain for two weeks or several months, if the optimal conditions for their life cycle are not present. From the pupa emerges the female, ready to mate and restart its life cycle.

The larvae are recognizable by the presence of a 'Y' on their head in dorsal view, formed by lines of ecdysis, and by the presence of four black spots that form a trapezoid in the last abdominal segment, also in dorsal view (King and Saunders, 1984).

High temperatures, above 25 °C, and long periods of drought favor the excessive increase in the population of this insect. In addition, it is considered that where the conditions are ideal for the development of this pest, the most severe damage occurs due to limitations in the functioning of beneficial insects (parasitoids and predators); this is largely caused, first, by the loss of functional biodiversity, or in other words, by the promotion of extensive monocultures where all surrounding vegetation is eliminated, and, second, by the excessive (and often unnecessary) use of chemical pesticides.

Late actions and the widespread belief that chemical control is the only viable option for managing this pest have led to more problems than solutions, resulting in a dependence on producers to continuously apply treatments with synthetic chemical pesticides.

In nature, the populations of this pest are regulated by a wide range of beneficial organisms, including parasitoids, predators, and entomopathogens (Bahena and Cortez, 2015); however, wanting to make these organisms compatible with chemical insecticide treatments is contrary to what is defined and practiced as Integrated Pest Management (IPM). In this sense, the strategy for managing the earworm of corn includes actions that are applied simultaneously and have a holistic view of the problem that this pest represents.

<!-- image -->

The aim is to address the causes that increase its populations to prevent them from increasing excessively, and, on the other hand, to favor the activity of the beneficial organisms present in nature or those that may be introduced.

For IPM of the earworm, the following actions are suggested simultaneously: conservation tillage, permanent monitoring of the pest and the beneficial organisms present, use of traps with sexual attractants, replacing highly toxic and generalist pesticides with more friendly or low-impact alternatives, and the use of biopesticides that are low-impact environmental alternatives. In addition, it is recommended to restore functional biodiversity by using plants that serve as refuges or attract beneficial organisms.

Conservation tillage does not eliminate this pest, but it has been observed that there are better conditions for the presence of beneficial organisms of the pest and for less oviposition by the females (All, 1988).

Not tilling the soil and leaving crop residues on the surface, continuously and permanently, increases the biodiversity of microorganisms in the soil, which in turn favors a series of more complex interactions between harmful and beneficial organisms (Turnock et al., 1993; Valdés et al., 1993). Other consequences of conservation practices that have been observed are the reduction in the use of insecticides against the earworm when tillage is reduced and crop residues are incorporated (Roberts and All, 1993), and the increase in entomopathogenic organisms of the pest, such as Beauveria bassiana, Metarhizium anisopliae and Paecilomyces spp. (Sosa-Gómez and Moscardi, 1994).

Each trap should be checked (preferably) twice a week, counting and removing the captured caterpillars and replacing the water. It is advisable to avoid direct exposure of the pheromone capsules to sunlight and to use gloves when handling them. During each check, the number of captured caterpillars is recorded, and an average of captures over a fixed period of time (per day or per week) is obtained. This will allow you to create a graph of population fluctuations, where you can observe its behavior and establish maximum capture peaks, and, if applicable, determine whether it is appropriate to schedule the application of a control treatment in a more efficient way. The sexual pheromone of each trap should be replaced after 30 days of being placed in the field. There is no evidence that the color of the trap has any preference, and it is also not recommended to add anything besides water and a small amount of soap.

With the excessive use of synthetic pesticides, not only have pests been controlled, but some problems have also been generated, such as the development of resistance in insects, the elimination of beneficial entomofauna, increased production costs, health risks for workers and consumers, and environmental impacts. The chemical insecticides that cause the most undesirable effects are those belonging to the chlorinated, phosphated, carbamate, and pyrethroid groups, so many of them are currently prohibited in several countries.

It is important that the decision to apply treatments with any insecticide is justified based on a sampling of the insect population to be controlled, and not based on application schedules or as a preventative measure. On the other hand, when applying any treatment, it is necessary to know the association of the toxicological groups of each pesticide product and the level of toxicity of the same, in order to select the one that most effectively solves the problem, avoiding continuous applications with products belonging to the same toxicological group. In case of having to use a chemical insecticide, it is very important to follow these three recommendations: do not increase the dose, do not mix products, and do not repeatedly apply the same product (maximum three consecutive applications), even though it remains very effective in its results.

Currently, it is possible to replace the use of restricted pesticides in other countries, or those classified as highly toxic, with other products with a lower risk and less undesirable impact on people and beneficial entomofauna. Some alternative products that can be used to reduce high infestations of corn earworm are: spinetoram (Palgus), at a dose of 75 to 100 ml/ha; benzoato de emamectina (Denim), using 100 to 200 ml/ha; novalurón (Rimon), at a dose of 100 to 150 ml/ha; diflubenzurón (Dimilin), using 200 to 300 ml/ha; methoxyfenozide (Intrepid), using 125 to 170 ml/ha; tebufenozide (Fensor), at a dose of 250 ml/ha; indoxacarb (Avaunt), using 100 to 250 ml/ha; clorantraniliprol (Coragen), using 75 to 125 ml/ha; flubendiamida (Belt), at a dose of 75 to 100 ml/ha; benzoato de emamectina 50 (Proclaim), using 200 to 300 gr/ha; and spinosad (Spintor), using 300 to 500 ml/ha.

In all cases, any treatment should be carried out preferably during the early stages of development (larvae L1 to L3) and when a monitoring justifies the application, considering the economic threshold (between 15 and 20% infestation in the plants).

A recent review shows that the corn earworm can be naturally controlled by a wide range of beneficial organisms, in Mexico alone, 87 parasitoids, 45 predators and 13 entomopathogens (Bahena and Cortez, 2015) have been identified. This complex of organisms can attack practically at any stage of the pest's development.

The presence of a wide group of entomophagous organisms suggests that when it is necessary to carry out a treatment to reduce the pest population, this should be done by applying products that do not interfere with, or even favor, the activity of these beneficial organisms. It is recommended, as an indicator of the activity of natural biological control, to measure the parasitism in three stages of development of the crop, by collecting larvae, which should be a minimum of 100 individuals on the first 20 days after planting (first), at 40 days (second) and at 60 days (third).

In the sampling carried out to obtain parasitoids and to estimate the parasitism, the procedure is to collect and individualize larvae in the field, preferably of the same size and shape, and place them in a vial with a little food (maize or nettle leaves) and take them to the laboratory to monitor them until the possible emergence of the parasitoid. The collected and individualized larvae should be checked every two days, and the food should be replaced in each check. The number of healthy and parasitized larvae is counted, eliminating the dead larvae due to unknown causes. For each sampling date, the percentage of parasitism is calculated. To identify beneficial predators, the procedure is to observe them directly in the crop and to support it with bibliographic information. It is necessary to quantify and identify each group of predators observed, and it is important to have photographic evidence of the observation, particularly if the insect is observed preying. In this case, it is possible to observe several species of Coccinellidae, leafhoppers, dermapterans, neuropterans and spiders, among other groups.
