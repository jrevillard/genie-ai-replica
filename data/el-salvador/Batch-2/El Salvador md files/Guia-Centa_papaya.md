## I. IMPORTANCE

The cultivation of papaya (Carica papaya L) has seen growth worldwide in recent years, due to consumer demand for the fruit's nutritional, medicinal, and flavor properties. It is also a crop that offers income from the 7th month of transplanting, making it one of the most precocious fruits. This is further enhanced by the fact that social investment projects see this plant as a good alternative for food, options for diversifying farms, a source of employment, and high profitability. In 2016, El Salvador imported 17,253,267 kg, equivalent to $1,886,190, demonstrating an unmet demand and paving the way for continued promotion of this crop.

## ORIGIN

The first written mention of papaya is in Oviedo's "Natural and General History of the Indies," who, around 1535, in a letter to his sovereign, mentioned having seen this plant growing in southern Mexico and Central America.

In the early days of the conquest, it was quickly distributed throughout the Caribbean and South America. By the late 14th and early 15th centuries, it spread to the Philippines, Malaysia, southern China, Ceylon, and Hawaii, thanks to Spanish and Portuguese sailors. Today, it is cultivated in extensive areas throughout tropical and subtropical regions. In recent years, 50% of global production is concentrated in Brazil, Mexico, and India.

## ROOT

It has a main pivot root that can develop up to 1 meter in depth. Secondary roots develop in a radius of 80 cm, and the main concentration of absorbing roots is in the first 20 cm.

## STEM

The papaya tree is considered a shrub, with a hollow stem, except for the nodes. It can reach a height of 8 to 10 meters in 3 agricultural cycles and develop a diameter of 10 to 30 cm. The stem grows in a single axis, and each node contains a bud that can develop into a branch.

## LEAVES

Papaya leaves grow in simple, alternate pairs, and are palmate. The leaf blade measures between 25 and 75 cm and has 7 to 10 lobes. The petiole is long, reaching up to 125 cm in length, and its color can vary from green to purple depending on the variety. The papaya plant produces an average of 2 leaves per week, developing around 100 leaves in a year. A mature plant typically has around 30 functional leaves, and a minimum of 15 leaves is considered necessary for proper development.

## FLOWER

Papaya flowers are white, and grow on the stem near the leaf axils. They have 5 petals and 5 sepals. Pollination of female and hermaphrodite flowers occurs by wind and often by insects. Papaya develops three types of flowers: the female or pistillate flower, the male or staminate flower, and the hermaphrodite flower.

## FEMALE OR PISTILLATE FLOWER

They measure between 5 and 6.5 cm in length. They are found individually or in small clusters of 5 to 6 flowers, attached with short peduncles, and lack stamens. The ovary is ovoid, the stigma is divided, and the fruits derived from them are round or oval, with a pentagonal scar at the base. They are identified by being wide at the base and thin at the end (Figure 2).

Sometimes, according to environmental conditions, these flowers can develop into hermaphrodite flowers and produce non-commercial fruits.

## PENTANDRICA HERMAPHRODITE

The corolla is composed of 5 petals joined at the base. The ovary is well developed, globular (Figure 4), and has 5 lobes, and has the same shape as a female flower. ## INTERMEDIATE HERMAPHRODITE

This is an intermediate type with 2 to 10 stamens, placed irregularly in the corolla tube and originating from the inner part of the petals. The filaments fuse with the ovary wall and produce fruits of various shapes and low commercial value in some cases.

## PERFECT OR ELONGATED HERMAPHRODITE

This is the most common hermaphrodite flower, and its corolla is formed by 5 petals joined in the lower third of its length. It has stamens arranged in two series of 5 each, attached to the middle part of the corolla. This flower is narrow at the base and then widens to end in a point (Figure 5). The fruits from this flower are elongated, smooth on their first half, and slightly lobed towards the tip. These fruits are of good quality, fleshy, and with a smaller internal space than round or lobed fruits.

Although the basic sex of the papaya is genetically determined, some trees can experience sexual reversals to varying degrees, caused by seasonal changes in the climate. This is common, and you can often find trees producing elongated fruits that, when exposed to high or low temperatures, high or low relative humidity, begin to produce round fruits. This is not a change in sex, but rather a change from the elongated type to the pentandric type.

## TYPES OF PLANTS

According to the type of flower, there are three types of plants: female, male, and hermaphrodite.

## FEMALE PLANTS

These produce female flowers, requiring the presence of pollen from other plants for fertilization and seed formation. Fruit formation occurs even without pollination, due to the phenomenon of parthenocarpy.

## MALE PLANTS

These produce male flowers, which are found on an elongated peduncle. These flowers have a rudimentary ovary that can become functional at some point and produce very small fruits.

## FRUIT

The papaya fruit is a berry, which can be cylindrical, elongated, pear-shaped, or globular oval or round. The shape of the fruits depends on the variety and the type of flower from which they are formed. According to the varieties, the fruits can reach a length of 15 to 50 cm, a diameter of 12 to 25 cm, and a weight of 1.5 to 15 pounds or more.

The fruit is composed of 3 parts:

- a-The exocarp or skin.
- b-The mesocarp or pulp.
- c-The endocarp, which contains the seeds and mucilage.

The pulp is rich in water, sugars, vitamins, minerals, and coloring substances. Its color varies from pale yellow to reddish yellow.

## SEED

It consists of a small, laterally flattened embryo and is surrounded by the endosperm, as well as a covering formed by a hard, waxy endocarp and a translucent sarcocarp containing a thin, mucilaginous fluid. Each fruit can produce between 300 and 800 seeds, which have a spicy taste and a considerable amount of yellow fat. The weight of 100 seeds is 2.11 g.

## USAGE

In El Salvador, papaya is consumed as fresh fruit, in smoothies, and on a smaller scale in desserts, produced in an artisanal way. However, it has great potential for industrialization in the pharmaceutical, culinary, medical, brewery, and non-alcoholic beverage industries.

papain, pectin, extracts, oils, various medicines, nectars, preserves, honey, jelly, dried fruit, jams, juices. It is also used in medical treatments for gastric and duodenal insufficiency, for the production of culture media, as a meat tenderizer, as a gum softener, in expectorant syrups, and for clarifying beers, among others.

## III. CLIMATIC AND GROWING REQUIREMENTS

Papaya adapts to latitudes between 32 and 35 degrees north and south, in tropical and subtropical zones, which correspond to warm areas located from sea level to 1000 meters; however, the best yields and quality of fruits are obtained between 30-600 m above sea level. As papaya is grown at higher altitudes, the fruits are less sweet due to a reduced ability to convert sugars.

The optimal temperature ranges between 18 and 38°C, with a relative humidity of 60 to 85%. The annual water requirement is 1,500 to 2,000 mm, distributed over the twelve months, but since there are only six months of rainfall, it must be planted under irrigation. Due to the high water content of the fruits and the constant formation of these, it must be provided with water throughout the year to ensure a continuous harvest. In addition, the plant requires high light intensity for the fruits to reach a desirable sugar content, so it should not be grown in the shade of other plants that could restrict the entry of sunlight.

Wind is a factor to consider, in places with strong winds, there is a risk that the plant will break due to the weight of the fruits and the fact that the pseudostem is hollow.

Adequate light is fundamental to achieve fruits of excellent quality: taste, color, and aroma, therefore, it is not recommended to cultivate it in the shade of other plants that could restrict the entry of sunlight. Excessive solar radiation is also detrimental, as it causes burns on the fruits, which reduces their commercial value.

This plant grows very well in soils with a sandy texture, although it can be grown in any other type of soil, provided it has a minimum depth of 0.50 m, good water retention capacity, and ease of removing excess water. For external drainage, plots with slight slopes are suitable; the roots of papaya are very susceptible to death due to lack of oxygen, therefore, the importance of drainage. In addition, it is favorable that the soil pH is between 5.5 and 7.5 and has a good organic matter content.

## IV. CULTIVATION ZONING AT THE NATIONAL LEVEL

In El Salvador, some areas have been characterized by producing fruits of excellent quality, such as the coastal strip from Cara Sucia in Ahuachapán to La Unión, also in the northern department of Chalatenango, in San Miguel, and in other areas of the country. In the map (Figure 9) of the cultivation zoning, the areas destined for coffee cultivation, areas above 800 m above sea level, and human settlements have been excluded, and it can be seen that a large percentage of the national territory is suitable for its cultivation. It is not recommended to plant in areas where cucurbitaceae are cultivated, as these serve as hosts for the papaya ring spot virus and are asymptomatic.

Currently, in the country, there are hybrids with a high potential for yield and good fruit quality, the Creole varieties, such as Izalco, have been replaced by introduced hybrids from Taiwan and have gradually gained acceptance among consumers, eventually dominating the formal and, more recently, the informal markets.

## VI. PROPAGATION METHODS

The most common method is by seed, creating nurseries for subsequent transplanting. Currently, asexual reproduction through tissue culture using lateral buds is also being used. This technique allows for the production of 100% hermaphrodite plants.

To develop this technique, explants are obtained from hermaphrodite mother plants, which are healthy and have good genetic quality. Subsequently, in the laboratory, artificial conditions are provided to promote mass multiplication, followed by rooting (Figure 16) and then acclimatization or adaptation of the seedlings (Figure 17) for their corresponding transplanting in the field.

## VII. NURSERY DEVELOPMENT

## PLANTING SEASON

Due to the recommendation to cultivate under irrigation, the planting date can be scheduled to ensure a harvest when there is a limited supply, which occurs during the months of March to July. Therefore, transplanting should be done in September, and the nursery should be planted in July. Other factors should also be considered, such as the fact that some months are more rainy, with frequent continuous rainfall for up to four days, which can lead to the loss of many plants. It is also important to evaluate the high risk of having small plants during the peak season of insect vector diseases.

## NURSERY LOCATION

The nursery should be located at least 1.5 km away from any papaya plantation, to reduce the risk of plants becoming infected with a viral disease. The land should have water for irrigation, sufficient sunlight, and natural barriers to prevent the entry of insect vectors. It is very important to take into account that the papaya plant in its early stages is very susceptible to water with a very high electrical conductivity, therefore, an analysis of the water should be carried out in laboratories that perform this test, such as the Agricultural Chemistry of CENTA.

## TYPES OF SUBSTRATES TO USE

The substrate is the material or mixture of soil in which the seed is planted. The management mainly refers to the irrigation regime, which is inextricably linked to the physical properties of the medium, the water requirements of the plants to be cultivated, and the climatic conditions in which they develop. The recommended proportions are 1:1:1, that is, 33% sand, 33% organic matter (cow manure, well-decomposed leaves, dry, sifted, and disinfected), and 33% sandy soil. Commercial substrates for seed germination based on coconut or peat (Figure 18) can also be used, which has the advantage of being sterile, but it must be supplemented with fertilizer for better seedling development, otherwise, it will grow very weakly.

## SUBSTRATE DISINFECTION

It can be done using different methods: a) solar, by placing the substrate directly under the sun; b) thermal, with hot water; and c) chemical, which is the most used because it is less complicated. The products Propamocarb 72 SL and Carbendazim 50 SC are used in doses of 1-2 cc of product per liter of water.

## FILLING BAGS AND FORMING ROWS

The recommended bags are 6'x9', they should be filled completely, then placed in the location where the nursery will be established (Figure 19), under a microtubing mesh and covered with straw to control the amount of light (Figure 20). The arrangement and placement of the bags should allow for free access for the necessary cultural operations for the nursery, leaving a space of 40 to 60 centimeters between the rows. The width of the row is normally 80 to 120 centimeters and the length up to 20 meters. With that width and a length of 1 meter, you have 180 plants. It is recommended to place black plastic under the bags to prevent the roots from penetrating the soil when they emerge.

## PRE-GERMINATION OF THE SEED

This activity allows for the production of a nursery with uniform plants and at the same time accelerates germination. This method consists of placing the seed in water for 48 hours, changing the water every 6-8 hours. After 48 hours, the seeds are removed and placed in damp cloths, previously boiled to eliminate pathogens, wrapped in black plastic to isolate them from light, and placed in a warm place. As the seeds show a radicle of 1 to 2 cm in length, they should be sown in the bags with a thin pin, at a depth of 3 cm (Figure 21). This occurs, depending on the variety, between 4-10 days after placing the seeds in water.

## DISEASE AND PEST PREVENTION.

The main problem in the nursery is the seedling blight caused by a complex of fungi. Pre-disinfection helps to minimize this problem, but applications are necessary after the seedling germinates. The products to use are: Propamocarb 72 SL plus Carbendazim 50 SC at a ratio of 1-2 cc of each per liter of water, for foliar diseases, you can apply Copper oxychloride 50 WP, 3 g per liter of water, Mancozeb 40 SC, 3 g per liter of water, these products can be applied every 7-10 days.

To prevent insect attacks that transmit viruses, it is recommended to apply Thiamethoxam 25 WG every 21 days. 13 g per 4-gallon pump and protect with insect mesh. This ensures that the plants that reach the final field are not contaminated with any virus.

## FERTILIZATION

To ensure that the plants are vigorous and of good quality, it is recommended to fertilize with a 12-12-17-2 formula, which also contains sulfur, calcium, boron, and zinc. Place 4 to 6 granules per plant.

## VIII. PLANTING IN THE FIELD

Planting should be done on raised beds or beds that are 30 cm high, which facilitate the flow of water towards the drainage between rows.

The most common spacings are: Between plants: 2.0 m to 2.5 m Between rows: 2.0 m to 2.5 m (figure 21)

In addition, when planting large areas, the double-row system can be used to mechanize the work, and the spacings are 3.8 between rows x 1.8 x 1.8 m between plants.

## PLANTING SYSTEMS

Papaya can be grown as a monoculture or in association with other fruit trees, vegetables, and basic grains, taking into consideration that in the case of fruit trees such as peach, mango, and anon, these will be the main crops and papaya will be adjusted to the spacing of these. Regarding vegetables, it is advisable to avoid cucurbits as they are hosts for the ring spot virus, and those crops with a long cycle, as the papaya plant will prevent sunlight from reaching the leaves of these.

## SOIL PREPARATION

The soil should be prepared at least 15 days before transplanting. It should be mechanized when the slope allows, in order to ensure that the soil is well-loam, loose, free of weeds, and that the soil pests are exposed to the sun and natural enemies. For this, two passes of heavy tillage and one harrowing can be done to remove furrows and depressions that favor waterlogging. In the case of slopes or other areas with slopes, it is recommended to follow soil conservation practices such as the construction of furrows, lines following level curves, etc. Then, beds or raised beds are constructed in which the holes of 0.40 x 0.4 x 0.4 m are made.

## TRANSPLANTING

The appropriate size for transplanting is when the seedling has reached a height of 12-15 cm, or at 5 weeks after sowing the seeds. It should be slightly above the soil level, so that when compacted or after rain or irrigation, the hill does not sink, as this will facilitate waterlogging around the stem, creating the optimal conditions for the development of fungi that cause seedling blight (figure 22). In the bottom of the hole, place two ounces of 18-46-0 fertilizer, 16 ml/liter of water of a botanical nematicide based on marigold 80% and 10 pounds of organic matter. At the time of adding the soil to the hole, add another two ounces of the fertilizer. Immediately after transplanting, apply a dose with a backpack sprayer, directed at the base of the plant, with a mixture that contains Propamocarb 72 SL (1.5 cc/liter of water) plus Carbendazim 50 SC (1.5 cc/liter of water) and Thiametoxan, 25 WG (0.6 grams/liter of water.

## IX. FERTILIZATION

The papaya crop requires high levels of fertilization and short application frequencies due to its constant growth, flowering, and fruit production, which will ensure uninterrupted production (figure 23).

To prepare the fertilization plan, a complete soil analysis should be carried out, in order to first determine the pH, a characteristic that will allow you to choose the fertilizers that can offer the best results in that type of soil; secondly, the elements to apply to supplement any deficiencies. At the same time, if the pH is below the minimum allowed for the crop, a product should be applied to raise the pH to a less harmful value; among these are Cal Dolomite, Calcium Hydroxide and Magnesium, and Gypsum. The Soil Laboratory at CENTA will recommend the one that is most suitable for the soil, as well as the amount to apply and the time to apply it.

## FUNCTIONS AND SYMPTOMS OF DEFICIENCIES OF THE MAIN NUTRIENTS

## NITROGEN

Fundamental for vegetative growth, provides the intense green color of the plants, activates rapid growth, increases the production of leaves, is a component of chlorophyll, which allows photosynthesis. It is a component of RNA and DNA.

Technical Guide

Its deficiency causes low yields, loss of green color of the leaves towards yellow, premature maturity. Excessive application of this element makes the fruits less consistent and the plant has less resistance to the attack of pests.

## PHOSPHORUS

Essential for cell division, it provides energy during photosynthesis and carbohydrate transport, facilitates the rapid formation and growth of roots, stimulates seed formation, and helps with fruit set. A deficiency is manifested by delayed flowering and low fruit and seed production. An excess can cause the fixation of elements such as zinc in the soil.

## POTASSIUM

This is the most important nutrient in terms of quantity and quality for production. It actively participates in the process of cell division, regulating sugar availability, and is involved in the absorption of Ca, N, and Na. It also contributes to vigor and resistance to diseases and low temperatures, helps in protein production, and is responsible for the transport of sugars from the leaves to the fruit. Its importance increases during flowering, as it significantly affects the sugar content of the fruit.

A deficiency is shown as necrosis on the margins and tips of older leaves, low plant yield and stability, low-quality fruits, and increased post-harvest losses. An excess can lead to the blockage of magnesium and calcium fixation.

## CALCIUM

This is an essential nutrient in cell walls, regulating nutrient absorption, promoting root growth and multiplication, and, along with potassium, is responsible for giving fruits greater consistency. This element has low mobility in the xylem and even lower in the phloem, meaning that foliar applications are not very effective. A deficiency is shown in young leaves. A deficiency causes fruits to be of poor consistency and therefore have a shorter shelf life.

When analyzing the curves, it is observed that absorption of all nutrients starts in an ascending manner after 120 days after transplanting.

According to researchers, from 360 days onwards, the requirements tend to be less ascending, which is why a fertilization program with the same trend should be carried out to achieve better nutrient use, without losing sight of the need to support it with soil analysis.

The fact that some elements are absorbed less than others should not be a reason not to pay attention to them. It is important to remember that if a nutrient is below the normal level, all processes will be affected and the maximum genetic potential of the plant will not be achieved.

## REMOVAL OF SUCKERS, BRANCHES OR SHOOTS

Papaya plants tend to produce lateral shoots in the axils of the leaves, which can form fruits. If this happens, all the fruits are smaller, both on the main stem and on the shoots. Suckers are hosts for mites, for these reasons, it is recommended to remove them when they are small, and it is better to do this by hand (Figure 27), as using a cutting tool can spread viral diseases.

## PRUNING OR THINNING OF FRUITS

When the harvest is intended for consumption as fresh fruit, and the market demands quality, it is necessary to remove excess fruits, leaving only one in each axil. This allows them to be larger and without deformations due to being too crowded, and also allows for better air circulation between the fruits, which reduces the incidence of anthracnose (Figure 28).

Figure 28. Thinning of fruits.

<!-- image -->

If the market requires small fruits, then it is not necessary to remove them, but the risk of increasing diseases increases.

## PRUNING OR DE-LEAFING

The oldest leaves of the papaya plant should be removed, as they are hosts for insects and inoculum for many diseases. During the rainy season, they create a favorable microclimate for the development of diseases and make it difficult to carry out fumigation in the fruit column.

To remove the leaves, only the leaf lamina should be removed, leaving the petiole attached to the stem (figure 29), which will detach on its own later. Knives or scissors should not be used, as using these tools increases the risk of transmitting the virus from diseased plants to healthy plants. This work should be done manually.

The leaves should be removed to a height that prevents direct sunlight from falling on the fruit, as this causes scorching. The cut leaves should be removed from the plantation and sprayed with a fungicide-acaricide to prevent them from becoming a source of inoculum.

Once the plant is infected, there is no cure, so preventative measures must be taken to reduce the incidence of the virus in the plantation. Some of the species used as live hedges include maize and forage sorghum, which are more attractive to aphids than papayas (figure 30).

It is also advisable to plant Jamaican rose around the plantation, as its coloration is repellent to insects. Live hedges should be planted before transplanting and should be renewed before they dry out, in order to maintain them throughout the productive cycle.

## STAKING OR SUPPORTS

When the plants have a large number of fruits, and the soil is too wet due to rain or irrigation, the plants may topple over. To prevent this, supports such as stakes or wooden poles should be placed. Cord or nylon can also be used to tie the plants together or to a stake fixed in the ground (figure 31).

A common practice is to support the plants by leaning them against a wall, but only when they are mature, as the accumulation of soil in the trunk can cause rot in the early stages of growth.

## SEXING

This activity involves removing plants that are not desired, which are the female plants, as they produce round fruits, and leaving the hermaphrodite plants, which produce long fruits. To carry out this activity, it is necessary to plant two plants per position (figure 32), and until the flowering stage, it will be possible to determine the sex of the plant by following the descriptions in the section on flowers.

If you use seeds from local varieties, you will need to plant three plants, as these produce a high percentage of male plants if not manually pollinated.

It is very important to observe that the plants can bend due to competition for space, so a support and tying system should be installed, but it should be checked periodically to avoid strangulation.

## WEED CONTROL

Weeds, in addition to competing with the crop for water, light, and nutrients, are reservoirs of pests and a source of inoculum for some diseases. Their control is important in the early stages of the crop, as the shade from the papaya plants limits their development later on.

Weed control can be carried out manually and chemically. When using manual control, it is important to avoid damaging the roots to prevent the penetration of pathogens (figure 33).

Figure 33. Manual weed control in a papaya plantation.

Herbicides are used after one month of transplanting to prevent any negative effects. If the weeds are newly germinated or have 2-3 true leaves, residual herbicides such as Diuron 80 WP can be used, at the maximum doses for heavy soils and minimum doses for light soils.

For weeds with more than 3 true leaves, the herbicide should be mixed with a post-emergent herbicide, taking care to direct the application to the weeds and avoid spraying the papaya plant.

To ensure that herbicides are effective in controlling weeds, the following recommendations should be followed:

- At the time of application, the soil should have sufficient moisture, which is necessary for the herbicide to penetrate to the depth where the roots are.
- Distribute the herbicide evenly, depending on the product, in the soil and/or on the foliage.
- Do not disturb the soil after applying residual herbicides.

## XI. INTEGRATED PEST MANAGEMENT

## INSECTS

PAPAYA WEEVIL

_Taxotrypana curvicauda_

Family: Tephritidae

Order: Diptera

## LIFE CYCLE

The adult is a large fly, measuring 20 to 26 mm in length for females, and 12 mm for males. The female has a long, curved ovipositor that is as long as the body (figure 34). The appearance of these insects is similar to that of a wasp, with narrow, long wings and a brown spot.

After mating, the females seek out papaya fruits for reproduction, inserting their long ovipositor into the fruit and laying their eggs in groups of 10 or more, depositing a total of 100 eggs during their lifetime.

The eggs are elongated and are laid between the seeds. They incubate for 12 to 14 days. The larvae are apodal, whitish or pale yellow, and can reach a length of 10 mm. The larvae develop over a period of 14 to 16 days.

To pupate, the larvae leave the fruits and burrow into the soil to create a light brown puparium, measuring 8 to 9 mm in length and oval in shape. The pupal stage lasts for 14 to 20 days. The entire life cycle lasts between 45 and 55 days.

## HABITS AND DAMAGE

The adult prefers to rest and feed on nearby trees. It is nocturnal, which makes it difficult to observe in the field. When observed, it is typically seen early in the morning and at dusk. It appears to dislike the heat of the day.

For reproduction, it prefers small fruits with a diameter of 8.5 to 8 cm, although it can lay its eggs from the moment the petals of the flowers fall until the fruit is ripe. As a result of the oviposition punctures, the fruit exudes white latex, causing premature ripening and easy detachment from the plant.

The larvae feed on the developing seeds and then on the pulp, causing rotting and fruit drop. The main means of spread is through infested fruits, as the females do not fly very far.

## CONTROL METHODS

To control this pest, a combination of methods is necessary to achieve effective control.

## PEST MONITORING

It is necessary to implement monitoring to detect the presence of this pest in a timely manner. It is recommended to do this when small fruits begin to form (approximately 5 cm in diameter). A total of 50 plants can be inspected per manzana (a unit of measurement in the region). Monitoring involves observing "shot" fruits with latex, yellow-orange spots in the areas where the fly lays its eggs (premature ripening), and fruits on the ground. If 2% of the damaged fruits are found, chemical control should be implemented.

Figure 34. On the left, an adult female papaya weevil; in the center, evidence of oviposition; and on the right, false ripening caused by the larva emerging from the fruit.

## GUIDELINE

## CHEMICAL CONTROL

When 2% of the fruits are damaged, apply treatments to healthy, small fruits using products such as Deltamethrin 2.5% EC at the dosage recommended by the manufacturer.

## CULTURAL CONTROL

This involves collecting fallen or damaged fruits to eliminate the larvae inside. If this practice is carried out continuously, it prevents the larvae from emerging from the fruits and pupating in the soil, thus interrupting the cycle.

Another important aspect of cultural control is the use of traps to capture the adults. Traps can be made from plastic containers with a capacity of 2 liters (figure 35), with 3 windows cut into the middle part, each 5 cm high by 3 cm wide. The inside of the trap is filled with a sweet solution of brown sugar. Trials conducted by the Fruit and Cocoa Program have shown that this material is more attractive to papaya weevils than ripe papaya juice.

Figure 35. Lerafal trap used for capturing papaya weevils.

## APHIDS OR MITE FAMILIES: Aphididae ORDER: Homoptera

In El Salvador, the following aphids have been reported as vectors of the annular spot virus, and depending on the species, they can vary in color: _Aphis citricola_, _A. cracivora_, _Aphis illinoisensis_, _A. middletoni_ and _Rhopalosiphum maidis_. In other countries, _A. gassipii_, _A. nerii_, _Mlyzus persicae_ and others have also been reported.

## LIFE CYCLE

The winged females of aphids (figure 36) can visit papaya plants from the early stages of growth. They reproduce parthenogenetically and viviparously, meaning that they only give birth to live females. The duration of a generation depends on the temperature and can be as long as 10 days in warm climates. A single female can give birth to up to 100 nymphs at once.

## HABITS AND DAMAGE

The adults and nymphs live in colonies on the underside of the terminal leaves of the host plants. In the case of papaya plants, they are not particularly attractive for colonization. When they suck sap, they inject a toxic saliva that causes curling of the leaves, reducing the vigor of the plant. As they suck, some of the sap falls onto the surface of the leaf, which serves as a substrate for the development of a fungus called _Capnodium sp_, which affects the normal function of photosynthesis in the plants.

The main damage caused by aphids is the transmission of the papaya annular spot virus (VMAP), from older plants or from host plants such as cucurbits.

The aphids acquire the virus by feeding on infected plants for only a few seconds, and they carry it attached to the ends of their mouthparts. When they move to another plant, they transmit the virus. The retention of the virus in the insect is very short, and it remains infectious for a few minutes to a few hours.

## CONTROL METHODS

Control should aim to prevent the transmission of the virus to the plant, and for this, a set of alternatives must be implemented to make it difficult for the aphids to access the crop.

## CULTURAL CONTROL

- Regularly remove weeds, especially broadleaf weeds like cucurbits, from around the crop, thereby reducing populations of aphids and sources of viruses.

RHINOCEROS BEETLE: Rhynchophorus palmarum Family: Curculionidae Order: Coleoptera

## HABITS AND DAMAGE

This is a pest that eventually causes problems in the papaya crop. The females create holes at a height of one meter from the stem, in which to lay their eggs, from which the larvae develop, which can reach a length of about 6 cm. They bore into the trunk lengthwise, mainly towards the root zone.

## LIFE CYCLE

Mites have a short life cycle and their populations increase rapidly. The eggs of these arachnids are spherical, placed, generally one at a time on the underside of the leaves. The female lays 4 to 6 eggs per day, for a period of one month. They hatch in about 3 days. The nymph stage goes through 3 stages: in the first, it has 3 pairs of legs and 4 pairs in the remaining ones. They complete their nymphal phase in 5 to 10 days.

Adults measure from 0.5 to 0.7 mm in length and can have a green, red, or orange color. The time it takes to complete a generation is between 9 and 21 days, and they are generally dispersed by the wind.

## HABITS AND DAMAGE

Adults and nymphs feed by sucking sap, mainly on the underside of the leaf; they are found near the main and secondary veins and live under a silk web when numerous.

Due to their feeding, they cause a white, yellowish, or brownish spotting, which can distort and curl the edges of the leaves upwards. This symptom can be mistaken for viral disease (Figure 38). In the fruits, they cause scarring and reduce their commercial value.

## VIRAL DISEASES

## · PAPAYA RING ROT

This is the most important disease of papaya worldwide. It is transmitted by various species of aphids, and is considered to be the same disease known as "mosaico deformador de la hoja" (leaf deformation mosaic). It manifests as the appearance of chlorosis (yellowing) in young leaves, followed by a mottled yellow and green appearance, and the clearing of leaf veins. The leaves show dark green spots at the base of the petioles, giving them a "monkey hand" appearance (figure 39). In addition, the leaves may also show variable, oily spots. The fruits show concentric, half-moon-shaped, oily rings with a diameter of approximately 1 to 2 cm.

As the disease progresses, the number of leaves produced decreases, and they become shorter than normal. The number of fruits also decreases.

[Image of papaya ring rot]

[Image of papaya ring rot]

Figure 39. Ring-shaped spots on the fruit, "monkey hand" symptom in the center, and green spots on the petioles.

The fruits may also fail to develop properly, resulting in very small fruits.

In addition to papaya, the virus also infects 17 other plant species from 3 families (Caricaceae, Chenopodiaceae, and Cucurbitaceae). Infected papaya and cucurbit plants are the most important in the spread of the virus.

After inoculation with the virus, which is a potyvirus, it takes 2 to 6 weeks for the first symptoms to appear, depending on the age of the plant, environmental conditions, and the plant's resistance level. The disease is not transmitted through seeds, and aphids do not feed on trees with recent pruning, so in some areas, the practice of removing infected plants is not carried out.

## MANAGEMENT MEASURES FOR VIRUS DISEASES

Apply a growth stimulant (3.46% Amino Acids + 0.97% Cytokinins) preventatively at a rate of 50 cc per plant, and 100 cc per 4-gallon pump when there is a virus outbreak. Repeat the application 3 and 5 days after the first application, then apply monthly, to improve the plant's condition and help it resist the infection caused by the virus.

## VECTOR CONTROL

Use potassium salts of fatty acids from palm oil at a concentration of 50% in a 90-115 cc per 4-gallon pump, and Thiamethoxam 25 WG at a concentration of 10 g per 4-gallon pump.

A control method used in Hawaii and Florida involves using plants that are biologically protected with attenuated virus strains. This is a form of cross-protection, achieved by inoculating healthy papaya plants with a weakened strain of the virus, derived from a mutation of a severe native strain, using the treatment with nitrous acid. This strain has the ability to reduce or delay the attack of severe strains.

Modern plant breeding techniques have allowed for the development of genetically modified hybrids that are resistant to the disease, and their use has allowed areas that were previously abandoned to become productive again, specifically in Hawaii. In El Salvador, genetically modified organisms (GMOs) are not authorized.

## PREVENTION MEASURES FOR VIRAL DISEASES

The following measures should be taken to reduce the damage caused by viruses:

- Use seeds from healthy plants, preferably obtained from plantations where the disease is not present.
- Protect the nursery with aphid-proof netting and apply systemic insecticides against sucking insects.
- Use healthy plants when establishing the plantation.
- Maintain a minimum distance of 1.5 km from plantations with symptoms of the disease.
- In nurseries and plantations, use live barriers of maize or sorghum, in addition to rose of Jamaica, which is a repellent.
- Remove infected plants
- Destroy wild papaya and cucurbit plants near the crop.
- Maintain good weed control to prevent them from serving as a refuge for vectors.
- Strengthen fertilization and maintain adequate soil moisture.
- Disinfect tools with sodium hypochlorite.

XII. HARVEST AND POST-HARVEST

## HARVEST

Papaya fruit is climacteric, meaning that when it reaches maturity, there is a rapid increase in the rate of respiration and ethylene release from the fruit.

The fruit is ready for harvest when the color of the epidermis changes from a dark green to a light green, forming areas with yellow pigments that subsequently turn into yellow lines from the apex to the peduncle (Figure 47). The first areas to ripen are the internal parts, starting with the seeds and the placenta; subsequently, the yellow areas on the outside appear, which gradually expand to cover the entire fruit.

Figure 47. Fruits with yellow lines.

<!-- image -->

The degree of maturity for harvest will depend on the market requirements; some prefer two yellow lines, others prefer three, four, or the entire distal area yellow, while others prefer a completely yellow fruit. It is also important to consider the distance to the market, although El Salvador is a small country where you can travel from east to west in a single day.

One thing that should never be forgotten is that all the bumps that the fruit receives from when it is cut from the plant to when it is delivered to the consumer will greatly influence its shelf life and the physical and visual quality of the fruit. Therefore, all necessary measures should be taken to avoid bumps during transport within the farm, during washing and treatment, during conditioning in the vehicle, and during handling, without making sudden movements, and during delivery at the market, among others. When the fruit is cut, it should be wrapped in newspaper to prevent mechanical damage to the skin, as these accelerate ripening and are a point of entry for fungi that damage the fruit.

In the washing area, there should be a container with clean water, which should be changed frequently as it becomes cloudy due to field dirt from the fruits. Then, the fruits are transferred to another container with 10% bleach, and finally to a third container that contains a fungicide such as Carbendazim at a dose of 2 cc per liter of water. These two last steps can be omitted if the buyer requests it (Figure 48).

The fruits should be placed with the base of the peduncle facing down, as this is the last part to ripen and is the most resistant to weight.

## PAPAYA CONFIT

This is a type of confit fruit, also known as crystallized, made from green papaya or with a higher degree of ripeness. It is presented in pieces, cubes, or flakes. It is used as an ingredient in creamy ice creams and in bakery and pastry products.

The process begins with cutting the fruit, followed by a pre-treatment with a 2% calcium chloride + 0.1% sodium bisulfite solution for 6-8 hours. Then, the fruit is washed, followed by immersion in syrup, starting with a concentration of 30ºBrix and increasing the sugar concentration each day until it reaches 75º Brix. If you want to make crystallized papaya, bring it to 90º Brix. The resulting product has very low moisture (10%), so it can be stored for up to a year.

## PROCESS DESCRIPTION

Selection: Remove fruits that do not have the appropriate degree of ripeness or that have bruises or rot.

Washing: The fruits are immersed in a chlorine-treated water bath. The water is chlorinated by adding chlorine to the washing water at a concentration of 2 ppm.

Peeling: The papaya is peeled with a knife, cut in half, and the seeds are removed.

Chopping: The papaya is cut into quarters or eighths (depending on the size) and then into 5 mm cubes. This operation is done manually or using a machine called a cuber.

Confit: A sugar syrup of 30% with 0.2% citric acid is prepared and heated to boiling. Then, the pieces are immersed in a ratio of 1:1 fruit to syrup and left to rest for 2 hours. At the end of this stage, the syrup is drained and concentrated by adding more sugar until the °Brix reaches 50°. The fruit is then added back in.

<!-- image -->

fruit syrup and left to rest for a new period of 2 hours. The process is repeated, concentrating the syrup to 50-65 ° Brix and leaving it to rest for 2 hours. To finish the confit, the 65-degree syrup is concentrated to 75 ° Brix and left to rest for 24 hours.

Draining: Once the fruit has been confited, the syrup is drained, and the fruit is removed, passing it through a sieve to remove excess syrup.

Drying: The drying of the fruit is complemented by applying temperatures of 60-65 °C for 4 hours, in an electric dryer with hot air. If you wish to obtain crystallized fruit, you should first prepare another syrup.

Technical Guide

Using the syrup resulting from the draining of the fruit, a new syrup of 90 ° Brix is prepared, to which more sugar, 1% glucose, and 0.2% citric acid are added. The glucose prevents the syrup from solidifying and also improves the appearance of the fruit pieces. The syrup is heated to boiling, added to the fruit, and left to dry for 4 hours.

Packaging: The product should preferably be packaged in a polyethylene cellophane bag with vacuum sealing.

Storage: The packaged product should be stored in dry places with good ventilation, away from direct sunlight, and on shelves.

At the end of the process, an average of 110 kg of finished product is obtained for every 100 kg of fruit.

## PAPAYA SLICES

Slices are a method of fruit preservation, through the process of dehydration. They are thin slices of pulp with a soft and elastic consistency, a sweet taste, and a characteristic color of the fruit used as raw material. The slices can be rolled into plastic films and stored for several weeks, or cut into small pieces for use in confectionery and pastry.

## PROCESS DESCRIPTION

Selection: Fruit that is not at the appropriate maturity stage or that shows signs of rot or bruising is removed.

Weighing: The fruit is weighed to determine the proportions to be used.

Washing: The fruit is washed in a sink or container with chlorinated water (2 ppm of chlorine).

Peeling: The papaya is peeled with a knife, halved, and the seeds are removed.

Pulping: Using a fine mesh pulper or, if not available, a blender.

Formulation: The papaya pulp is mixed with lemon juice or citric acid in a ratio of 3:1.

Thermal treatment: The mixture is heated in a pot or saucepan to a temperature of 75 °C. Once the temperature is reached, the mixture is cooled to 40 °C.

Laminating: Trays are prepared, which are coated with glycerin to prevent the product from sticking. The pulp is spread evenly across the tray, reaching a uniform thickness of about 3 mm.

Drying: The trays are transferred to an artificial drying device with indirect heating, where they are dried for several hours at 60 °C, until the product reaches a leather-like consistency (approximately 15% moisture). If the weather conditions allow, the drying can be done in solar dryers.

Packaging: The slices are removed from the tray and spread on a flat surface, where they are cut into strips of varying width (2 to 5 cm).

Sugar can be sprinkled on the strips to prevent them from sticking, and then they are rolled and packaged in cellophane bags. An alternative is to pile up several layers of the dried product and then cut into small squares of 4 x 4 cm.
